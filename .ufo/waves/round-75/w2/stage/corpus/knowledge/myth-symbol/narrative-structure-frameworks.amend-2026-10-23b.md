## 1. The article installs a hierarchy of levels, and refuses to fix their number

The concept the 1966 article installs is stated on printed p. 5 in one sentence: "Ce concept est celui de niveau de description" (8 words, p. 5). The next clause is what makes it usable rather than decorative: the levels are "dans un rapport hiérarchique", and "aucun niveau ne peut à lui seul produire du sens" (p. 5). A unit counts only by integration — "toute unité qui appartient à un certain niveau ne prend de sens que si elle peut s'intégrer dans un niveau supérieur" (21 words, p. 5).

The page then names the mechanism. Benveniste's theory of levels "fournit deux types de relations" — distributional ones, and integrative ones — where the first hold "sur un même niveau" and the second are "saisies d'un niveau à l'autre" (p. 5). The conclusion is stated flatly: "les relations distributionnelles ne suffisent pas à rendre compte du sens" (p. 5). From that the page draws its own working order, which is the first design order this entry carries: one must "distinguer plusieurs instances de description et placer ces instances dans une perspective hiérarchique" before analysing anything (p. 5). A level is not a container but an operation: "Les niveaux sont des opérations" (p. 5).

What the page will not do is fix how many levels there are. Its sentence is explicit: "Quel que soit le nombre des niveaux qu'on propose et quelque définition qu'on en donne, on ne peut douter que le récit soit une hiérarchie d'instances" (p. 5). Two things follow for this lane. The hierarchy is the article's claim on p. 5; the count is not. Any count — three, four, five — has to be checked against the page that carries it, and §3 shows what happens when a secondary text's count is read back as the article's own.

Reading is the mirror of that hierarchy. Understanding a narrative is "y reconnaître des « étages »", which means "projeter les enchaînements horizontaux du « fil » narratif sur un axe implicitement vertical" (p. 5); and reading a narrative is not passing from word to word but "passer d'un niveau à l'autre" (p. 5). Two directions are therefore always in play at once: the chain of what happens, and the axis that chain is projected onto. A world bible that writes only the chain has written half of what this page describes.

The page also shows its own debts, which is why any sentence beginning *Barthes says* should name which borrowed part it is repeating. The levels theory is credited to Benveniste, with a footnote adding the Prague School's postulate of integration levels (p. 5). Rhetoric had already assigned discourse "au moins deux plans de description" (p. 5). Lévi-Strauss had shown that mythic units acquire signification by being "groupées en paquets" (p. 5). And Todorov, following the Russian Formalists, "propose de travailler sur deux grands niveaux, eux-mêmes subdivisés", the second of which is "le discours, comprenant les temps, les aspects et les modes du récit" (p. 5). The article is a synthesis that says so on the page.

Two limits of the page are recorded here rather than smoothed over. Its closing analogy, Poe's *Lettre volée*, begins at the foot of p. 5 and continues past it, so this file takes only its opening — "Que l'on permette ici une manière d'apologue" — and no conclusion from it. The transcription is machine-made and damages single characters: "V analyse" for *l'analyse*, "Yelocutio" for *elocutio*, "Vhistoire" for *l'histoire* (p. 5). Every span quoted above avoids a damaged word, and no claim rests on a damaged one.

## 2. The five codes are *S/Z*'s, and the page that names them is a reading sheet

The document behind the five codes identifies itself. It is headed "Fiche de lecture", is signed (DKW, mai 2000), and is about *S/Z* (Paris: Seuil, 1970) — a reading sheet on the 1970 book, not an excerpt of the 1966 article and not an excerpt of *S/Z* either (S891). It describes the book as "la lecture appliquée d'un texte de Balzac", as an attempt to "fonder la première typologie des textes", and as written from the side of "du scriptible" rather than "du point de vue du lisible" (S891).

Its working unit is the lexie: "Barthes isole des blocs de signifiants qu'il appelle lexies", and "A chaque lexie correspondent 3 ou 4 sens à énumérer" (S891). The page's own image for the text is a "réseau à mille entrées" that a reader can star but not gather up (S891). The codes enter at exactly this granularity, as the registers a lexie's meanings are distributed across.

The enumeration is one sentence plus a list. "les premières lexies suscitent l'établissement des cinq grands codes que vont rejoindre tous les signifiés du texte" (17 words, S891). The five are then named in French with a gloss each: "le code herméneutique (l'énigme à déchiffrer)", "le code sémique (champ thématique)", "le code symbolique (l'antithèse)", "le code proaïrétique (code des actions et des comportements)", "le code gnomique (sagesse, savoir, code culturel)" (S891). Those are the names this lane uses: *sémique* and *gnomique*, not the English *semantic* and *cultural*, which are a gloss and not the page's words.

What the codes are, on this page, is stated twice and both statements matter. "Ces cinq codes représentent le réseau topique à travers quoi tout le texte passe" (S891). And the codes are "le lieu de disparition des voix dont est tissé le texte" — the place where the voices disappear, not a chorus of them (S891). The analytical aim is correspondingly modest: "non pas de manifester une structure, mais de produire une structuration à partir des blancs du texte" (S891). A design order follows: the codes describe how signification is distributed in a text already written; they are not five stages to build in order, and they are not rules a reader applies.

The page says nothing about the 1966 article. It cites *S/Z*, names its five codes, and stops. So it cannot support any sentence about levels of description, and §1's claims are not drawn from it.

## 3. Where the codes sit: *en partie*, in a peer's own sentence

The relation is put on the record by a semiotician, and he begins with the question rather than the answer: "Mais combien, et de quelle nature, sont ces codes ?" (10 words, printed p. 58, S892). His answer names five and gives them his own spellings: "cinq codes : proaïretique, sémantique, culturel, herméneutique et symbolique" (S892).

The label drift between the two documents is worth stating because it is the reason this entry cites the five-part structure and not a spelling. The reading sheet says *sémique* and *gnomique* with "code culturel" as a gloss (S891).

The scholar spells two of the five differently: his list says *sémantique* and *culturel* (S892). Both name five; neither is quoting the other; the edition of record is the 1970 book, which this round did not open. An entry that picks one spelling and calls it Barthes's is over-claiming.

The scholar's own calibration is on the same page and is better than anything this file could add. The list "doit être lue, aujourd’hui, davantage comme une série de questions ouvertes, auxquelles la sémiotique subséquente a donné des solutions possibles, que comme une proposition de résultats déjà établis" (S892). He adds that Barthes had in mind the principal problems of a semiotics still to be made, his own contrast being "plutôt que des bases d’une théorie textuelle déjà faite" (S892). That is a statement about the status of the five-code typology, made in the secondary literature, and it is why §6 marks the codes contested rather than settled.

The relation itself is one sentence with a qualifier that carries the whole claim: "ces cinq codes sont en partie une reformulation des quatre niveaux du récit individués dans l’« Introduction à L’analyse structurale des récits » (1966) : fonctions, actions, indices et informants" (30 words, S892). The operative words are *en partie*. The same passage says which code does which work: "Le code proaïretique est en quelque sorte une synthèse des fonctions et actions" (S892). The proairetic code is therefore not a sixth level and not a fifth level of the article; it is a synthesis of two of them.

The correction this entry carries is about the four-item list in that sentence, not about the scholar's argument. His list — fonctions, actions, indices, informants — is his own compression of two different things in the article. The article's printed p. 6 enumeration is three levels, « fonctions », « actions » and « narration », each credited to a precedent (Propp and Bremond; Greimas's actants; Todorov's *discours*), and the page calls the proposal provisional, its advantage being still almost exclusively didactic — a locator only, not quoted here (§7). The items *indices* and *informants* are, on printed p. 11, two of four classes into which the units of the *functional* level divide, the others being noyaux and catalyses; that page also states that a unit may belong to two classes at once — again a locator, not quoted. The noyau/catalyse test itself sits on printed p. 10 as a deletion test — locator. So *the article's own four levels* is a sentence no page opened for this file carries: it is the secondary literature's compression, and adopting it as the article's list is the citation error this amendment exists to stop.

## 5. Design orders a world bible can run without this file

**Checklist, in order.**

1. Decide which object you are specifying. Levels of description describe how a narrative is told; the five codes describe which registers a written text's meanings are distributed across. They come from different books and answer different questions.
2. Write the level map before the prose. Name each instance of description, then state for each unit which higher level it integrates into, because a unit that integrates nowhere carries no sense on the article's own rule.
3. Keep the two directions separate in the note. The horizontal chain is what happens; the vertical axis is what the chain is projected onto. A draft that has one and not the other is unfinished, not terse.
4. State the count you are using and where it comes from. The article fixes the hierarchy and not the count; a three-level scheme, a four-class scheme and a five-code scheme are three different borrowings.
5. If you use the classes, test a unit by deletion: what the story cannot lose and what the discourse cannot lose are different losses, and mixed membership is the normal case rather than an anomaly.
6. Never renumber the codes. If you want five registers, name them with the reading sheet's French names or say plainly that you are using your own list.

**Decision table — which framework is being copied.**

| The design question in front of you | Framework | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| At what level is this told, and what does this unit serve? | the 1966 hierarchy | the level map and the integration rule |
| Which register of meaning is this detail working in? | the five codes | the register, with the French name |
| Which events can the story not lose? | the four classes of the functional level | test result per unit, mixed membership allowed |
| Is one structure shared by all stories? | none of the above | nothing: no page read here supports it |

**Decision table — what the world-bible record must carry.**

| Field | Required when | Why |
|---|---|---|
| level map | any story specified against the article | the hierarchy is the article's actual claim |
| count and its source | whenever a count is printed | the article refuses to fix the number |
| code register per scene | any scene specified against the codes | the codes are registers, not stages |
| attribution note | every borrowed term | the two documents name the codes differently |

**Worked example, no canon decided.** A designer is specifying the founding story of a river-port town for a language-and-culture bible, and the bible needs the story before the town's charter can be written. Run the checklist in order: the level map first — the founding as a chain of events, and the level of the charter myth that chain is projected onto — then the count and where it came from, then the two directions kept separate, then a code register for each scene. The article's side supplies the map and the integration rule; the codes supply the register column, not the order of scenes. If the next draft asks for *the archetypal level* or *the fourth level*, the checklist stops it: the article fixes the hierarchy and leaves the number open, and the four-item list belongs to the classes of one level. What the town actually holds as canon is lore-shokunin's call, and this entry ends before it.

## 6. Calibration

**The hierarchy of levels of description — mainstream as method, contested as a claim about texts.** It is a standard descriptive heuristic of the structuralist phase of narratology, and the article itself presents its own list as provisional, with an advantage it calls almost exclusively didactic, which is a locator on printed p. 6 and not a quotation here. Use it to order a description; do not report it as a discovered structure of narrative.

**The five codes — contested, and by the secondary literature's own account.** The scholar whose passage this entry quotes says the list should be read today as a series of open questions rather than as established results (S892). That is the calibration, and it is stronger than a lane's own hedge: the typology is a live question in the field that produced it.

***The article's own four levels* — discredited as an attribution.** The article's p. 6 enumeration is three levels; the four items in circulation are three levels plus classes of one of them. The error is a citation error rather than a refuted theory, and it is the exact error this amendment was written to stop: a draft that keeps the phrase *the article's four levels* keeps a sentence no page carries.

**A universal narrative grammar — outside what these pages support.** Nothing read here licenses one structure for all stories: the article argues for a hierarchy of instances and explicitly leaves the number open, and the codes are offered as a typology of texts, not of minds. Treat any universalist extension as the file's own speculation and label it so.
