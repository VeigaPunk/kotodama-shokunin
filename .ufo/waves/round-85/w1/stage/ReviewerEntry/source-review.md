## Body-supported scope and rejection traps

1. Metallurgy, extracted lines 80 onward: defines metallurgy as extracting metals from ores, customarily commercial rather than laboratory methods. General Sequence distinguishes ore proper and gangue, crushing/washing, chemical extraction, then refining an impure product. Fiery Operations, Amalgamation, and Wet Processes are separate categories. These support a dated technical register, not a universal chronological sequence through which every society passed.
2. Metallurgy, Fluxes: flux converts gangue into fusible slag; slag can protect the regulus from furnace gases. Therefore an entry equating all slag with worthless accidental dirt or equating smelting with mere melting contradicts this source. These are vocabulary/design distinctions, not operating instructions or modern safety advice.
3. Iron, extracted lines 101–110: the article expressly confines itself to chemistry and refers manufacture/properties/uses to the distinct Iron and Steel article. It gives carbon proportion AND condition as regulating cast iron, steel, wrought iron. It supplies no attested historical derivation of the English word iron. No inference from chemical symbol Fe or ferrum to the genealogy of a fictional iron word is licensed.
4. Iron, opening: its remote-origin and Iron-Age succession statements are dated historical assertions, not modern archaeological findings. Do not silently import them as a universal stage law. Magnetism/rusting descriptions support dated property-vocabulary options only; none establishes universal smith caste, inherited occupation or ritual taboo.
5. Bronze, opening body paragraph: copper/tin in variable proportions; harder and less malleable than copper, more fusible and suited for casting. Later in the same paragraph, manganese bronze may have little/no tin, aluminium bronze can lack tin, and silicon bronze is named. Thus not every object called bronze must have the same composition; a name is not an assay. Modern recipes, telegraph wires, British/French coinage and engineering grades must not be retrojected into an undated ancient workshop.
6. Bronze, second body paragraph: explicitly distinguishes ancient metal names chalcos/aes and uncertainty between pure and alloyed copper. Its Homer, mythic smiths, and Pausanias discussion is EB11 interpretation, not independent verification of ancient practice or linguistic etymology. The brown / aes Brundusianum alternatives are explicitly presented as rival etymological proposals; do not choose one as settled.
7. Alloys, body lines 80–142: the term need not imply fixed proportions, homogeneity or chemical-compound status. Surface appearance does not establish uniform microscopic structure. The introductory etymology through French aloyer / Latin alligare belongs to this article and remains attributed 1911 wording, not modern lexical adjudication.
8. Alloys, body lines 219–250: fusion is the general method, NOT the only possible method; diffusion, precipitation, electrodeposition and pressure examples follow. A universal definition requiring every alloy to have been melted together would erase the source's explicit qualification.
9. Alloys has a large plate/caption block at extracted lines 144–218. Exact micrograph percentages, magnifications, ferrite/carbide labels and quench temperatures found ONLY there must be called captions or replaced with body quotations. Adjacent body at lines 124–129 independently discusses 23.3% tin bronze, so that particular percentage is not automatically caption-only; inspect each quote, not a blanket figure heuristic.
10. Bronze_Age body is substantial, not unavailable or prose-free. Lines 80–81 contain the explicit evolutionary/racial/diffusion assertions: “most races passed”, “there can be but little doubt”, foreign teaching of European metallurgy, and a hand-size inference contrasting “large-handed races of Europe”. The scoped rejection is refusal to use this as universal diffusion/racial stage-law. It is NOT a claim that all information on this page is absent, inaccessible, or unusable for historiography.
