Abstract: The aim of the text is to study the way in which the funeral song, a mournful, interactive,
narrative genre that frames the ritual process of exit from life is transformed when its content
changes. In other words, interaction between the morphological expression and the content on
the one hand and social function on the other hand is a basic issue in the text. The tradition of
lament in Greek history is not a modern phenomenon. It became a significant poetic art in the
Iliad by Homer, who, however, used the existing poetic tradition. Taking into consideration this
historical tradition, an attempt is made to understand the way in which the funeral song is used
as a genre of oral literature, in order to honor and glorify those who were killed while fighting for
the liberation of the homeland.
Keywords: lament, ritual process, history, narrative genre, oral literature, Homer

I dealt with the subject of death and how it is represented in discourse several
years ago, when I was asked to take part in a two-day colloquium on the Greek
poet Georgios Zalokostas, in whose poetry death is diffuse. He focuses on death
in two ways: in the first he concentrates on those sacrificed on the battlefields

to safeguard the value system of the group, in which case he uses the “rhetoric
of public lamentation”, while in the second, which laments the death of several
of his offspring, “he resorts to the rich tradition of the lament song” (Avdikos
2001: 411–412). Things are clear for the poet Zalokostas; he uses a different
poetic form for the “honourable death” of those killed in battle than for the
untimely death of his sons.
What happens, however, in the case where the lament song is used to praise
the dead in battle? It is generally agreed that the lament is the poetry of the
final parting, of the exodus from life (Honko 1974: 9). Fedakâr points out that
the poetry of the lament is diffused in ancient Turkish literature and notes as its
principal trait its identification with death, and specifically with the procedure
of the departure of the deceased from the world of the living (2010: 122; Šunde
2006: 682). In this sense, laments exist the world over and their beginnings are
to be traced back to the remote past, as all men must face the exit from life and
the unease, as well as the angst, about what awaits them after death.
The Greek word for lament, μοιρολόγι (pl. μοιρολόγια), is a compound
formed from the noun μοίρα (fate) and the verb λέγω (to say, to speak about).
The lament is therefore the dialogical narrative discourse framing the ceremonial procedure not only of the departure from life, but also of the transition
and repositioning of the relations between the living (Bourke 1993). One other
basic issue raised by the investigation of laments is the interaction between
the formal expression and the content on the one hand, and the social function on the other. Specifically, laments, as the dictional part of a ritual process,
can contribute to the decipherment of its basic character. The close interaction
between stylistic and thematic units on the one hand, and social function on the
other has far-reaching implications, behind the use of poetic conventions, as
they serve to affect rather than merely to describe behaviour (Caraveli-Chaves
1980: 156).
The tradition of the lament in Greek history is not a recent phenomenon. The
lament was enhanced as a potent poetic art in Homer’s Iliad, which utilized the
existing poetic tradition. In many cultures, including that of ancient Greece,
women were the composers and singers of powerful, sophisticated oral lament
poetry (Kurtz & Boardman 1971: 30). At the end of the Iliad, the Trojans gather
at the palace of Priam for Hector’s funeral. The lamentation begins with the
threnos (24.720-21), performed by professional singers, followed by the gooi,
the dirges of Hector’s kinswomenl – Andromache, Hecabe, and Helen –, individual laments mourning Hector’s death and the devastating consequences of
his loss for the city and its people. Their songs are answered antiphonally by
a refrain of wails and cries from a chorus of Trojan women (Pantelia 2002: 21).
Researchers agree that the incorporation of this lament in the epic is due to
the mourning tradition cultivated for centuries by women in their daily life,
when they bid farewell to their loved ones. Indeed, they go one step further,
arguing that: “the forms of Homeric epics are cognate or derive from a common

source - women’s prayer uttered in group lament” (Weinbaum 2001: 21; Nagy

creation of epic poetry, the mourning tradition becomes valuable material in
the hands of the bards, who create the new narrative forms by utilizing the formal as well as the thematic components of the dirge. Thus, I contend that the
Iliad and the Odyssey may actually be examples of original female creation and
secondary male transmission, a pattern that obscures women’s participation
in the creation of myth and culture. That is, the men who record and transmit
the epics become the cultural historians and later leave the original women who
might have created the project out of their official discourse (Weinbaum 2001:
21). Death and mourning in Homer are unavoidably connected to the concept of
heroic kleos, that is, the glory that epic poetry grants to its greatest heroes. In
the context of the Iliad, where premature death is viewed as an inevitable reality, kleos functions as compensation for early death and as the means of attaining a different kind of immortality (Pantelia 2002: 23; Meltzer 1994: 235).
Taking this historical tradition into account, an attempt is made here
to understand the way in which the lament, as an oral literary genre, was
employed to honour and glorify those killed while fighting for the liberation
of their fatherland. This endeavour was stimulated by the lament songs created by women, mainly, in the region of Mani, which were published by Politis
in the fourth volume of the periodical Laographia, which circulated after the
end of the Balkan Wars. These laments paid homage to soldiers who lost their
lives in the battles of 1912–1913, for the liberation of Greek lands still subject to
the Ottoman Empire. The lamentation took place in Mani, in the homes of the
dead, except in the cases of the two laments referring to King George, who was
assassinated in Thessaloniki (1913) after the city’s liberation (October 1912),
which were chanted in churches in Mani in commemoration of the loss.

MANI, THE GEOGRAPHICAL REGION
Mani (Maina, Magne) is the name given to the middle of the three southern headlands of the Peloponnese. Its seaward end is Cape Matapan. On the
landward side its boundaries are less clearly defined, but the line joining the
northern coasts of the Gulf of Messenia and the Gulf of Laconia is a rough
approximation.
The great ridge of the Taygetos runs like a spine down the thirty-five miles
or so of the peninsula, dividing it into two parts – “Sunny” Mani (Prosiliaki) to
the east, and “Shady” Mani (Aposkiaderi) to the west. But this division is not as
important as the distinction between Outer Mani (to the north) and Inner Mani
(to the south). The dividing line crosses the principal saddle of Taygetos, from
Itylo and Areopolis to Gythion. Inner Mani is felt by its inhabitants to be the
part of Greece which has best maintained its independence from foreign domination and therefore has the most direct connection with antiquity.

THE INTRODUCTORY MOTIFS
In the fourth volume of Laographia, Politis publishes a total of thirteen laments,
all composed by women, except for one which was created by a man. He writes:
“In this first fascicle of the fourth volume of Laographia, the publication of
which has been delayed for a long time by national events, we have decided to
give precedence to two extempore folk poems, in which are expressed without
affectation the feelings captivating the soul of the Greek people in the critical
hour of its life. And this expression of feelings is true and unfeigned, because
it was made at that moment when the overflowing of the heart summons the
mouth, when there is no room for pretence, by naïve and unschooled peasant women, who through improvised laments mourn the beloved deceased”
(Politis 1912–1913: 3).
Politis understands the importance of this poetic creation, which he attributes to “naïve and unschooled peasant women”. There is a problem regarding
this point, even though the poetic material that reaches Politis’s hands has the
stamp of the poetic creation of an illiterate woman or a village woman.
The lamenters use the formal and thematic characteristics of the lament
song, which is shifted to another ideological field. A case in point is “the lament
for Livanas’s son”, which has attracted the interest of several researchers
(Morgan 1972: 265–298). The first scholar after Politis who was involved with
this lament was Rhomaios, in an article entitled “Venizelos in a lament from
Mani” (1980: 218–225). Obviously, the aim of this text is not Venizelos but the
change of epicentre in an oral literary genre. “The lament dramatizes death,
and the heartbreak itself, precisely because it is an extreme and condensed –
truly unbearable – form of mourning, allows and facilitates the subsequent
relief and the appeasing of pain” (Saunier 1999: 23).
The lament begins with a typical introduction, in which the woman who
wants to sing the lament asks the permission of the deceased’s loved ones

The lament (lines 3–11) uses expressive means and imagery which are common in laments in order to state the most important issue, which is declared
by the deceased himself. His wish that the war will end has as its starting point
a concept inherent in laments and in folk culture in general – the good death,
which is the prerequisite for completion of the deceased’s journey with his final
incorporation into the world of the dead. In other words, the precondition for
a good death, which reflects primarily upon the deceased but also his family,
is the ceremonial burial of the deceased (Bourke 1993: 388). What the deceased
undergoes is extreme humiliation; he experiences a “bad” death.

THE GOOD DEATH FOR THE FATHERLAND
Most of the laments exploit the formal elements of the moirologi in order to
reorient its social function by giving death a different content. “Death on the
field of military operations goes beyond its biological dimension. It is not simply the end of existence. It is the beginning for the reproduction of the cultural
system” (Avdikos 2001: 406).
We should remember that the laments were created for those who fell on the
battlefields for the liberation of territories under Ottoman rule (Epirus, part of
Thessaly and Macedonia 1912–1913) and their incorporation into the hitherto
small Greek State. War, particularly war of liberation, needs ceremonies that
will make time stand still, so that the national group can be remustered with
the redrawing of the borders (Marvin & Ingle 1999: 248).
Laments of this category try to transform the ceremonial procedure of the
traditional threnos into a field of national ratification and of repositioning the
relationship between the local and the national (Adebanwi 2008: 420).
Specifically, the “lament for Livanas’s son” is turned, on the pretext of
mourning the dead soldier, into a doxology of the national struggle. The structure imprints the reassessment of the ideological discourse of the lament,
which loses its philosophical/cosmological character concerning life and
death, and is historicized through its inclusion in the effort to constitute collective memory. Paraphrasing the views of Morinis (1985), we could say that
the individual pain and the consequent ceremonial are used as a means for the

This is crystal clear in the lament for Livanas. Familiar formal traits of the
specific poetic genre are used to transform it, at least from the standpoint of
content, into epic poetry, if we bear in mind that epic poetry functions as an
agent of living testimonies (Konate Deme 2009: 404). The lament does indeed
intersect the generic boundaries and can be perceived as having nuclei of historical and epic poetry. It draws on the known function of the lament, commemorative rhetoric, according to which “the past makes the present. Commemoration is a way of claiming that the past has something to offer the
present” (Olick 1999: 381), in other words, that memories, memorial services
and stories are semeiological means for negotiating the past (Konate Deme
2009: 404).
So, in the lament for Livanas the “weeping” over the deceased is used as
a means for changing the content of the procedure. A traditional practice
(social control) is utilized in order to constitute the kleos (glory) of the dead, in
accordance with ancient Greek habit. The specific lament is organized around
five axes: the introductory motif, the historical framework – Balkan alliance,
the good death, the local, the national and diplomacy. In the historical framework it describes briefly the Balkan environment and introduces the leadership
figure of Venizelos, who dominates these laments to a greater or lesser extent.

Death, now, is not dealt with as a biological loss but as sacrifice for national
integration. Mavromichalaina’s son Livanas and all the others become martyrs
for Greece’s territorial expansion. According to Marvin and Ingle, “sacrifice to
the totem god, the nation, implies the existence of a religious community of
devotees who carry out the religious mission. This community is the military
one” (1999: 99). This was precisely the role and the contribution of the Maniots who fell on the battlefields of the Balkan Wars. Their sacrifice links both
them and their families to a wider community, the national one. This is a differentiation from what was considered a good death in the traditional laments
and what the bloodshed of the menfolk of Mani served. It is imprinted in the

Consequently, in the period of the Balkan Wars the laments function as
a space of creation and community dialogue, in which issues of cultural values
and identities of Maniot society are broached. Mani has entered a critical period
for its social organization. With the creation of the Greek State, an introverted
society organized around the clans and the vendetta as the means of restructuring social relations and resolving differences was incorporated into the
basically military5 mechanism, and through this the Maniots acquired access to
power. As a result, they look to the manipulation of the State in order to achieve
pecuniary, individual and family goals. Characteristic is the petition of the sister of the dead soldier Giannis Michaleas, and of her father, to her brother the
parliamentary deputy, to intervene to prevent the recruitment of Giannis, in

THE LAMENT FOR THE KING
Particularly interesting are the laments for King George I, who was assassinated in Thessaloniki (18 March 1913), where he had settled after its liberation (26 October 1912), in order to be closer to political developments following
the Bulgarians’ entry into the city. The people of Mani mourn the killing of the
king, but they do so in church. The structure of the following lament (Politis
1912–1913: 272), which was sung by an illiterate peasant-woman in the Maniot
village of Oitylos on the day of the king’s funeral, is indicative of how the sentiments generated affect the mourning of the monarch.

The laments mourning the loss of the king focus essentially on that process by which the death of a ruling figure and his memorial service contribute to the constitution of national memory and history (Adebanwi 2008: 420).
The lamenters of the king express a collective pain (the papa and the king of the
Greeks has been killed, our father has been slain), which indicates the change of
the political model in Maniot society. Although the local council of elders continues to have authority in the local community, the institution of the Nation
State has created another authoritative superstructure which is represented by
the king. With the patriarchal structure of the family as tool, it now widens the
boundaries of this structure by putting as head of the national patriarchy the
figure of the king, whose death evokes feelings corresponding to those evoked
by the death of the pater familias.
Consequently, the memorial service becomes the occasion for expressing
the anxieties caused by the death of the king, as well as the determination to
secure the survival and the expansion of the Nation State. Thus, the Maniots
now project their localism into a wider collectivity, which links them to the traditions relating to the recapture of Constantinople, which fell to the Ottomans
in 1453.

IN CONCLUSION: EXTEMPORE CREATION IN MANI IN 1912–1913;
A CRITICAL LOOK
Those scholars who deal with the lament treat it as a basically improvised oral
creation of women (Danforth 1982: 73; Seremetakis 1990: 481; Håland 2011:
191). However, the issue of the spontaneous, improvised creation is a moot one.
Politis (1912–1913: 271), a leading figure in recording the folk songs, historical
songs and laments with the subject of the Balkan Wars, writes:

The war and the events associated with it provide an opportunity for
the plenteous flowering of demotic Greek poetry. Numerous are the
songs which soldiers and men and women of the people sing today on
various occasions, some of them improvised, others cobbled together
in makeshift fashion from elements of earlier songs, adapted more or
less pertinently to the prevailing circumstances. All of these indeed do
not purely have the character of the folk song. Nonetheless, obvious
in them is the influence of the technique of poetry but born of the
moment and created to meet a temporary need ; it is not an offering
to be kept for long, and several songs in this category have faults of
many kinds or sometimes express individual sentiments which are
not shared by all the people. And through their dissemination they
take on the final and purely demotic type, because the women who
repeat them for a short while, correct the technical and linguistic
imperfections, fill in the gaps and cast out or assimilate the alien
elements.
In this excerpt, Politis sets forth his central views on folk creation. He
observes that works collected and published in the periodical Laographia have
technical, formal imperfections. In the laments there is the creator, who is as
a rule a woman. However, he ascertains that sometimes these creations are
not aesthetically perfect, which is because the laments were created “to meet
a temporary need”. In other words, it is not only the pain but also the need to
declare an identity, on the part of the creator, as well as to ratify and publicize his/her verses, which push him/her to choices that do not permit aesthetic
processing of the text.
So, with regard to the “lament for the king” (Politis 1912–1913: 272), created by the illiterate peasant from Itylos, Karambelas, Politis notes that “the
creator, perhaps a compelling improviser, was persuaded by the formality of
the occasion and the magnitude of the subject to display his art, and for this
reason probably he deemed it good to make a rhyming lament, but unaccustomed to using rhyme, he was forced to subject his meanings to its demands,
and so are explained the hasty lines and the fillings in” (Ibid.).

However, we should point out a difference in the laments created for the
king. They were sung in a church, in front of the entire congregation, in contrast to the laments sung in the home of the deceased. By taking into account
Caraveli-Chaves’s view that the “poetic devices within a living oral tradition
cannot be approached only in terms of textual aspects, such as sets of opposition, but through a system of continuous dialogue between aesthetic considerations and social functions” (1980: 156), we are able to understand the priorities of those who sang the laments for the king. What had priority for them
was the social function, in which is included both their personal position in the
local society but also in the State or national environment (kingdom), which
contributed to the downgrading of the aesthetic result.
The composer of the lament borrows from the tradition of rhyme, possibly because he believes that this befits the head of state, the dead king. Marigo
Leontakianakou too resorts to this poetic form in “another lament for the king”
(Politis 1912–1913: 273), in which “the lamenter tried to process more technically through the rhyme, but she did not follow this type systematically”. In
this spirit, that is in order to transcend the local normality, she does not follow the traditional eight-syllable line (Seremetakis 1990: 482) but introduces
rhythm and rhyme, which is not an essential element in the poetry of Inner
Mani (Morgan 1973: 273), as well as the fifteen-syllable line and other motifs,
which draw from laments of other regions. Specifically, the rhyme, but mainly
the fifteen-syllable line, contributes to the endeavour of redefining the meaning of the content of the laments, given that it is also related to epic poetry12.
To return to the issue of improvisation, I stress my reservation as to the
degree or even the actuality of improvisation. Politis admits in his comments
from Kynouria that “we corrected the lines for which the restoration was obvious, enclosing the words removed or added, respectively in square […] and in
angle <….> brackets” (681). To summarize:
1. Politis confesses to making interventions himself, with the aim of adapting
the formal elements to what he and the folklorists consider as suiting the specific genre of folk poetry (Seremetakis 1990: 489).
2. The recorders of the laments are schoolmasters, philologists with PhDs and
headmasters of the region, who, although it is not written anywhere, will have
been close to their teacher in the Faculty of Letters of the University of Athens,
Professor Nikolaos Politis. Moreover, it is common knowledge that Politis
had organized a wide-cast network of collaborators throughout Greece, who
recorded and dispatched folklore material to him; this was particularly true for
the Peloponnese and even more for Mani, which neighboured his birthplace,
Kalamata.

3. What is of interest is the manner of recording. As a rule, the recording of
the lament songs is not primary. Researchers are not present at the time the
laments are being delivered. Therefore, in order to record the lament, they
consult the lamenters after the ceremony, or other sources. The lament for
Mitsos Demestichas “was written down at the dictation of P. Demestichas
himself” (Manolakos 1912–1913: 680), brother of the dead soldier, who was
literate. “Another case is this of Despo, wife of Stelios Georgoulis who fell in
Epirus on 7 January 1913.” Despo capitalized on the opportunity of the lamenting of another deceased and created a lament for her own deceased, which “the
lamenter herself wrote down at my request”, as Politis notes (Kastri, Kynouria,
681).
A second source is the relatives and other members of the community13.
“The first was said to some persons in Kotroni by the brother of the slain Petros
Michail Michalakakos, from whom I heard it and I wrote it down”, writes one
of Politis’s collaborators in the region of Mani (Manolakos 1912–1913: 675). We
may also include in this category the recording by the groups of children who
learnt by heart the lament for the dead king and sang it to collect money, in the
same way as carol-singers do. The lament composed by Karambelas in Itylos
was also recorded by this source. “It seems that the lament was very pleasing, because even children, having learnt it by heart, toured the villages and
sang it for money. The way the lament was used by the children was according to information from Epameinonda N. Basoukeas, who told us about the
song, which he wrote down in another village, where it was sung by children”
(Politis 1912–1913: 272). The same happened also with the lament that Marigo
A. Leontakianakou composed for the king, which “groups of children sang,
coming to the house” (Dimopoulos 1912–1913: 273).
4. Publication and literacy. The laments for the king were published in the
Kalamata newspaper Neos Tachydromos. “It was printed in Kalamata in a small
newspaper”, comments Politis, “to whom the headmaster of Arpharas,
Dimopoulos, sent it, who pointed it out, accompanied by the issue of the newspaper Tachydromos, which had also published it” (Politis 1912–1913: 272). Doctor S. Tsaphos does the same with another lament which was sung in Areopolis
in memory of King George I. “It was published in the issue of 31 March of the
newspaper Neos Tachydromos, published in Kalamata”, writes Politis (1912–
1913: 692).
All these snippets of information cast doubts on what we know about the
ceremonial performance, as well as about the climate of extempore composition of the lament. Reference has been made to the extensive presence of
historical and diplomatic events in the laments, something that changes the
typical traits of the specific genre. It is transmuted from a song about the pain
for the loss of loved ones to a hymn lauding the sacrifice for the survival of

380

www.folklore.ee/folklore

Lament Songs Glorifying Greek Soldiers Killed in Battle During the Balkan Wars (1912–1913)

the national collectivity. Beyond this arises the issue of improvised creation,
which touches on the aforesaid transformation. Even in the laments for Maniot
soldiers the female lamenters, although in the majority illiterate, handle with
competence the contemporary historical events of the Balkan Wars, which they
incorporate into their poetic compositions.
This fact raises the question of their sources. It seems, however, that in Mani
there is a diffuse literacy (the recorders have university education)14, while it
seems that the laments become known to the entire community. As far as the
laments for the king are concerned, these are published in newspapers of the
nearest urban centre (Kalamata), are printed in pamphlets which are possibly
distributed, and also constitute narrative material for groups of children, who
use them to collect pocket money. The inclusion of the laments for the king
in the repertoire of the groups of children also attests to the response of the
public, which rewards the children for their effort. This is material that creates
channels of communication and reciprocity between the oral creation, the publication of the laments in newspapers and their transformation into historical
genealogy which disseminates the historical information. This was always the
case with itinerant bards, who roved from place to place, singing and publicizing important events. Therefore, in this particular case we cannot speak about
improvised spontaneous creation. The illiterate women lamenters come into
contact with this material through the aforesaid channels. And the doubting of
the extempore creation is augmented in the case of the laments for the king, for
which we do not know whether they were sung or read by their creators, who
had written them prior to the memorial service in the churches.
5. The epic genealogy. The laments for the king are transformed into a newspaper column but they are also a means of diffusing historical knowledge
and forming national consciousness on the margins of public space. Both the
laments for the soldiers and, primarily, the corresponding ones for the dead
king change the character of the genre. They become part of an epic historical process in which they meet with corresponding genres of the past, which
spread the historical information and at the same time reinforce the cohesion
of the group. The giving of new meaning to the familiar formal traits of the
lament plays a major role in this process.
