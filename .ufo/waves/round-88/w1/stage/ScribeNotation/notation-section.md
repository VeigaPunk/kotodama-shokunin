## 4b. Numeral notation: two axes, five types, and their trade-offs

This section adds the *written* side of numbering. Sections 1–4 classify numeral **words** (their base, their morphology, their classifier behaviour) and section 5 covers **counting technologies** (fingers, bodies, tallies, knots, rods). Between them sits a fourth thing: numeral **notation** — the graphic signs a culture writes a number with. A culture can count fluently in words and write its numbers badly, or the reverse; the mismatch is a dating tool (section 5, `writing-systems.md`).

Scope and limits, stated up front:

- **Method and reference, non-canon.** This section supplies a typology and a cost sheet. It does not decide what any fictional culture uses; that is lore's call (`synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md`).
- **It does not certify the historical truth of any notation system.** The type names are one scholar's analytical grid (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations"), not a census of what cultures used. Where a case study below rests on a tertiary reference page (the MacTutor Babylonian and Egyptian articles), that page's own account is what is reported, at its own date, with its own uncertainties.
- **It does not supersede `writing-systems.md`,** which owns scripts, conscripts and the sign-to-speech mapping. What follows is about *numeric* sign systems only: how a culture writes a quantity, not how it writes a word.
- **No "perfect" system and no ladder.** The typology is a classification of attested structures. Nothing here ranks one type as a stage the others passed through, and the source this section rests on states plainly that there is no perfect system among them (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations").

### 4b.1 Four things that are easy to confuse

The single most common worldbuilding error in this corner of the topic is treating these as one system:

| Thing | What it is | Does it have a base? | Where this entry covers it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Numeral words | Lexical items in a spoken language ("two", "twenty", "three hundred") | Yes — usually, via a base and its powers | Sections 1–3 |
| Numeral notation | A graphic sign system for writing quantities, semiotic and translinguistic rather than a transcription of speech | Yes — its powers get distinct notation | This section (4b) |
| Tallying | One-to-one marks kept for a count in progress (notches, knots, strokes); no base, a modulus that restarts at 1 | No | Section 5 |
| Counting devices | Beads, rods and boards whose columns carry the powers of a base — a computational instrument, not a written notation | Yes | Section 5 (`civilizations/inca.md` for khipu) |

The distinctions are not pedantry; each one changes what a design implies. A tally has no base at all: every mark still stands for a single unit, so a distinguished fifth or tenth mark is an ordinal "the tenth mark", not the value ten, and the mark set is far too long for written contexts (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Tallying"). A counting device is not a notation: place-value devices were developed independently in the Near East, East Asia, India, the Andes and Mesoamerica, while *written* cumulative-positional notation is cross-culturally the rarest of the five types (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Counting devices"). A board can be positional while the same culture's written numerals are additive — Roman numerals are the classic case, because they were not used directly for arithmetic (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Counting devices").

One consequence deserves emphasis, because it is a live design lever: **notation is a representational system, not a computing one.** Written numeral notation is used for arithmetic only rarely even today, and in pre-modern periods hardly at all (Chrisomalis 2018, body paragraph 3, before the first section heading). A fictional culture that wants reliable multiplication should be given a *device* and a *notation* separately, and the two may legitimately disagree.

### 4b.2 The two axes, and why five types and not six

Classifying a numeral notation means answering two questions in order (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations"):

1. **Inside one power of the base — the coefficient.** How are the signs formed and combined *within* any single power? Three answers: **cumulative** (signs are repeated and added within the power), **ciphered** (one sign per value of the power), **multiplicative** (two signs per power, a multiplier and a power sign, multiplied together).
2. **Across powers — how the total is assembled.** How are the different powers combined into one numeral phrase? Two answers: **additive** (the reader takes the sum of the signs for the expressed powers) or **positional** (the order or location of the signs within the phrase carries the power).

Three times two gives six combinations in principle, and the source rules one of them out on a structural ground rather than on a historical one: a multiplicative scheme names its powers with their own signs, so it cannot also let *position* supply the power — the power would be doing the work twice, and the reader would have no way to know which signal governs. What remains are **five attested types**. The combination that falls is not a sixth type and has no exemplar; it is a cell in the grid that no attested system occupies (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations", paragraphs 7–9).

Two things follow immediately, and both are load-bearing for design:

- **The grid is classificatory, not genealogical.** No type is "later" than another, and none is better. The source is explicit that its two-axis typology "avoids the teleological argument of Ifrah (1987) and Dehaene (1997) that sees decimal, positional numeration as the end-point of a developmental sequence towards perfection", and that "it is evident that there is no perfect system" (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations"). A fictional culture that adopts a plainer notation is not regressing; it is choosing different trade-offs.
- **The two axes vary independently.** A culture can change how it writes a coefficient without changing how it assembles a total, and the reverse. That is why "which stage is it at?" is the wrong question to ask about a notation, and why a mixed, internally inconsistent notation is a legitimate design object rather than a defect.

### 4b.3 The five types, with what each one costs

The type names below follow the source's own naming: the intraexponential principle (inside a power) hyphenated to the interexponential principle (across powers). Exemplars are the ones the source attaches to each type; the Egyptian and Babylonian cases are taken from the tertiary reference pages cited in the anchors below and are marked as such where they extend the source's own list.

| Type | Inside one power (coefficient) | Across powers | Attested exemplar | What it buys | What it costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative–additive | Signs repeated and added within the power | The expressed powers are simply summed | Greek acrophonic numerals; Roman numerals; early Egyptian hieroglyphic numerals | Few signs to learn and no positional reading: a reader just adds | Numerals get long (276 took fifteen Egyptian hieroglyphic signs), and the value grows with the count |
| Cumulative–positional | Signs repeated and added within the power | Order of the groups carries the power | Babylonian sexagesimal cuneiform | Tiny sign inventory — the 59 digits of each place are built from a unit sign and a ten sign — and the place carries the power while the base stays sixty | The least compact of the five; and with no empty-place sign the gap between powers had to be carried by spacing and context |
| Ciphered–additive | One distinct sign per value of the power | The expressed powers are summed | Georgian alphabetic numerals; Egyptian hieratic numerals | The most compact of the five; order within the phrase does not change the value, so it can be written either way round | A large inventory to memorise, and the reader must know which order is conventional |
| Ciphered–positional | One sign per digit plus a zero, the same set reused in every position | Order of the digits carries the power | Lao numerals; the Western Hindu-Arabic numerals | Compact and directly manipulable — the same signs do duty in every column, so a column can be worked in place | Requires a real zero, and reading the signs aloud is a second, separate task |
| Multiplicative–additive | A multiplier sign and a power sign, multiplied together | The expressed powers are summed | Classical Chinese numerals | Compact for high values, and it names the power explicitly instead of leaving it to position | Needs more signs than a ciphered scheme, and it is rare as a written notation even though it is common in numeral *words* |

Read the table as a cost sheet, not a ranking. The trade-off that runs across it is **sign inventory against numeral length**: the schemes with the smallest inventories (cumulative) produce the longest numerals, and the schemes with the most compact numerals (ciphered) demand the largest inventories. The source states both halves — ciphered-additive systems "write numerals more compactly than any of the other types", while cumulative-positional systems "use a much smaller inventory of signs than other systems (often as few as two or three), but are far less compact" — and adds that the Western numerals are "mediocre in both of these criteria" (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations"). A third axis of cost runs underneath: **cross-linguistic legibility**. Notation is translinguistic — the numeral 17 is read as 10 + 7 in French, 7 + 10 in English and 2 + 15 in Welsh by speakers who read the same signs — so a notation can outlive the language that invented it and be read by people whose numeral words order the parts differently (Chrisomalis 2018, §"The transcoding engine"). A numeric script that a neighbouring culture can read without a gloss is a different kind of cultural asset from one that only its own speakers can.

### 4b.4 Two case studies, and what they add to the grid

The two case studies below come from tertiary reference pages, and are reported as those pages state them.

**Babylonian sexagesimal, and the empty place.** The Babylonians inherited the base of 60 from the Sumerians and Akkadians, "neither the Sumerian nor the Akkadian system was a positional system", and the Babylonians' advance was the place value itself. Their economy of signs is remarkable: to produce a base-60 positional system they "only had to learn two symbols" — a unit sign and a ten sign — from which all 59 digits of every place are built; the rightmost place holds units, and each place to the left multiplies by 60. The price of that economy appeared at the empty place: there was "no zero to put into an empty position", so 1 and 1,0 had exactly the same representation, and the tablet AO 17264 shows the scribe of 147 squared to 6,09 leaving a wider-than-usual gap between the 6 and the 9. A symbol for the empty place was invented later (MacTutor, "Babylonian numerals", last updated December 2000).

**Egyptian hieroglyphic and hieratic numerals.** The hieroglyphic set has one sign each for the unit, the ten, the hundred, the thousand, the ten thousand, the hundred thousand and the million; a number such as 276 was written with fifteen signs, two hundreds, seven tens and six units. Addition was easy and substitution was routine — ten copies of a sign were replaced by one sign of the next higher value. The hieratic set, used on papyrus, has a *separate sign for every multiple*: 1 to 9, then each ten, each hundred and each thousand up to 9000, so 9999 took just four hieratic signs where the hieroglyphic form needed thirty-six. The two consequences the source draws out are the ones the typology predicts: hieratic is far more compact but "required many more symbols to be memorised", and hieratic "did not form a positional system so the particular numerals could be written in any order" (MacTutor, "Egyptian numerals", last updated December 2000). Put beside the typology, the two Egyptian registers sit on two different cells — hieroglyphic numerals are cumulative-additive in the source's own classification, and the hieratic set has exactly the shape the source describes for Georgian ciphered-additive numerals (a distinct sign for each multiple of 1–9, 10–90, 100–900 and 1000–9000, combined additively). Egyptian fractions are a separate layer again: unit fractions were written with a "mouth" sign for "part" set above the numeral, and where the numeral was too long the sign was set over its first part, read from right to left.

Two cautions the case studies force on a fictional design. First, **a base is not a notation.** Babylonian sexagesimal and Babylonian numerals are separable: the base was inherited, the place value was a local advance, and the empty-place problem was solved generations after the system was in use. Second, **the origin of a base is usually not settled**, and a design should not pretend otherwise. The Babylonian page sets out competing explanations for base 60 — divisibility (Theon of Alexandria), weights and measures permitting thirds (Neugebauer), a 360-day year (Cantor), the sexagesimal division of a circle, finger-counting to 60, and the meeting of two peoples counting in 12 and 5 — and records the author's own view that "I just do not believe that anyone ever chose a number base for any civilisation". Treat all of these as hypotheses, and treat the *choice* in a fictional culture as something the story has to explain anyway.

### 4b.5 Worked example (NON-CANON, illustrative only)

A fictional market-and-archive town, *Kharsund*, keeps written accounts on limestone tags and does its arithmetic on a bead board carried by a travelling clerk. Its numeral words are vigesimal, with an opaque low core and a portmanteau set of tens (section 2). What should the tags carry?

**The decision, and the reasoning.** The town has three constraints, and each one is a different trade-off:

1. *Tags must be legible to a scribe who is not a clerk* — the tags are read in court and at the gate, by people whose arithmetic training stops at the board. That argues against a type whose reading requires the reader to already know the conventions.
2. *The board is what computes; the tags only record* — the clerk's board is place-driven, and the town does not want two different readings of the same quantity in the same hearing. A notation that mirrors the board's column logic would be read aloud consistently with the clerk's own reckoning.
3. *The town is a crossroads, and its neighbours write decimal numerals that its tags are sometimes read against* — a translinguistic notation is an asset here, since a foreign reader can extract the quantity without a translator.

Those three constraints do not produce a unique answer, and that is the point of the cost sheet: they pull in different directions, and the design has to name which one wins. Two coherent packages, both available:

- **Package A — cumulative-additive tags, the board unchanged.** Tags carry signs for 1 and 5 of each power, added downward, exactly the way the town's own vigesimal words are built. Short inventories: a single scribe can be taught the tag alphabet in an afternoon, and every tag in the town is readable by any literate neighbour who has been taught it once. The cost is length — a big levy needs a long tag — and the *rate* is spoken in the town's word order, while the board reports in column order, so the town accepts two readings of a quantity in the same session. This is the honest, cheap, internally coherent choice: the board is where arithmetic happens, the tag is where the record happens, and the gap between them is the town's business.
- **Package B — ciphered-positional tags, retrained clerks.** Tags use one sign per digit plus a zero, reused in every column, so a levy fits on a short tag and a foreign decimal reader can parse it unaided. The costs are real: the tag alphabet needs a zero, which the town's vigesimal words do not have anywhere, so the zero is an institution in its own right; every sign must be taught and retaught; and the subtractive, subtler reading aloud that a positional notation invites has to be trained rather than assumed.

**What the worked example is for.** It is a template, not a world. The reusable move is to run the three questions — legibility to non-clerks, arithmetic locus, and cross-linguistic use — against each candidate type in the cost sheet, and to let the answer be a package with a stated loser rather than an optimum. It is also the place to keep the section's own limits visible: *Kharsund* is invented for this illustration, its stones and its board are not evidence of anything, and the decision above is a design inference from the typology, not a finding about any real town.

### 4b.6 Limits, and what this section does not license

- **The typology is one analytical grid.** It was built to classify attested written numeral systems, and the source itself notes that the range of attested patterns is far narrower than the range of imaginable ones (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Classifying numerical notations"). A fictional notation may combine principles the grid does not name — the grid constrains what a *realistic* system looks like, not what an invented one may do.
- **Exemplars are not a census.** The five types are attested unevenly: cumulative systems are among the most common and the earliest used (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Tallying", body paragraph 19), and cumulative-positional notation is the rarest of the five (Chrisomalis 2018, §"Counting devices", body paragraph 25). Distribution in the record is not merit.
- **The Egyptian and Babylonian cases are tertiary accounts.** They are reported here as the MacTutor pages state them, at those pages' December 2000 revision date, and they are used for structure (which signs exist, how the places work, what the empty place did) rather than for chronology, priority or consensus.
- **Unsettled questions stay unsettled.** Why base 60, why the Egyptian registers changed as they did, and how any of these systems reached the cultures that used them are historical questions this section does not answer; the Babylonian page itself presents several incompatible explanations for the base.
- **No canon.** Whether any fictional culture writes its numbers this way is lore's decision, and a lore decision may legitimately pick a type this typology finds surprising.
