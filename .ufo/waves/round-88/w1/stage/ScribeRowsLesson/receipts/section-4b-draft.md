
## 4b. Numeral notation: two axes, five types, and their trade-offs

Numeral *words* and numeral *writing* are separate systems; this section
classifies the writing side only. Chrisomalis's typological scheme asks two
questions of any notation: how are signs formed and combined within one
power of the base, and how are the different powers combined into a numeral
phrase (S931). Five types result from crossing the two axes, and the sixth
combination is logically excluded because multiplicative signs express power
by their form rather than by their position (S931).

| Type | How a power is written | How powers combine | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cumulative-additive | repeated signs for 1 and 5 (Greek acrophonic, Roman) | addition | no place value; long numerals |
| Cumulative-positional | units of 10 and 1 per power (Babylonian sexagesimal) | order by place | needs a zero or an empty-place convention |
| Ciphered-additive | one sign per power (Georgian alphabetic) | addition | compact, but each power needs its own symbol |
| Ciphered-positional | one sign per power (Lao numerals) | order by place | compact; still needs a zero |
| Multiplicative-additive | two signs per power (classical Chinese) | multiplication | no positional reading; transparent to word order |

Two worked cases keep the axes apart, and they point in opposite directions.
Babylon used a base-60 positional system whose powers were written
cumulatively; because it had no sign for an empty place, the same ambiguity
arose for 1 and 60 as for 1 and 1,0 (S932). Egypt used a base-10 system with
a separate symbol for each power — one, ten, hundred, thousand, ten
thousand, hundred thousand, million — and no place value at all, so base and
positionality are independent design choices (S933).

Calibration, plainly. The scheme is typological, not a ranking: Chrisomalis
explicitly refuses the teleological argument that decimal positional
numeration is the end-point of a developmental sequence towards perfection,
and states that on this typology there is no perfect system (S931). A
fictional culture can therefore take base 12 with additive power symbols, or
base 10 with a positional system whose empty place is marked by context
rather than by a zero; neither choice is a step toward the other, and the
typology supplies no ladder on which to climb.

Where this stops. The section is method and reference, not canon: it says how
to describe a notation, not which notations existed, and not which is better.
