## Source-backed principles and research routes

These are scoped introductory principles and resource pointers. Institutional pages are tier A only for their own stated remit or publications; expert explanatory pages are tier B. Catalogue rows do not pretend that a listed work has been read. Design instructions are proposed inferences, never fictional canon.

- Keep multiple explanations for uncertain place-names when evidence does not identify one correct derivation. (S500)
- Mark river crossings in a naming inventory and allow different place-name words for the same practical feature. (S501)
- Record the languages transmitting a place-name: later adaptations can obscure its origins without replacing the feature. (S502)
- Do not infer current speech directly from inherited place-names: the Welsh example retains linguistic inheritance in now English-speaking areas. (S503)
- Set explicit survey boundaries: include both mapped place-names and obsolete settlement names, as the Scottish project does with its dated cutoff. (S504)
- Give a naming institution an explicit legal remit: Harrison's order supplies a concrete BGN example of authority over unsettled geographic names. (S505)
- Distinguish the registry from local usage: GNIS is the official geographic names source for federal departments and their products. (S506)
- For foreign geographic names, record the Geographic Names Server as an information source maintained by the National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency. (S507)
- Build some city names by combining an inherited Celtic-style place-name or river-name with a later element, rather than replacing every older form. (S508)
- Treat spelling standardisation as an interoperability problem: UNGEGN arose partly because inconsistent geographical names caused practical difficulties. (S509)
- Separate national and international coordination when designing geographical-name standardization; UNGEGN explicitly supports both levels. (S510)
- For institutional design, consult the UNGEGN manual on organization and functions of a geographical names standardization programme; this row establishes the resource, not its unread contents. (S511)
- Allow place names to carry cultural memories and traditions as well as geographical reference, following Relph's discussion rather than assuming labels are merely coordinates. (S512)
- Distinguish patterns across many names from individual etymologies: this paper applies GeoNames and the toponym package to pattern-seeking toponymic data. (S513)
- Choose an explicit geographic unit for a survey: the EPNS English place-name project uses counties, rather than an unbounded list of interesting examples. (S514)
- Check survey coverage before interpreting absences: early English volumes emphasized major place-names such as towns and larger rivers, not every minor feature. (S515)
- Use the EPNS journal's articles and notes as a route to further English place-name research; this is a publication pointer, not a claim to have read every item. (S516)
- Do not equate mapping with naming authority: Ordnance Survey explicitly separates its national mapping service from authority on place names. (S517)
- Map selected name-parts to inspect spatial patterns: the featured application searches place names and displays the matches geographically, without proving etymologies by itself. (S518)
- Distinguish deliberate place-name creation from gradual development; the article presents both pathways rather than a single naming event. (S519)

## Design decisions and lore handoff

| Decision | Record |
|---|---|
| Feature | Stable identity, extent and feature type. |
| Form | Language, pronunciation, earliest spellings and competing interpretations. |
| Stratum | Coinage, later adaptation, changing speakers and documentary dates. |
| Authority | Local usage, official remit, decision cadence and recognition process. |
| Evidence | Read passage, source scope, uncertainty and author-designed inference. |

Hand lore a feature identifier, era, language, pronunciation, derivation, competing forms, users, authority and uncertainty. Do not turn an official spelling into a unique historical etymology.
