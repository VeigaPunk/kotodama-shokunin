# ScoutNzimbuMoney — round 56, claim C source slice: nzimbu, the shell money of the Kongo kingdom

## Question answered

What the shells were (species and form), where they came from (the island of Luanda and its
fishery), and who controlled them (the crown, its governor, the grades and the packaging), with
quotable locators from one early account and one open scholarly source, plus one curated record
that carries the control statement in a single sentence.

## Method and confidence

Searched archive.org's index for the Hutchinson translation of Pigafetta and Lopes, then read the
scanned pages themselves (printed pp. 18, 19 and Notes p. 157) rather than trusting OCR. The OCR of
this 1881 volume prints the long s as f ("furnifhes", "ufed"); every quotation below is transcribed
from the page image with the long s read as s, and nothing else altered. The two modern sources were
read as full text: the UGent repository PDF of the 2014 BEADS article, and the Yale MAVCOR card.
Confidence high for what each page says; medium for the political reading, because the 1591 text is
internally tense about who rules the island (see the design notes).

Access date for every source below: 2026-10-22. Route for all: direct HTTP 200, no proxy, no login,
no challenge passed.

### NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2

quotes:
- p. 23: "nzimbu shells (Olivancillaria nana), collected in the Luanda region to the southwest of the Kongo Kingdom, were used as a means of payment"

- p. 23: "The kingdom was ruled by a king or ntinu, its capital was Mbanza Kongo"
- p. 27: "kaori shells had lost their value almost completely in the Kingdom of Kongo and were only used for ornamental purposes"
teaches: A 2014 archaeology paper identifies nzimbu as Olivancillaria nana gathered near Luanda, lists it beside Far-Eastern kaori cowries as Kongo payment, and dates kaori's loss of value.
draft sentences:
- The nzimbu shell is Olivancillaria nana, collected in the Luanda region southwest of the kingdom rather than on Kongo's own coast, so its supply runs through a single outside corridor (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2, p. 23).
- Kaori cowries reached Kongo from the Far East while nzimbu came from Luanda, and the paper treats both as means of payment, so a worldbuilder should give the two currencies separate suppliers and separate politics (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2, p. 23).
- The kingdom was ruled by a king, the ntinu, from Mbanza Kongo, the titles a money chapter should use when it says who ordered the shells fished (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2, p. 23).
- In the nineteenth-century tombs at Kindoki, kaori had lost almost all its value and survived as ornament, which gives a dated hinge for staging a currency's decline (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2, p. 27).
- Those same tombs held false cowries instead of kaori or nzimbu, and one line of reasoning the authors report is that perhaps the elite were less rich and displayed power through personal belongings, so later Kongo wealth need not be shown in shell money at all (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2, p. 27).

## Design notes for the entry (money section)

- Name layers, all attested: Portuguese Lumache (the 1881 note derives it from Limax, a snail); Simbo/Zimbo in sixteenth- and seventeenth-century European texts; nzimbu in modern Kikongo orthography. Keep the outsider's word and the local word on different lips.
- Control: the crown's order (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-3), a governor sent from Kongo living at il Santo Spirito who amasses riches from the shells (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-1, p. 19), and the crown as consumer of the island's money (p. 18). The same 1591 chapter also says the surrounding province is ruled by a governor who "professes to be an ally and not a vassal of the king" (p. 18), so the page carries a jurisdiction tension; the entry should say the crown drew the shells and posted a governor, not that the island was a quiet Kongo province.
- Logistics a worldbuilder can use: two grades of Loanda simbo separated by sifting, the coarser called Simbos Sifados, the finer Fonda and Bomba, shipped to Kongo in straw sacks of two arrobas, about sixty-four pounds (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-1, Notes, p. 157). Those grade names and the sack weight are the concrete handles.
- Counting: by quantity and not one by one (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-3). No exchange rate appears on any opened page; do not invent one and attribute it to these sources.
- Change over time: nzimbu currency is a sixteenth- to eighteenth-century fact on these pages; by the nineteenth-century Nsundi tombs neither kaori nor nzimbu was the currency of display (NEW-ScoutNzimbuMoney-2, p. 23 and p. 27).
- Where outsiders misname: the 1881 editor heads the note "the Simbo or Cowrie" and mixes Loanda shells with Brazilian cowries, while the 2014 paper keeps kaori (Cypraea moneta) and nzimbu (Olivancillaria nana) apart; the report also sexes the shells, male and female, and prizes the female. Use these as the source's own over-readings, flagged, not as Kongo belief.
- Place names for the entry: Luanda Island, il Santo Spirito (the chief town with the governor), Libata (small village) against banza (large village) per the note citing Burton, and Loanda glossed as flat country.

## Remaining unknowns

- Whether the crown's interest was a fishing monopoly, a tax in shells, or ritual consumption; the opened pages show command and enrichment, not a mechanism.
- Whether il Santo Spirito can be matched to a known site on the island; not addressed by any opened source.
- Any exchange rate between nzimbu and goods, or between nzimbu and kaori; not on the opened pages.
