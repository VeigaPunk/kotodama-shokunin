# ScoutKimpaVita — the Antonian movement (Dona Beatriz Kimpa Vita, 1704-1706)

Question: what the Antonian movement taught about Kongo, Christ and Saint Anthony; what its offices,
objects and places were called; how it ended; and where the outsider's record over-reads or misnames.
Three sources filed below, all opened by plain HTTP request on 2026-10-22; a fourth was opened, used for orientation and left unfiled (see the method notes). No tool was installed or upgraded.

## Findings for the entry

Names and what the words mean (all from NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-1):

- `nganga marinda`: Gallo's own gloss is "a priestess or doctress of a certain superstition or witchcraft called marinda" (relatione para. 79). The word is an office, not an insult only: he uses it to deny her sanctity by naming her ritual profession, so a worldbuilder keeps both senses — a recognised Kongo religious specialist and a hostile label.
- "little Anthonys": the movement's travelling missionaries. Gallo calls them "the ambassadors, or Antonian missionaries, called 'little Anthonys'," who "had gone everywhere" (relatione para. 61), and later says they "perhaps did more harm with their false and lying reports than the false St. Anthony herself" (relatione para. 127). A rank-and-file emissary office exists in the record only under this nickname.
- "Ne sari": the oath by which adherents swore, which Gallo renders as "by the merciful St. Anthony" (relatione para. 145). A distinct oath gives a worldbuilder a public marker of adherence that a bystander could hear.
- "Salve Antoniana": the Salve Regina rewritten; Gallo says the Queen's side sang it, that he heard it at two in the morning (relatione paras. 56 and 57), and that crowds learned it easily and sang it "through the streets, the fields, and everywhere" (relatione para. 87).
- `musanda` and `fuma`: in her teaching, black people descend from the musanda tree, whose bark makes cords and cloths, and white people from a soft white stone called fuma (relatione para. 92). This is an origin story that classifies people by the materials of Kongo daily life.

Offices, titles and succession:

- Her teaching on kingship was procedural: until the claimants were united in the church there was no King of Kongo, and the one on whose head at the door an angel placed a crown come down from heaven would be king (relatione para. 90). That is a succession rule a worldbuilder can stage: coronation at the church door, crown lowered from heaven, unity of the claimants as the precondition.
- The titled magnates in the report are the Kibenga (of the house of Kimpanzu, her chief patron, wearer of the greatest musanda crown, relatione paras 90 and 93), the Duke of Bamba and the old Queen of Kongo sending ambassadors (para. 107), the Marquis of Vunda (a living magnate pressing the King to descend from Kibangu, and, in her teaching, the lineage of Saint Francis, paras 5 and 91), and the Marchioness Nzimba Npangi, from a slave or servant of whom she said the Madonna descended (para. 91).
- Judicial power sits with a named commoner officer: D. Manuel da Cruz Barbosa, of whom Gallo says that "after the King, he had the power to condemn to death or to release" (relatione para. 118). Capital judgment is therefore a royal-delegated office, not only a king's act.

Objects, dress and places:

- The musanda-bark crowns are the movement's one described regalia: ranked by size, worn by notable supporters, withheld from women except the prophetess, read by Gallo as "livery, or banner" (relatione para. 93). He promises a sketch of them "at the end of the present relation"; the posted page carries no such image, so no drawing can be cited from this edition.
- Her base at São Salvador was a hut behind the walls of the former cathedral, on the site of the episcopal palace (relatione para. 53), and it had three rooms with the middle one given no door, where she withdrew when she claimed to go to heaven (relatione para. 149) — a stage-set detail for any scene of her "dying on Friday and rising on Saturday" (relatione para. 104).
- After the burning, followers said the two burning places became deep wells each holding a star, and gathered leftover bone fragments as relics (relatione para. 137). Relic practice continues after the leader's death, which is a design decision: the movement does not end at the execution.
- The date and the logistics of the end: the King proclaimed that every man should bring a piece of wood for each of the condemned into the square, and on Sunday, 1 July 1706 the judge pronounced the sentence (relatione paras. 135-136). The crowd's wood supply is the detail that makes the execution a civic act rather than a private killing.

Two witnesses compared (Gallo and Da Lucca):

- Rank names diverge between the two eyewitnesses for the same people: Gallo writes of Antonians and "little Anthonys" (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-1, paras 61 and 93), Da Lucca says her disciples were "appelés par elle : anges" and were set apart by the crown (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 226). A worldbuilder choosing one English word is choosing one witness's word.
- The bark tree is spelled two ways in the same decade: `musanda` in Gallo (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-1, paras 92-93), `ensanda` in Da Lucca (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 226). Neither spelling should be presented as the fixed Kikongo form.
- Da Lucca supplies a teaching Gallo does not report: the crown was said to be of the cloth of the first garment of the Christ Child, and its wearers would want for nothing (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 226). Cloth is the argument, not the decoration.
- Royal regalia sat outside the capital: she was sent to the king of Embula to fetch "les insignes royaux des rois de Congo" so that Pedro IV could be crowned with them (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 226). Whoever holds the insignia holds the making of a king.
- Court offices carry Kongo names through a French filter: basciamocano, glossed as the judge who pronounces sentences and literally the place of palavers, and the majordome as `mani lumbu` (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 230, with Cuvelier's footnote). A judge with a court title, not the king alone, reads the sentence.
- The burning is described by both witnesses as alive: Gallo's sentence is being thrown alive into the flames (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-1, para. 136), Da Lucca's account ends "ils furent brûlés vifs" (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 237).
- Her infant son was condemned with her and saved by Da Lucca's plea: he asked the king for the child's life and the king granted it (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 237). A worldbuilder staging the execution must decide what happens to the child, because the record says a plea was made and granted.
- After the death the devotion did not stop: Da Lucca reports that the Antonians said she appeared at the tops of the highest trees in San Salvador, and that another woman preached she was the mother of the false Saint Anthony under the name "la mère des vertus" (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 238).

Where the outsider's record over-reads or misnames:

- Gallo names her and immediately passes sentence on her: "The false St. Anthony was a black woman of Kongo, called Kimpa Vita, and in baptism Beatriz" (relatione para. 79), and to the King he calls her "a wicked woman, a possessed sorceress deceiving the poor, coarse, and ignorant people" (para. 39). Barletta's introduction concedes that Gallo "can't be considered an objective voice" while holding that the report carries important data. Use his observations; do not adopt his labels.
- Translation instability is observable, not hypothetical: the same itinerary of the saint's spirit appears as Mugeto, Soyo, Mbwila and Kibangu in Barletta's English (relatione para. 36) and as Nseto, Soyo, Bula and Kibangu in the Portuguese version Farias quotes (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-2, p. 209). A worldbuilder should not fix a toponym from one translation.
- Farias frames the movement within a long Christian Kongo, not as a sudden import: she writes of "uma verdadeira igreja antoniana, uma nova perspectiva da identidade e da política do Kongo" as what Kimpa Vita prophesied, and says the Roman clergy and some nobles found it subversive (p. 210). The record itself calls them antoniens (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-3, p. 238), so the label is the observers' word; treat it as the name the sources use, not as a self-description.

Chronology for staging: the illness and vision are dated August 1704 (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-2, p. 209; Barletta's introduction, first paragraph), the trial and burning 1 July 1706 (NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-1, relatione para. 136; NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-2, p. 210), and the report itself closes at Rome, in the Convent of the Capuchins, 17 October 1710 (relatione closing), four years after the execution.

## Notes on method and confidence

- High confidence: every quoted string was extracted programmatically from the downloaded file and verified as an exact substring (word counts 7-22, under the 25-word limit); Farias quotes were checked against a de-hyphenated normalisation of the PDF text, so line-break hyphenation is not reproduced in the quotes.
- Medium confidence: the paragraph numbering of NEW-ScoutKimpaVita-1 is a convention of this report (splitting the posted text at its paragraph breaks), because the posted edition has no page or section numbers. A verifier must split the same way to reproduce a locator.
- Observed limitation: the Cambridge Core page for John K. Thornton, The Kongolese Saint Anthony (Cambridge, 1998) and the Cambridge review PDF of it both returned HTTP 429 "Too many automated requests from this network" on three attempts between 18:29 and 18:35 on 2026-10-22, so the monograph itself is unopened here and no row is filed for it.
- The Da Lucca volume is a scan with an OCR text layer: every quoted page was rasterised and read as an image as well as extracted as text, and the wording above matches the printed page on those pages. The book's own page numbers were read off the scanned headers, because the text layer carries no page numbers and the bracketed numbers in the text belong to the original relation.
- One tier-C lead was seen in search results and deliberately not used: a reference-work snippet dates the execution to 2 July 1706 at Evululu and names Da Lucca as her interrogator. Neither Gallo (1 July, in the square at São Salvador) nor the Da Lucca pages read here carry that detail, so the entry should not adopt it without opening the reference work.
- One further source was opened and deliberately not filed, because it adds reception of Thornton rather than further evidence on the movement itself: the Portuguese review António Custódio Gonçalves and Maciel Morais Santos, review of Thornton, The Kongolese Saint Anthony, Africana Studia 1 (1999) 269-272, https://ojs.letras.up.pt/index.php/AfricanaStudia/article/view/7065 (PDF galley id 7065/6491/23298), HTTP 200 application/pdf, 135905 bytes, 2026-10-22.
