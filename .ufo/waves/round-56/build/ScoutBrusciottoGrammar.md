kind: book
tier: A for the Kongo noun-class system and the mfumu glosses as this resident missionary recorded them at San Salvador, and for his own stated judgement on Brusciotto's dialect; B for any claim about the sixteenth- or seventeenth-century kingdom.
scope: the Kongo noun-class architecture as taught at San Salvador (eleven archaic classes lettered A-K, then the modern prefixes); class A as the class of persons, titles and personifications with a/ba plurals; the dictionary range of mfumu and of the compound mfumu ankento; Bentley's argument that Brusciotto's 1659 Rules describe the Soyo or St Antonio dialect rather than court Kongo; the British Museum copy and Guinness translation.
quotes:
- Preface, p. xii: "Some rules for the more easy understanding of the most difficult idiom of the people of the Congo"
- Preface, p. xii: "is not that of the court of Kongo, but that of Sonyo or St. Antonio"
- Preface, p. xii: "We are much indebted to Mr. H. Grattan Guinness for the translation which he has printed and published"
- p. 541: "originally all Kongo nouns possessed a prefix, and according to those prefixes they group themselves in classes"
- p. 541: "Class A consisted of nouns which were the names, titles, or classes of persons, or personifications"
- p. 541: "Various prefixes existed in the singular, which were replaced by a or ba in the plural"
- p. 541: "Every Kongo noun may be traced to one of these eleven archaic classes."
- p. 348: "free man, nobleman, gentleman ; sir"
- p. 349: "queen, chief- tainess, lady, mistress, a free woman." (the hyphen is the scan's line break inside "chieftainess")
teaches: Bentley records the Kongo noun-class system at San Salvador, glosses mfumu and its compounds, and denies that Brusciotto's Rules describe court Kongo.
draft sentences:
- Bentley holds that every Kongo noun may be traced to one of eleven archaic classes and that all Kongo nouns once carried a prefix which put them in one (NEW-ScoutBrusciottoGrammar-2, p. 541).
- His first class held the names, titles and classes of persons, and its singular prefixes were replaced by a or ba in the plural (NEW-ScoutBrusciottoGrammar-2, p. 541).
- Class A is the class a worldbuilder needs for offices and dignities, because a title and the people who bear it take the same class word, while things, places and diminutives fall into the other lettered classes (NEW-ScoutBrusciottoGrammar-2, p. 541).
- The dictionary glosses mfumu with the honorific range of a free man, nobleman and gentleman, and mfumu ankento as queen, chieftainess, lady and mistress, so rank is built by compounding mfumu with a following noun, not by a separate word for ruler (NEW-ScoutBrusciottoGrammar-2, pp. 348-349).
- Bentley, writing at San Salvador, judged that the dialect of Brusciotto's 1659 Rules was not that of the court of Kongo but that of Soyo or St Antonio at the river mouth, so an outsider's grammar can misname the speech community it describes (NEW-ScoutBrusciottoGrammar-2, p. xii).
- The letters A to K are Bentley's own device, chosen to avoid confusion with his final numbering, so they are a nineteenth-century analysis of Kongo and not a taxonomy the speakers used (NEW-ScoutBrusciottoGrammar-2, p. 541).
