# ScoutKongoKingArchaeology — claim C source report (KongoKing archaeology)

Result: two sources opened, both from the KongoKing project (Ghent University, ERC Starting Grant #284126, 2012–2016), both read in full as PDF text in this session. They carry the capital's form, the dating of provincial sites, elite grave goods, imported-goods chronology, the word *mbanza*, and the excavators' own stated limits. Neither carries Kikongo grammar, minkisi, the Antonian movement or nzimbu currency: those need other rows.

Note for the tier decision: both sources are the excavators reporting their own fieldwork. Their summaries of sixteenth- and seventeenth-century documents (Carmelites in 1584, Capuchins in 1648, Afonso I's letters) are secondary inside an otherwise primary report, so the scope lines below stop at the excavation results and the paper's own dating.

draft sentences:
- A provincial capital can be archaeologically almost invisible: the seat of Mbanza Nsundi at Kindoki stays elusive because Kongo mbanza towns housed government at low population density, so a worldbuilder should model a capital as a settled landscape, not a dense walled town (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-1, postprint p. 2).
- Rank can be read from grave assemblages rather than monuments: at Kindoki male tombs held swords and sabres of honour while female burials held anklets with large quantities of glass, copper and shell beads (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-1, postprint p. 3).
- A trade settlement has a usable three-stage curve: Ngongo Mbata was occupied in the late sixteenth century, grew strongly in the seventeenth and declined towards the end of the eighteenth (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-1, postprint p. 6).
- Elite consumption need not be European: stone smoking pipes made for the kingdom's elite after AD 1600 and elaborate decorated pottery were probably status symbols, and their distribution traces communication between the northern provinces (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-1, postprint p. 6).
- Written records can omit monumental building entirely: no historical source mentions the stone church at Ngongo Mbata, yet burials inside it held swords, sabres and a Knights of the Order of Christ medallion, which the excavators read as the highest ruling elite (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-1, postprint p. 6).

quotes:
- p. 467, Introduction, on Kongo towns: "With its mbanza or capital towns, which have been described as “open, scattered garden-cities with a ceremonial and symbolic centre”"
- p. 468, Introduction, on Ngongo Mbata's trade role: "it was an important supplier of raffia cloth amongst other things"
- p. 474, discussion of the Mbanza Mbata identification: "Since the term mbanza refers to where the headman of a certain constituency is settled"
- p. 494, discussion of the church burials: "Sabers and swords were symbols of chiefly power in the Kongo"
teaches: A peer-reviewed excavation report dates Ngongo Mbata's stone church and imported goods, defines mbanza as the headman's seat, and shows provincial capitals relocating after succession disputes.
draft sentences:
- Kongo capitals are better modelled as open, scattered garden-cities with a ceremonial and symbolic centre than as dense walled towns, which is how the excavators summarise the mbanza form (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-2, p. 467).
- The word mbanza names where the headman of a constituency is settled, so a capital toponym follows the office-holder and not the ground; the excavators note that relocations of a duchy's capital happened as a corollary of succession disputes, which is exactly how a worldbuilder can stage a moved capital (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-2, p. 474).
- Ngongo Mbata, called Congo de Batta in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century sources, was an important supplier of raffia cloth and a thoroughfare between the Atlantic harbours and the eastern Kwango region (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-2, pp. 468, 496).
- Imported ceramics give century-scale staging: 89 of 93 exotic sherds were Portuguese, with English tin glaze, Rhenish stoneware and a single Kangxi porcelain fragment marking later contacts (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-2, p. 478).
- Swords and sabres were symbols of chiefly power in the Kongo, and membership of the Knights of the Order of Christ was restricted to the King of Kongo and his closest relatives, so a medal in a grave marks the highest rank rather than ordinary wealth (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-2, p. 494).
- The excavators could not confirm that the capital of Mbata province stood on the excavated hilltop and allow that the 1584 Carmelite testimony to the Duke's residence is exaggerated or that the capital lies elsewhere, a caution to keep when an outsider's record names a capital (NEW-ScoutKongoKingArchaeology-2, pp. 495–496).
