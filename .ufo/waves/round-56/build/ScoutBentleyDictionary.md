# ScoutBentleyDictionary — round 56, claim C source slice: Bentley's Kongo dictionary (1887) and its 1895 Appendix

## Question answered

Which dialect Bentley's dictionary records, how the two volumes spell the words a worldbuilder needs
(nkisi, mfumu, kanda, nzimbu), and what their head entries say that a designer can build on. Two
public-domain archive.org items were opened by plain HTTP; no login, no challenge, no browser.

## Method and confidence

Searched archive.org's advanced-search index for Bentley, chose the two unrestricted scans
(dictionarygramma00bent, 1887; dictionarygramma00bentuoft, 1895), downloaded each volume's djvu text
and page-number sidecar, and then read the cited pages as page images rather than trusting the text
layer. The 1887 dictionary is set in two columns per page and its text layer interleaves the columns,
so every quotation below was checked against the image; fifteen page images were read, listed in the
two route lines. Confidence high for what each page prints (image-checked), high for the
orthography rule (stated in the 1895 preface and visible in both volumes), medium for Bentley's own
reconstruction of earlier Kongo history, which is a missionary's argument and is reported as his.

Access date for both sources: 2026-10-22. Route for both: direct plain GET, HTTP 200, no proxy.

## What the two volumes are, and where the entries sit

- 1887 volume: preface in roman numerals (the printed folios run from X at object 13 to XXIII at
  object 27); English-Kongo dictionary pp. 1-244; Kongo-English dictionary pp. 245-482; appendices
  pp. 483-514, the English-Kongo appendix starting at p. 485 and errata at pp. 510-514; grammar
  last, with its own pagination. Section headings verified at scan objects 28, 272, 510, 512 and 542;
  page headers verified at objects 300 (p. 273), 380 (p. 353), 500 (p. 473) and 536 (p. 509).
- 1895 volume: preface pp. i-vii; appendix to the English-Kongo dictionary from p. 721; an appendix
  to the Kongo-English dictionary later in the volume (header 827 at object 116); a grammar appendix
  (objects 234-335); an index to p. 1052. Headers read at objects 11 (p. 722), 150 (p. 861), 200
  (p. 911), 219 (p. 930), 256 (p. 967), 340 (p. 1051).

So a worldbuilder looking for Kongo head words (nkixi, mfumu, ekanda) must go to the 1887 volume's
second dictionary (pp. 245-482), not its first; the 1887 text is English-Kongo until p. 244.

## The dialect statement (the assignment's first question)

Bentley prints his own claim on p. xi of the preface: the Kongo "as spoken at San Salvador" is the
best medium of speech as far as Stanley Pool, because it is the language of the old capital. On
p. xii he then argues that the 1659 Brusciotto grammar, long treated as the oldest Kongo grammar,
records not the court but the coastal Sonyo (Soyo) dialect, identifying its R-for-D and its verb
zitisa; he adds that Cannecattim's 1804 vocabulary is explicitly the Sonho dialect, and that the
1624 catechism shows D for R as court Kongo did then and does still. On p. xxi he states the
preference plainly: the mission studied the form spoken at the old capital because it had the widest
range, while some dialects were "harsh, uncouth, restricted to small districts". The preface also
lists the old kingdom's provinces from Pigafetta (1591): Mbamba, Sonio (Soyo, from San Antonio at the
river mouth), Nsundi, Mpangu, Mbata and Mpemba, with modern identifications.

Design consequence: the dictionary is evidence for one prestige variety of Kikongo/Koongo in the
1880s, and its author himself warns that the earliest grammar is a different, coastal variety. An
entry that uses Bentley for "the Kongo language" without that distinction will over-read him.

## The spelling rule that hides the key words (the assignment's second question)

The 1895 preface, p. vi, states the rule that explains why the two volumes look like different
languages: before i, s and z were written x and j in the 1887 dictionary, and s and z in the 1895
appendix. Hence "simba appears as ximba under X in the Dictionary, and as simba under S in the
Appendix". This is why the 1887 volume has nkixi (not nkisi) and njimbu (not nzimbu): my own first
searches of the 1887 text for nkisi and nzimbu returned nothing, and the entries sit under Nkixi
(p. 383) and under Money, n., njimbu (p. 139). A worldbuilder naming a charm or a currency should
decide which of the two spellings the entry's own orthography uses and say so once.

## Head entries located and read

- Mfumu, p. 348: "chief, sovereign, emperor, king; master, lord, proprietor; free man, nobleman,
  gentleman; sir!" with mfumu a muntu, a free person, and, on p. 349, mfumu ankento, queen or
  chieftainess, and mfumu a nzo, householder. Image-checked.
- Chief, p. 35: mfumu for chief, nkuluntu for an elder, ntinu for king, nyadi for ruler, nkaka as a
  very respectful title, nkumbi for a sub-chief under a king. Image-checked at object 62.
- Clan, p. 36: ekanda and luvila; clans are "intermixed freely" but mutually responsible; all
  relationships and inheritances go through the mother; luvila is the word used when asking a man his
  clan, ekanda otherwise; named makanda include Kilukene, Kivaxi, Kinlaza, Nenlaza. The Kongo-English
  side repeats it: Ekanda, 8, clan, family (p. 267); Luvila, 10, relationship, family, clan (p. 333);
  and Kanda, 6, by itself is the underpart of an animal's paw (p. 288), with the grammar deriving
  ekanda, a paw-mark, from it. So "kanda" alone is not the clan word in this record.
- Nkixi, p. 383: a charm, an enchantment, a fetish, medicine; -ankixi, fetish; nzo nkixi, a grave;
  the proverb nkixi ampa mumbana makazu, a new fetish takes a lot of kola nut, glossed as a new broom
  sweeps clean. The same page gives Nkidi a mbwa and Nkidimbwa as cowrie shell, a separate word from
  the money terms.
- Fetish, p. 82: nkixi, with vanda o nkixi, to make a charm, lembula, to destroy its influence, and
  "For further matters, see Appendix".
- Money, p. 139: njimbu, and for goods, mbongo. Image-checked.
- Emperor, p. 69: "The king of Kongo is styled, ntotela (2) ntinu nekongo." Ntotela as a head word is
  at p. 401, glossed emperor, title of the kings of Congo.

## Traps for integration

1. The archive.org page-number sidecar for the 1887 item is off by one: dictionarygramma00bent_page_numbers.json lists object 15 as XI, but the image of object 13 prints X and object 15 prints XII. Never take a folio from that file; use the printed number in the page header or the image.
2. Three page indices are in play: the djvu object index (0-based, what page/nN.jpg uses), the PDF page (object plus one), and the printed folio. Every locator in this report is the printed folio, with the image page given in the route line. The printed folios are not a constant offset from the objects (inserted leaves shift it), so do not compute them.
3. Two-column pages: the text layer interleaves the columns, so a naive search can splice two entries into one sentence. Every quotation above was read on the image; a checker working from the djvu text alone will see garbled neighbours.
4. The 1887 text yields nothing for "nkisi" or "nzimbu"; the forms are nkixi and njimbu. Any automated search for the modern spellings must also try x and j forms.
5. "Fetish" is Bentley's gloss and must stay inside quotation marks, flagged as the source's word; the appendix itself shows an earlier generation of missionaries stretching nkisi to mean holy.
6. The 1887 volume is two dictionaries back to back: English-Kongo pp. 1-244, Kongo-English pp. 245-484. A citation to "Bentley 1887, p. 300" is in the Kongo-English part; a citation to p. 82 is in the English-Kongo part and its head word is the English one.
