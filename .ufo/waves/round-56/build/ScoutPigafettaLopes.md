## What the row hands the entry: recorded terms and what the report says they mean

All page locators below are in the same 1881 volume; the words are the report's, not Kikongo spellings confirmed elsewhere.

- Mani, "Lord", prefixed to a place: Mani Bamba, Mani Lemba, Mani Dandi, Mani Bengo, Mani Loanda, Mani Corimba, Mani Coanza, Mani Cazzanzi (p. 43). The same pattern is used for the province lords Don Sebastião Manibamba and his lieutenant Don António Mani Bamba.
- The king's style is a list of provinces and lost claims: King of Congo and of Abundos, Matama, Quizama, Angola, Angoi, Cacongo, the seven Kingdoms of Congere Amolaza, the Pangelungos, Lord of the River Zaire, the Anziquos, Anziquana and Loango (p. 42). The report adds that in process of time these were lost, though the titles remain.
- Bamba is called the kingdom's key, buckler, sword and defence, able to field four hundred thousand armed men, which is the sixth part of the kingdom (p. 44). The number is the reporter's rhetoric, not a count.
- The capital is called San Salvador, "formerly known as Banza in the language of the country, which generally means Court" (p. 65); the report describes the king's and the Portuguese quarters, walls built by Dom Afonso, and nobles' houses spreading outside them (pp. 65-67).
- Loanda island's towns are called Libata in the language of the country, seven or eight of them, the chief one il Santo Spirito, where a governor sent from Kongo administers justice and collects the shells (p. 19). Provisions are brought from the mainland and exchanged for Lumache (p. 20).
- The people are called Mociconghi in their own tongue (p. 110), singular Mocicongo in the same chapter (p. 110). The report treats the word as an endonym but spells it in Portuguese orthography.
- Dress terms: the net-like over-garment Incutto, thrown back over the right shoulder, with a zebra's tail carried on the same shoulder (p. 108); cloth names Incorimbas, Enzachas, Infulas, Maricas, Tangas, Engombos (p. 31); the tree Enzanda, whose inner bark makes cloth for the poorest (p. 19).
- Court: the king eats alone, princes stand with heads covered, vessels of gold and silver, a guard of Anzichi, drums audible five or six miles off, audience twice a week to the great men only, no writing in the Kongo tongue, disputes with Portuguese judged by Portuguese law or the Portuguese consul (pp. 110-111). Exile to a desert island is preferred to execution (p. 110).
- Succession: the narrative runs through Dom Francisco, Dom Diego, the three simultaneous claimants, the murders in the church, Dom Henrique chosen as a brother of the late king, and the extinction of the old royal line on his death, after which Dom Álvaro was chosen by common consent (pp. 92-94).

## Where the record over-reads or misnames

- "Lumache" is Italian for snails or shelled creatures, not a Kikongo word; the report gives no local name for the money shells. The string nzimbu does not occur anywhere in the 1881 text (checked: zero hits), so this row cannot source that term.
- The cloth passage explains Kongo weaving through European categories (velvets, brocades, satins, taffetas, damasks, p. 30) and says the local products are "reckoned much more valuable than our brocades" (p. 30). The European words are analogies; only the six names on p. 31 are presented as local.
- The capital's name is given as Banza and glossed "Court" (p. 65); that is a generic word for a royal residence, so the entry should not treat Banza as a proper name for Mbanza Kongo without another source.
- The king's style (p. 42) is a claim of title over regions now governed by others, including Angola, whose governor, the report says, has since the king became a Christian made himself absolute ruler (pp. 32-33). Titular claim and effective rule must be kept apart.
- The frame is European: Prester John, Ethiopia, the Nile's source, and comparisons with Timbuktu, China and Bengal (pp. 19-20). The geographic chapters are speculation; the court chapters read as eyewitness-derived.
- The report reached print at two removes: Pigafetta in Rome set down what the Portuguese trader Duarte Lopes told him, as the title page states. The translator's notes in the same volume correct several names, which is itself evidence that the transcription layer is unstable.
- The report's present is after 1580, since it calls the Kingdom of Congo "belonging to the King of Spain" (p. 107) during the Iberian Union; it names Don Sebastião Manibamba as cousin of King Dom Álvaro, "lately deceased" (p. 43).
- The province names Bamba, Sogno, Sundi, Pango, Batta and Pemba are the report's Portuguese spellings. They are often equated with Mbamba, Soyo, Nsundi, Mpangu, Mbata and Mpemba, but this row does not itself make those equations, so a world bible should record the modern identification as coming from a different source.

## Gaps this row leaves open

- No Kikongo term for the shell money and no exchange rates; the report gives only that nothing was bought without Lumache (p. 19).
- Nothing on minkisi, divination, burial, or the four-point cosmogram; those must come from other rows.
- Nothing on Kikongo grammar, dictionaries, or catechisms; the report is a travel and court description, not a linguistic source.
- No absolute dates for kings; the succession is episodic and its chronology has to be reconciled with Portuguese and modern histories elsewhere.
- The report's numbers, distances and ethnonyms are not internally consistent; treat every figure as the reporter's estimate.
