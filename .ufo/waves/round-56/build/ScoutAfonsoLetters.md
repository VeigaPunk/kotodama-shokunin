# ScoutAfonsoLetters — claim C calibration item: the letters of Afonso I of Kongo (1526)

Question: a public primary text of Afonso I Mvemba a Nzinga's letters to João III on the slave trade, plus one open scholarly source on how to read them (complaint against unlicensed trade, not abolition).

Verdict: both found and opened. The primary text is a scholarly documentary edition, António Brásio's *Monumenta Missionaria Africana*, vol. I (1471–1531), on a public Internet Archive scan (tier A). The reading is a research article by Ronaldo Vainfas and Marina de Mello e Souza in *Tempo* (UFF), openly hosted by the journal (tier B). Its argument is the royal-monopoly and lawful-routes reading; the article itself does not date the complaint to 1526, so the date comes from the primary row. No tools installed.

draft sentences:
- Read the letters as enforcement of the royal monopoly: the article describes merchants "desrespeitando as rotas estabelecidas e o monopólio real", so a worldbuilder should make contraband routes, not the mere presence of Europeans, the political crisis. (NEW-ScoutAfonsoLetters-2, section "Conversão e catolização", paragraph beginning "Quando o comércio de pessoas fugiu do controle do rei")
- Because "O comércio de escravos era antigo naquela região", the setting needs Kongo's own lawful slave institutions and route chiefs before 1483, with the Portuguese entering an existing trade. (NEW-ScoutAfonsoLetters-2, same section and paragraph)
- The article's point that "nobres congoleses estavam sendo capturados em guerras interprovinciais" lets provincial war supply both the captives and the scandal. (NEW-ScoutAfonsoLetters-2, same section and paragraph)
- It says Afonso took the throne after a fratricidal succession struggle, so design contested royal succession and noble factions, not automatic inheritance. (NEW-ScoutAfonsoLetters-2, section "Conversão e catolização", paragraph beginning "Morto D.João I do Congo")
- It records that after the 1512 Regimento carried by Simão da Silva provincial lineage chiefs began to carry the Portuguese titles condes, marqueses and duques, a decision about how hybrid the court's titles look. (NEW-ScoutAfonsoLetters-2, section "Conversão e catolização", paragraph beginning "Mas não foi apenas o cristianismo")
- It explains "São Salvador" as the name given to mbanza Kongo after the kings' conversion, so a worldbuilder must decide which name the court uses in which period and which name outsiders use. (NEW-ScoutAfonsoLetters-2, section "Congo descoberto", paragraph beginning "São Salvador")

## Notes for the entry

- Names and offices a worldbuilder can use from these pages: *Mani Kongo* / "Rey de Comgo"; the capital *cidade de Congo* (São Salvador / mbanza Kongo); the port "Sonho"; *fidallgos* and *ofiçiaes de nossa Corte*; the offices *meirinho-mor* and *armador-mor* named among the three verifiers (the OCR prints the personal names inconsistently: Brásio "dom Pedro Manjpunzo" and "dom Manoell Manjissaba", Paiva Manso "dom pedro manipanza" and "dom manoell manissaba", so do not fix a spelling without a manuscript check).
- Objects: the garment receipt gives capuz, capa de cotray, pelote, jubam de damasco, calças, and the arca that carried them; it calls the consignment "tres vestidos" while itemising many pieces, so "vestido" here is a set or outfit, not one garment.
- Chronology: 1512 Regimento of Simão da Silva; 18 January 1526 garment receipt; 6 July 1526 letter on kidnapping and depopulation; 18 October 1526 letter setting the verification rule.
- Cross-check (opened, not offered as a row because the assignment capped sources at two): Paiva Manso, *Historia do Congo (Documentos)*, Lisbon 1877, archive.org item `historiadocongod00jorduoft`, prints the same letters as docs XXV, XXVII and XXIX with cleaner OCR; its heading dates the garment receipt 1520 while its own text reads 1526, which supports Brásio's 1526.
- Opened but not used: the World History Commons teaching module by James Diskant reads the letters as an appeal to end the slave trade and mentions twenty-four letters; it is a teaching resource, not research, and its count was not verified. The SciELO *Revista de História* article by Thiago Sapede covers Kongo diplomatic letters of 1780–1830, outside this item.
- Reading caution: the 6 July letter also asks that in his kingdoms there be no trade in slaves and no outlet for them (Brásio, doc. 142, pp. 469–470), which is stronger than the October licensing rule; treat the pair as a demand and its implementation, and do not flatten them into one consistent abolition programme.
- Confidence: high for the quoted wording and for the Brásio page numbers; medium for the document number of the 6 July letter, because the volume's table of contents prints the same letter at p. 468 under a number the OCR reads as 140 while the document heading reads 142.
