# ScoutKongoDiaspora — claim C source report (Kongo in the Atlantic diaspora, one-way pointer)

Result: two sources opened and read in full text this session. Thornton 1991 (AHR, JSTOR press scan publicly served by a James Madison University library host) covers the Kongo background of the Stono rebels: the "Angola" label on colonial records, voluntary conversion under João I, the pre-1665 centralized kingdom and the civil-war era, provincial titles such as Mani Lumbo, and the source's own stated limit that the record dries up after about 1720. Dianteill 2002 (Archives de sciences sociales des religions 117, full text open via the journal's own facsimile PDF) covers Kongo-derived religion in Cuba: the kongo/congo naming distinction, the nganga/ganga shift, the nkisi definition and bisimbi, the Antonian movement, and his conclusion that royal rites vanished while minkisi became the centre. Neither source is about the kingdom's grammar, dress or shell money; they carry diaspora transmission, naming and limits.

draft sentences:
- A colonial or enslaver's record that says "Angola" may name the stretch of central Africa English shippers called the Angola coast rather than the Portuguese colony or the Kingdom of Kongo; a worldbuilder should treat diaspora ethnicity labels as shipper tags, not polity names (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-1, p. 1104).
- Most of the central Africans enslaved into English America between about 1710 and 1740 were probably from Kongo, in Thornton's hedged reckoning, so an "Angolan" captive in a 1730s British colony plausibly carries Kongo culture (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-1, p. 1105).
- Kongo's Christianity was a self-declared identity, not colonial branding: the kingdom was voluntarily converted with the baptism of King Nzinga Nkuwu as João I in 1491, and Thornton notes some eighteenth-century priests called the Kongolese "Christians in name only" while the Kongolese regarded themselves as Christians (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-1, p. 1106).
- The record's own vocabulary hands a worldbuilder ready forms: European sources render titles with peerage ranks, the principality of Nsoyo and the duchy of Mbamba, and give the Kongo formula "Mani Lumbo" for a lord and "libata" for a village (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-1, pp. 1108-1109).
- Kongo's era turns on 1665: before then a centralized kingdom where enslavement came by external attack, after then decades of civil war that fed the trade, with a formal restoration under Pedro IV between 1709 and 1716 and intermittent wars after (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-1, p. 1109).
- Source discipline matters at the kingdom's end as much as its height: the documentary record dries up after about 1720, so a worldbuilder staging late-Kongo scenes should expect scattered incident reports rather than dense coverage (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-1, p. 1109).

quotes:
- p. 59: "les ethnonymes utilisés dépendaient plus des factoreries sur la côte africaine que des peuples eux-mêmes"
- p. 60, note 2: "« kongo » désigne le peuple, la langue, la religion et la culture du royaume africain ; « congo » désigne les individus déportés à Cuba"
- p. 61, note 5: "Alors que le terme nganga désigne un guérisseur traditionnel en Afrique, il désigne à Cuba le récipient où est « fixé » un esprit"
- p. 63, note 8: "où tout nkisi est un objet matériel contenant un esprit"
- p. 65: "un mouvement messianique vît le jour au XVIIIe, mené par une princesse nommée Dona Béatrice qui se disait inspirée par saint Antoine"
- p. 76: "Les minkisi, qui ne représentaient qu'une partie des objets rituels en pays kongo, sont devenus le centre de l'attention des paleros"
teaches: Dianteill shows palo monte keeps Kongo terms while shifting their referents, warns that colonial ethnonyms come from trading posts, and argues Cuban palo layers Kongo, Yoruba, Catholic, Haitian and spiritist inputs.
draft sentences:
- Colonial name lists are trader-made, not self-names: the ethnonyms used in Cuba depended more on the trading posts on the African coast than on the peoples themselves, so a worldbuilder should let a slaver's roster misname a captive's polity (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-2, p. 59).
- Kongo words cross the ocean and change jobs: nganga names a traditional healer in Africa but in Cuba the vessel where a spirit is fixed, the ganga or prenda, an iron cauldron of sticks, earth, stones, metal and bone (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-2, p. 61, note 5).
- An nkisi is not only a nail-studded statue: Dianteill glosses it as any material object containing a spirit, with remedies and charged sculptures alike counting once loaded with bilongo (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-2, pp. 63-64).
- Kongo already carried a Christian layer before the diaspora: the messianic movement led by the princess Dona Béatrice, who said she was inspired by Saint Anthony, tried to restore the king in San Salvador where she claimed Christ was born, and she died at the stake in 1706 (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-2, p. 65).
- For the diaspora as a one-way pointer: in Cuba the minkisi, only part of Kongo's ritual objects at home, became the centre of palero practice, while every rite tied to the royal function disappeared (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-2, p. 76).
- The ritual language survives inside the religion: Dianteill transcribes a palero notebook chant whose Kikongo glosses include fwidi for death, mpangi for close kin and nzàzi for lightning, so a worldbuilder can keep an African lexicon in use long after the polity's rites are gone (NEW-ScoutKongoDiaspora-2, p. 67, note 15).
