## What the evidence settles for the entry

- The cap of rank is **mpu** (plural **zimpu**), long ranked form **mpu ngola**; it is raffia,
  knotted, and ranked by attachments (leopard claws), not by a metal crown. Attested in Loango
  and Kongo from at least 1607 in the museum's account, and a Copenhagen example reached Europe
  before 1674 (Fromont, figure 6 caption, p. 22).
- Kongo nobility wore the **Order of Christ cross with hollow branches of equal length** on
  medallions, embroidered coats, wax seals, signatures, rock paintings and caps; on a cap it
  "turned the mpu into an emblem of Christian nobility" (Fromont, p. 25).
- Crucifixes and large iron swords were **worn regalia**, one set with the caps (Fromont, p. 19);
  a prestige **staff** was "an insignia of office" (Met 318318).
- The **local term for a Kongo crucifix is nkangi kiditu** in the open teaching annotation, and
  that page lists the surrounding small figures as an open question, not a doctrine (WHC).
- The **dikenga reading is hedged by the holding museum itself** ("likely pre-Christian",
  CMA 2010.444), so the entry must carry it as one interpretation among others.
- **Change over periods is documented as loss and improvisation**: after the 1665 battle of
  Mbwila the European-style crown was gone and Pedro IV was crowned in 1696 with a hat bearing
  the royal coat of arms (Fromont, p. 23).

## Cautions for the entry

- **nkangi kiditu is thin.** Fromont 2017 does not use the term anywhere (checked the full extracted text), and no museum record opened here does either; the term appears only as the World History Commons page title, which cites nothing on the page. The entry should either cite WHC as a tier-B lead for the name and keep the gloss modest, or drop the term to a parenthetical.
- **Two museums, two datings of the same cap class.** Cleveland dates its mpu ngola late 1800s–early 1900s and credits a Kongo-style maker, possibly Yombe; Fromont's Copenhagen mpu is "before 1674" (figure 6 caption, p. 22) and the Met's is late 19th century. A worldbuilder can use the 1674 object as the early anchor and the Cleveland cap as the late survival, but the entry should not present one date range as the type's whole life.
- **The dikenga sentence is the museum's, and it hedges.** Keep "likely" and "references"; do not upgrade it to "Kongo crucifixes encode the dikenga".
- **The Order of Christ reading of a sword is explicitly cautious in the source.** Fromont writes of a Berlin sword guard that the openwork crosses "could be that of the Order of Christ" (p. 20); on the cap the identification is stated firmly (p. 25). Do not transfer the firm reading to every openwork cross.
- **Outsider's record over-reads.** The same article calls the earliest European Kongo dress plates "partly fanciful, partly accurate" (p. 15), which is the licence a worldbuilder needs to let an in-world foreign engraver get details wrong.
- **Museum API text carries markup.** The Cleveland descriptions wrap italicized Kongo terms in `em` tags (`<em>Zimpu </em>`, `<em>dikenga dia Kongo</em>`). The quotes above are the rendered text with the tags removed; the L1 should paste the rendered text, never the tags, and never an HTML character reference.
