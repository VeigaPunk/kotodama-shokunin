## 3. Gesture types and what each carries

The six rows are working categories a designer picks from, and they are loadings, not boxes: one gesture can be iconic and deictic at once (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-1, §5; NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2). Co-speech gesture as such is not conventionalized and systematic the way a sign language is (`sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64`), so each row says how far a community fixes it.

| Type | What it carries | How conventional it is | Example | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iconic | A depiction of the referent's concrete shape or action: iconic gestures ‘bear a close formal relationship to the semantic content of the speech’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | None: improvised per utterance (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | ‘An oscillating downward motion starting high to convey the wavy nature of a road descending a mountain’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, Table 3) | Decide which concrete referents your speakers can draw by hand and path; the typical failure is a culture whose nouns are all abstractions yet everyone pantomimes |
| Metaphoric | An abstract idea through a physical source domain — size, container, path: the gesture ‘presents an abstract idea rather than a concrete object or event’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | Low: the mapping is shared, the drawing improvised (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | ‘The hands with palms facing one another held outward at the width of the body’ to symbolize the size of an idea (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, Table 3); a cupped palm as if holding a discursive object (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-1, §1) | Fix one source domain per family of abstractions and keep it stable; the typical failure is two communities mapping the same abstract to opposite images |
| Deictic (pointing) | Reference to a point in space or time: McNeill limits the deictic to pointing, and ‘deixis in gesture is commonly thought of just in terms of pointing’, ‘especially with the index finger or open hand in many cultures’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | Medium: the pointing anatomy is conventional, the referent is inferred (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | ‘A pointed forefinger marking a position to the left of the interlocutor to establish the reference point for a person or place under discussion’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, Table 3) | Choose the pointing anatomy per community and the frame it points in; the forms and their communities are in §4, the absolute-frame constraint with `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:36` |
| Beat | Rhythm and prosody, not content: the hand ‘moves along with the rhythmical pulsation of speech’, and a beat can overlay the other types (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | Lowest: any speech can be beaten (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, §1) | ‘A clasped fist rising and falling on every word or syllable’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, Table 3) | Set your language's stress, then let the beat mark it; the typical failure is a beat on every syllable, which spends the emphasis |
| Emblem | A fixed dictionary meaning in a conventional form: Efron's emblems are ‘conventionalized body movements, which have fixed meanings in the culture they are used in’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2) | Highest: it can stand alone as a whole utterance (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-1, §1; NEW-ScribeSec03Types-3) | ‘Emblems such as the “OK” sign have independent status as symbolic forms’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-1, §1); ‘A thumbs up for “good”’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, Table 3) | Hand the emblem lexicon and its geography to `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:79`; finger counting is body code too, at `numerals-and-measures.md:107`; the typical failure is one emblem with two glosses in a single scene |
| Interactive or recurrent (palm-up open hand) | The interaction, not the topic: Bavelas et al.'s four roles, as reported by Williamson et al., are ‘citing the other's previous contribution; seeking agreement, understanding, or help; the delivery of new versus shared information; and events around the speaking turn’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, §1); the palm-up epistemic groups absence of knowledge, ability or concern, uncertainty, interrogatives, hypotheticality, obviousness and exclamatives (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-3, "Discussions of palm-ups in gesture") | Medium-high: the form is widespread, the glosses local; for Müller ‘this family is rooted in practical actions of giving and receiving objects’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-3) | an outstretched palm-up with co-occurring speech, ‘appearing to give reassurance to the confederate’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-4, Table 3); in Syuba (Tibeto-Burman, Nepal) ‘the emblematic form of the gesture is produced without speech to ask, “What are you doing?”’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-3) | Decide whether your culture has a quotable palm-up and which glosses it licenses; the typical failure is one gloss in every scene, since ‘form is not a foolproof guide to meaning’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-3) |

Two rules follow. First, decide per community which gestures are dictionary items and which are improvised — that split is the conventionality column, and it is what a scene can rely on (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-1, §1; NEW-ScribeSec03Types-2, §2).
Second, write the type as a load, not a label: ‘most gestures are multifaceted—iconicity is combined with deixis, deixis with metaphoricity, and so forth’ (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-1, §5), and the palm-up family already splits into presentational and epistemic variants (NEW-ScribeSec03Types-3, Abstract).
