## 9. Acquisition and learning

A gesture register is learned in a fixed order — before speech, beside speech and across languages — and each stage fixes one thing a designer must decide for the fiction (design frame, **H**).

| stage | what the opened source shows | design consequence in the fiction |
|---|---|---|
| preverbal pointing, around 12 months | index-finger pointing emerges "around 12 months of age" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 699), and in seven field sites — Rossel Island (Yélı̂ Dnye), Bali, Kyoto, Montaro Valley (Peru), the Tzeltal and Yucatec Maya areas of Mexico, and Nova Scotia (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, Table 1, p. 702) — "by 10–14 months of age, infants and their caregivers pointed in all cultures in the same basic situation with similar frequencies" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 698), while the majority of each site's infants began "with the index finger between 10 and 14 months of age, with no significant differences between cultures" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 705) | the child in the fiction points before it can name what it points at: give the culture a pointing repertoire its adults already use (§4), so a child's first request has a form an adult can read; the failure is a child whose first act of communication is a spoken word in a culture whose adults expect a point |
| gesture leads speech | children produced a gesture for an object "3.0 months" on average before the word for it (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-2, p. 369), and gesture-plus-word pairs carrying two different elements — pointing at a bird while saying "nap" — predicted the onset of two-word speech, while pairs repeating the same referent did not (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-2, pp. 367, 369) | the child's early hand must add information the word does not carry, because a pair that repeats the referent predicts nothing; the failure is a child whose gesture only echoes the word, so the pair adds no meaning and the next stage has nothing to build on |
| adults' pointing form is taught, not given | index-finger pointing "has been described as non-universal among adults but foundational to linguistic communication in ontogeny" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 701), and "certain forms of pointing may be learned through explicit teaching" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 700) | the newcomer is marked by the hand: decide whether the community teaches its pointing form in households, at school or nowhere, and let the outsider's different form stay visible; the failure is assuming one form everywhere — and the claim that some communities lack index-finger pointing altogether is `(to-verify)`, since the source reports it rests on untraceable hearsay (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 708) |
| a second-language learner keeps the first language's gestures | adult learners of L2 Dutch showed "a pattern similar to their L1 preferences but different from those of native Dutch speakers" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-3, ¶26), and learners of L2 French showed "both transfer of L1 semantic representations into the L2 and beginning shifts towards target representations" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-3, ¶29); heritage speakers of Turkish in the Netherlands kept "able to maintain gesture rate for each language" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-4, p. 422), with no rate transfer between the high-gesture Turkish and low-gesture Dutch patterns (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-4, p. 423) | the translator in the fiction carries one community's hands across two languages: decide whether the translator's L1 gestures persist in the L2 and write the mismatch into the scene; the failure is a newcomer or translator who instantly adopts the target community's gestures, so nobody can be read as a stranger in the hands |

Status: the developmental and transfer findings are **B** — bounded to the sampled sites, ages and tasks, and Liszkowski's own caution is that "any claims about universality must be treated with caution" (NEW-ScribeSec09Acquisition-1, p. 708) — and the design column is the lane's own heuristic (**H**, `../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`); §4 owns the pointing forms, the emblem and body-code register stays at `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:77-93`, the co-speech-gesture boundary row at `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64` and `:161`, finger counting at `numerals-and-measures.md:107`, and the absolute-frame pointing case at `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:6` and `:36`.
