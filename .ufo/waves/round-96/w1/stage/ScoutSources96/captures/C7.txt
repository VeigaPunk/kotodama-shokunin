Ritual speech, Performativity, and Modality Alexander Maxim Frangulov Balliol College University of Oxford A thesis submitted for the degree of Master of Philosophy Trinity 2023 Ritual speech, Performativity, and Modality 1 Introduction The power of the word to affect change in the world beyond one’s immediate conditions has long been ruminated by scholars of philosophy, theology, and anthropology. Hitherto, no study has been done in a formal semantic framework to model what it is that people are trying to do when they attempt to speak into existence an event or state of affairs which they may not be regularly thought to be in control of. This thesis attempts to fill this theoretical gap. It explores the semantic and pragmatic mechanisms which a speaker harnesses when they perform a ritual, spell, curse, charm, incantation, blessing, hymn, or mantra. Not only does the present endeavor deepen our scientific understanding of the human language behavior of ritual speech, but this thesis will show that the exclusion of this type of speech from previous research on performative theory, in particular, has skewed the data and therefore limited the formal theories of and broad generalizations about the class of speech acts. This thesis will also investigate these questions in part through exploration the ancient ritual corpora of the Vedic hymns of the Rigveda and the collection of Hittite ritual tablets. The central thread to this thesis is a number of observations around the generalization that a performative utterance may not have a quantified subject determiner phrase (DP)1 (such as most students) without the presence of the word hereby (Eckardt 2012). The data put forth and the reasoning offered will show the generalization to be false when ritual and magic performative speech is considered. Further, since the theory of performativity which this thesis primarily analyses, that of Eckardt (2012), is formulated to account for exactly that generalization, the data presented in this work will suggest certain adjustments to that formal analysis of performative utterances. Specifically, Eckardt (2012) posits that quantified subjects of sentences without hereby prevent the self-referentiality which she claims is necessary for performative interpretation. This self-reference which allows performative interpretation is a pragmatic process by which the speaker-hearer attempts to equate the event of the performative verb with the event of the ongoing transfer of information. If this equivalence can be made then the utterance may be interpreted as performative. The key observation of the present work is that sentences without hereby, with quantified subject DPs, do seem to be interpretable as performative utterances when they contain modals such 1 This work follows Eckardt (2012) in referring to such subjects as ‘quantified subject DPs’ as opposed to ‘Quantifier Phrase (QP) subjects’ 2 as may and shall. This thesis, in response, puts forward an account of quantified subject DPs in performatives by positing that the ‘default speech event’ (the assertion event) of modal utterances of Hacquard (2010) can attract the inference of self-referentiality away from the root event, thus circumventing the agent uniqueness problem which Eckardt (2012) identifies lies at the source of the quantification problem which prevents performative interpretation. A classic performative utterance can be seen in the utterance of (1). (1) I hereby promise to find the ring. The speaker of an utterance of (1) is not simply describing a state of affairs. They are performing the action expressed in the sentence and stating, in a self-referential way, that that is what they are doing by uttering that sentence. Sentences with hereby are invariably interpreted as performative utterances. However, a sentence need not contain hereby to be used in a performative utterance. (2) I promise to find the ring. An utterance of (2) can be a performance of a promising event. It can, through its utterance create a true promising event or state of promise. Eckardt (2012), Gruber (1975), propose that an utterance without hereby becomes performative through the assumption that the utterance is self-referential. It is held as a common generalization of performative utterances, for example by Eckardt (2012), that without hereby to force a performative interpretation, it is not possible to have a performative reading of an utterance with a quantified subject DP such as most, some, many students.... Eckardt (2012) argues that this is because the quantified subject DP blocks the inference of self-referentiality. 3 The key observation of the present work is that, despite this generalization, sentences without hereby, with quantified subject DPs, do seem to be interpretable as performative utterances when they contain modals such as may and shall. (4) Two gods shall protect you!2 An utterance of (3) behaves as Eckardt (2012) would predict, as it does not seem possible to interpret the utterance as itself being a performance of a thanking; rather it seems to be a description of one. An utterance of (4), on the other hand, seems to be able to do more than simply describe an eventuality. If said by the right speaker in the right context, for example in the context of a ritual, it may be understood by the hearers of such an utterance that the speaking of (4) performs an action by directly and self-referentially expressing that utterance. In other words, upon being spoken with the proper authority and circumstance the utterance, by nature of the fact that it was spoken in such a way that the performative event can be identified with the speaking event, the utterance can be understood to have been made true by being performed. The next part of this section will give evidence for the performativity of ritual speech and practice the performative intuition along the way. After the publication of Austin (1962), many authors have applied his concept of performativity to human ritual practices. Rituals are referred to as involving performative speech in Della Casa (2021), Frankfurter (2019), Tambiah (1968), Wheelock (1982), among others. That ritual speech should be seen as performative is unsurprising. Performative utterances purport to change the world by being spoken. In the same way, many cultures believed ritual speech to have the power to change the world. No work on language or the religions of the world fails to mention the ancient belief in the magical power of the word (Tambiah 1968). (Tambiah 1968: p. 182) elaborates on the ancient belief in the power of the word: “The Vedic hymns speculated on vac (the word) and asserted that the gods ruled the world through magical formulae; the Parsi religion states that in the battle between good and evil it was through the spoken word that chaos was transformed into cosmos; ancient Egypt believed in a God of the Word; the Semites and the Sumerians have held that the world and its objects were created by the word of God; and the Greek doctrine of logos postulated that the soul or essence of things resided in their names.” In fact Greek and Latin curse tablets (Lt. tabella defixionis Gk. κατάδεσμος) generally involved only the inscription of the target’s name on the tablet, though there was likely a spoken ritual associated with the creation of the tablets, less frequently tablets included a curse verb or gods appealed to (Urbanová & Cuzzolin 2016). The venerated position of language and its magical association is seen in many ways. ‘Abracadabra’ represents the Latin belief that the delimiting of characters was thought to have magical power and English ‘glamour’ (first meaning enchanting) had its origin in ‘grammar’(Macleod & Mees 2006). People have thus commonly believed that the world could be changed through words, paralleling the way performatives change the world. Urbanová & Cuzzolin (2016) positioned curses as being both what Austin (1962) calls ‘behabitive’ in that the utterance expresses the speakers attitude towards the cursed individual, and also what he calls ‘exercitive’ because the curse purports to form a directive (Urbanová & Cuzzolin 2016). The mythological re-contextualization of the space is reflected in the practice of the historiola; in which a mythological story is recounted which parallels the current situation usually ending with a similia similibus: as it happened then so it happens now (Della Casa 2021). In these ways, we see that ritual contexts involve augmenting the power of the ritual speaker. A performative utterance in this context is believed by that community to be able to do things such as heal or bless. Searle (1989) notes supernatural performatives but places them under declarations, does not think they have a performative element. He writes that "When God says, "Let there be light!", that I take it is a declaration. It is not a promise; it doesn’t mean, "When I get around to it, I’ll make light for you." And it is not an order; it doesn’t mean, "Sam over there, turn on the lights." It makes it the case by fiat that light exists. Fairy stories, by the way, are full of declarations performed by witches, wizards, magicians, etc. We ordinary humans do not have the ability to perform supernatural declarations, but we do have a quasi-magical power nonetheless of bringing about changes in the world through our utterances; and we are given this power by a kind of human agreement." (Searle 1989: p. 549) While "Let there be light!" is certainly not a promise, Searle’s summation that such utterances are not performatives is not as clear. The distinction he draws between declarations and performatives lies in what sort of event they create. Performatives, for Searle, create linguistic events such as promises orderings or statements whereas declarations, as the broader category which contains performatives, produces linguistic or non-linguistic events. Prototypical examples of declarations for him being marriage, war, or adjournment (Searle 1989). The work of Eckardt (2012), Diesing & McConnell-Ginet (2012), and the reasoning semantic nuances of this form compared to other modalities is not the topic of the thesis but an analysis of the ancient greek optative and its status as a psedo-directive by Bianconi & Magni (2022) may interest the reader. 14 presented below support the view that the event created in a performative utterance need not be a linguistic event. Eckardt (2012) specifies that a performative need only be selfreferential and speaker-defined as performative, while Diesing & McConnell-Ginet (2012) view the spells of the JK Rowling’s Harry Potter series as being performative. The distinction between linguistic and non-linguistic entities which can be declared is not clear upon close inspection. War is no less a social object than a promise. It is arguably easier to perform a promise with no linguistic event (such as a promise within one’s own heart), than it is to perform a war without a linguistic declaration/performative event. The discussion in §2.4 will further question whether it cannot be the case that speech communities may define what we may think of as non-linguistic events such as a healing event, as an event which can be a linguistic event in their community. Such that a healing is something that can be performed by speaking words. The distinction Searle draws between language and the world is not one which nor one which this thesis believes is reflected in the mechanisms of performative speech. It is the benefit of performative speech that it may purport to create non-linguistic entities which is the reason for its popularity in ritual and magical speech. This thesis therefore equates declarations and performatives and will refer to the category of speech act as performatives and further shows that this is a fruitful endeavor. Far from being limited to the domain of the arcane, ritual and magical speech is used everyday, as in the common sayings: "Rain rain go away.", “(May you) get well soon!”, “take care”, and "Let it snow". Forming spells remains a productive language capacity and thus we can tap our own intuitions on the matter. Taking the yes/no test described in §2.1 we can see that it would be infelicitous to challenge the description a ritual utterance appears to be saying. One cannot, for example, respond to "May you get well soon" with "that’s not true" or "yes, that’s correct". Yet, "May you get well soon" is a natural phrase to say. This suggests that it is a performative utterance. Additionally, ritual speech seems, at first glace, to have the same quantified subject DP prohibition described of performatives. An exorcist would not be able to say "most demons leave this body" in order to perform an exorcism. However, if they said it with hereby (or indeed may or shall) it would indeed be purporting to perform an exorcism (albeit an incomplete one): "Most demons shall now leave this body!" It will the address these concerns and finish the laying of the groundwork necessary to begin the formal analysis. 2.3 Felicity conditions of spells: what (else) it takes to be performative It should not be thought that the only thing that makes a performative is that it fails the yes/no test and seems to perform some action (§2.1). Other speech acts may behave similarly, such as greetings like "hello" which are neither true nor false and perform the act of greeting. There is therefore a desire to define what is performative and what is not. Though performative sentences are often defined, for example by the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, as "in the first person, present tense, indicative mood, active voice, that describes its speaker as performing a speech act" (Green 2021), Austin, who coined the term however, says that this form only "seems to deserve our favour" and that "there was certainly no one criterion of this kind [based on grammar or vocabulary]" (Austin 1962: p. 67). Eckardt provides the hypothesis which will support the argument of this thesis, that there are just two requirements for performatives: that they are self-referential and that they "comprise the speaker’s act of defining the category of the utterance"(Eckardt 2012). Both of these requirements will be discussed at length in §3. Diesing & McConnell-Ginet (2012) provides examples of performatives that do not fit the mould described by The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy, They show that the variety of grammatical forms of spells of J.K. Rowling’s Harry Potter world exceed the limits suggested above. The data introduced in this thesis suggest that first person, present.. etc. should not be understood as the archetype of performatives. With incantations included, which are a major aspect of human communication in our history, performatives can come in any number of combinations of grammatical form. Performatives should therefore be understood under Eckardt’s principles 18 of self-referentiality and definition rather than any grammatical criterion. Even if a sentence meets the requirements described above in order to be a performative utterance, it may still fail to do so for other reasons. Austin (1962) called these the felicity conditions of performatives. He says that an attempted performative which fails to meet the felicity requirements ‘fizzles out’ or ‘misfires’. A misfire might occur for example if one performs a knighting while lacking the authority required to do so, namely being a monarch. In this case despite an utterance such as I hereby knight you being both self-referential and speaker defined as performative, if the speaker lacks the prerequisite conditions then such a action as described would fail to take place. Austin, lays out four felicity conditions and two insincerity conditions for a performative sentence to be used as a performative utterance. (A. i) There must exist an accepted conventional procedure having a certain conventional effect, that procedure to include the uttering of certain words, by persons in certain circumstances, and further (A. 2) the particular persons and circumstances in a given case must be appropriate for the invocation of the particular procedure invoked. (B. 1) The procedure must be executed by all participants both correctly and (B. 2) completely. (r. 1) Where, as often, the procedure is designed for use by persons having certain thoughts or feelings, or for the inauguration of certain consequential conduct on the part of any participant, then a person participating in and so invoking the procedure must in fact have those thoughts or feelings, and the participants must intend so to conduct themselves, and further (r. 2) must actually so conduct themselves subsequently (Austin 1962) Violations of conditions A and B lead to what he calls "misfires" because the action fails to take place. Violations of r he labels "abuses" because the act takes place but it was not carried out with the intentions the act should carry with it. (Austin 1962) Take for instance the sentence (20) below. (20) The students welcome you to the college. An utterance of (20) may fail to be performative if it is said to you by a fisherman at sea (condition A. 2), or if the proper person says it, but fails to say it loud enough for anyone 19 to hear (B. 1). The performative may be performed but ‘insincerely’ if the students hold protests in their hearts (r. 1), or carry out those protests and boycott the welcome (r. 2). It is reasonable to think there may be additional requirements which a speaker must accomplish in order to successfully perform a ritual or magic performative. Diesing & McConnell-Ginet (2012) expands on Austin’s felicity requirements for a performative utterance and provide five felicity requirements for the spells of the Harry Potter world. (21) a. Only wizards and witches can perform spells. b. Incantations generally have conventional forms that must be produced correctly and completely. c. Wand movement must be executed properly and wands must be in good working order. d. Intentions must be appropriate. e. Concentration is required. (Diesing & McConnell-Ginet 2012) Of these requirements (b) and (c) seem to fall under Austin’s (A. 1) and (B) conditions, (a) falls under condition (A. 2), and (d) is covered by condition (r. 1). The fifth felicity requirement, (e), is perhaps the most unique requirement to spells as described by Diesing & McConnell-Ginet (2012). While in a typical performative the concentration of the speaker makes little difference so long as the conditions described by Austin hold, in the spells of the Harry Potter world Diesing & McConnell-Ginet (2012) argue that concentration is an addition factor for proper performance of the spell. A lapse in concentration, they argue, could lead to an unintended effect occurring during the casting of the spell, such as teleporting to the wrong location. A lapse in concentration in a performance of a marriage would not, for example, marry an unintended person. While many of these requirements of successful spells in the Harry Potter world can apply to current and historical traditions of magic discussed in the present work, which combination of these requirements applies depends on the tradition, with many cultures expressing many of these requirements as a should requirement rather than a must. It is these conditions which describe why a particular magic or ritual performative utterance may fail to perform the spoken action. The examples used throughout this thesis should be considered as if whatever the relevant felicity conditions of that culture’s practice hold at the time of speech. These conditions account for the practical ways that performatives may fail but there is another more abstract discussion to be had on whether performatives which claim to perform an eventuality not immediately or tangibly verifiable should be considered failed performatives. 20 2.4 On the truth actuality of performative utterances Despite their performative behavior in yes/no tests and the fact that many have claimed spells to be performative there is a key theoretical caveat. Performatives in their original formulation are not about sentences which seem to do or try to do, but utterances which necessarily perform an action if their felicity conditions are met (Austin 1962). Szabolcsi (1982) claims that performatives cause a change of fact in the world. In truth-conditional semantics language is directly evaluated against the conditions of the world. Performatives are those sentences which by their utterance purport to make something true in the world. For example, a sincere utterance of "I promise to find the ring" creates an event of a promising which is self evident and immediately verifiable. An event which, if performatives have truth-conditional content, would be the truth maker of the descriptive aspect of the utterance. The issue with spells is that whatever the language-to-world evaluator is, it claims to evaluate the conditions of the world objectively to determine the truth of the sentence. Where this is a problem for the study of ritual speech is that, though purporting to create eventualities through utterances, the effect of spells and blessings are not verifiable in the same way that normal performatives do so automatically. Jary (2007) writes that performatives are selfverificational, but this is not clear with spells. A believer of a spell may believe themselves to be cured, for example, by the words "I hereby heal you", but the facts of the world may not agree with that. From the objective perspective, it is still a correct performance of a blessing, but a misfire of a healing. From the subjective, internalist, perspective, if the entire speech community considers such an utterance to make the statement true, then no-one would consider the performative utterance a misfire. Put another way, a semanticist living in the speech community which believes that healing objectively takes place upon completion of a ritual would evaluate that a healing event did occur by the speaking of the utterance. This begs the question of performativity and truth, which is that the human behavior of making eventualities true by speaking utterances is defined in terms of the objective conditions of the world. Conditions which are ultimately still interpreted by humans as being true or false. Take a classic performative: I do (take this person as my lawfully wedded spouse). The event of the marriage is performed by saying those words. This is because the speech community defines marriage as an event which involves necessarily the speaking of those words. This is not set in stone however, over the years the speech community has widened and narrowed what constitutes the performance of a marriage. If a speech community defines the performance of a healing to involve a specific utterance, whether or not the subject is truly healed of their medical ailment, they have still been the subject of a healing as defined by the speech community. Do we then consider this case a ‘misfire’ as no biophysical healing occurred, in which case we attribute essentially a language ‘mistake’ to a widespread human behavior. Or do we consider that a speaker-defined healing or a community concept 21 of healing is what the performative is evaluated against; and whether or not a biophysical healing occurred be irrelevant to the performance of that performative. It does not seem that a particular utterance’s status as a performative should depend on the knowledge of the linguist doing the theoretical analysis. Many cultures have certainly believed that the utterance of a ritual did really make the proposition true. A trained truth-conditional semanticist in that culture would evaluate it as such, believing that they were using externalist criteria. It is therefore more logical to evaluate the behavior of a performative utterance by just that, its behavior; not the metaphysically fraught objective conditions of the world. As a behavior it should be evaluated based on the concepts and intentions of the speakers. If a speech community believes that saying a phrase prevents the volcano from erupting then they must believe that utterance to be performative one. This thesis does not rely on an interalist account of meaning, but will use the considerations raised in the present discussion to elaborate Eckardt’s "speaker-defining" requirement for felicitous performatives. For the purpose of this thesis there are a few options. One is to assume we are evaluating the utterances in a world where words objectively do have the power to change all aspects of the world, as ritual speakers purport to do. The second option, is to consider in the evaluation of the performative whether a proposition is now believed to be true by the hearer, and thus place a more pragmatic criterion on performativity. Another consideration and possible avenue towards the appropriate description of ritual and magical performatives is that regardless of whether the healing or a healing spell is a misfire or not there is always a true event of a blessing (or cursing). The fact that any performance of a healing is also a performance of a blessing will be compatible with the events-based account presented in §3 and §4 which use default speech/ assertion event in their formulation. In this case the ever-present assertion event is the performative utterance with blessing describing those benevolent ritual or magical performative assertions and curse describing the malevolent ones. With quantified subject DPs the performativity of the healing fails either because of the quantifier, or the words did not bio-physically heal, but the utterance remains performative because the assertion is performed. A blessing in this view is therefore the performance of an assertion for benevolent purposes. Thus, while whether "I hereby heal you" is a healing or not depends on the belief of the evaluator, any evaluator would consider it to always be a true performance of a blessing. Perhaps then, in ritual speech we are working with heal* and protect* which are different from their non-star counterparts in that they were defined in their speech community to be events not specifically tied to scientific therapeutic measures or outcomes but which could also be performed just by speaking certain words. Austin’s felicity conditions leave room for this interpretation as they only specify that a convention exist and if the convention for heal* be speaking then healing* can be performative in exactly the way Austin described. 22 This is not so far-fetched. Consider the psychological and psycho-somatic portions of a child’s injury which is instantly healed by a parent’s kiss21 or the Spanish: "sana, sana colita de rana, si no sana(s) hoy sanar(a/ás) mañana" - "heal heal frog’s tail, if it doesn’t heal today it will heal tomorrow". The placebo is an important fact to consider. It is well studied that performing an action under the assumption that it will help, even by someone aware of the effect, can have a real impact on health outcomes. In this way there is a true sense in which the words "You are hereby healed" did in fact truly heal countless people. For people in the speech community with heal*, in the event of a performative misfire, i.e. when a healing would fail, they could place the blame of the misfire not on the assumed relationship between words and the conditions of the world in that speech community, but on aspects of the performance or skill of the performer. Since the placebo did work for many, when it did not work it was often assumed that the intervention was just not performed correctly. Look to blood-letting, whose failures were explained as a failure to have done enough blood-letting. For these reasons it does not seem clear that the status of an utterance as a performative should rest on the best current scientific knowledge. This would have non-trivial implications. For example, Roman Catholics, who believe in transubstantiation, hold that when a priest, repeating Jesus’s words at the last supper, holds up the host (bread) and says "This is my body..", that that bread does actually become Christ’s body. A linguist analyzing the truth-conditions of such a performative with the objective conditions of the world would, if scientific knowledge is the best approximation of the conditions of the world, have to say that the Catholic sacrament of the Eucharist is a ‘misfire’ at a minimum, and if they take the stance that performatives have truth values, they would have to say that the utterance is false. A contentious position to be sure, but one which I think, whether technically correct or not, misses the point and fails to capture the phenomenon of performative utterances. The thread that unifies the human speech behavior is not that they truly make an objective change in the world by their utterance, but that they are believed to do so by their utterance. By this definition, at the very least, whether a particular historical utterance was performative or not, should not change as knowledge does. A counter-argument to the arguments offered above is that what I have described is not what performative analysis aims to capture. In the views of Searle (1989) and Jary (2007), performatives are only those events which are self-verificational and linguistic. i.e. they purport to create a promising event and at the same time necessarily do so as a promising is defined to be performed by saying certain words. A healing, on the other hand, even if defined to include a speech act as a healing act, presumably includes other sub-events as well which are not speech events. This view of performativity aims to narrow the scope of 21 There is also evidence that kissing provides true, non-placebo healing due to the wound-healing properties of saliva (Torres et al. 2017). 23 performatives to only events which are exclusively verbal events, i.e. events which only ever consist of one person saying or otherwise communicating to another person. A counter to this speaking-event only line is that these events are not necessarily speech events. A promising can occur with just the locking of pinky fingers and a thanking may occur with a simple nod. I will take the stance in this thesis that these actions are defined in their speech communities to be actions which can be performed by speaking certain words. Defined this way, a healing, for example, much like a blessing or a promise, may be considered to be an action which is performed by speaking certain words. The problem, therefore, of the non-performativity of "I hereby fry an egg" is not really that the words do not have the power to perform that action, but that our speech community does not consider them to have that power. Certain speech communities believe that "This is my blood" turn wine to blood and it seems that that action should be included in what we refer to as performatives. It is technically therefore possible to picture a speech community where all members believe that words can fry eggs. In that world "I hereby fry an egg" would be performative. As Searle (1989) states it is not the semantics, i.e. the relation between those words and the world, which prevent the utterance from being performative, it is the facts of the world. If the world were a fictional magical world where a magic user could perform that action through speaking those words it will not be because the semantics of "I hereby fry an egg" has changed but because the world is such that the event of frying may be made true through the utterance of a particular phrase. This is really important because the contrasting position would be that it is the semantics (the mapping from world to world) of particular verbs which allow them to be used performatively. It is not that "fry" somehow have a different meaning, a different word to world mapping, than promise. Both, in the compositional semantic view, contribute an event predicate which takes certain arguments. "I hereby fry an egg" and "I hereby thank you" do not represent different relationships between language and the world. Both represent an utterance which claims that there is an event and further claims that the speaking event is that event. It is not contained within the semantics of "I hereby fry an egg" that frying an egg is not something performed by speaking, if this were the case it would not be possible to imagine the fictional world where with those words and the wave of a wand someone could fry an egg. Any particular sentence cannot be said to be performative without the speech community which defines it to be so. Imagine a community in which thanking can only be done through the giving of a small gift. In that community speaking the utterance "I hereby thank you" does not achieve the performance of the thanking because in that community a thanking is not a linguistic event. The sentence has the same meaning as usual, the truth conditions are the same, it is still true if and only if there is a thanking event which is co-referential with the speaking event 24 and whose agent is the speaker and whose patient is the addressee. However, the ability to be interpreted as performative has changed because of the community’s belief of what a thanking event is. Therefore I conclude that, indifferent to the actual change of state in the real world, but still bound by the same felicity requirements applied to typical performatives, ritual speech can use performative utterances to purport to perform actions in the world. Dowty (1989) argued that events are fundamentally delimited by the fact that they have unique agents or group of agents (expanded by Landman (2000)). To further complicate matters these class divides often split a single vocabulary item into two apparent uses. For example, English must can be used with deontic necessity force (having to do with obligations): "You must clean your room this instant!"; or with epistemic necessity force (having to do with what knowledge you have available): "It must be raining". While it is desirable to have both uses of must be derived from the same lexical entry, previous attempts at this, Hacquard claims, fall short. Kratzer’s influential (1981, 1991) possible worlds account of modality manages to provide a single lexical entry for each modal, namely that a modal’s semantic contribution is quantification over possible worlds, either universal (must, have to) or existential (may, might). Each modal’s range of meanings (often called modal "flavors") is therefore determined in part by the conversational environments in which they reside, which Kratzer claims consists of an utterance’s modal base and ordering source. Modal bases may be epistemic or circumstantial and each returns a corresponding set of "accessible" worlds; providing, respectively, the set of worlds which are consistent with some information or knowledge, and the set of worlds which are consistent with the some set of ideals. The ordering source then ranks the set of worlds provided by the modal base outputting the most ideal world of the set. The benefit of the Hacquardian approach is that it provides a Kratzer like unification of the epistemic and deontic into single lexical entries of each modal but instead of appealing to the conversational context as the determiner of modal flavor, Hacquard (2010) uses the syntactic context to determine the meaning, using the closest event binder to determine the meaning. This approach unifies both the semantic desiderata of single lexical entry with derived uses, with the syntactic desiderata of accounting for the scopal properties of the different types of modals such as the observation by Cinque (1999) that modals scoping below aspect are interpreted as root modals while modals scoping above tense are interpreted as epistemic. It is in the very scopal phenomena that drove Hacquard’s theory that we see the striking resemblances between modals and performative utterances. Semblance does not make an argument but does inspire the present work to attempt to unify the events-based accounts of Eckardt (2012) and Hacquard (2010). Seeing later the success of this may provide impetus 39 in the exploration of the connection and or interaction between modality and performativity. A connection between modality and performativity or speech act theory generally has been offered by many. von Fintel & Gillies (2007) claim that epistemic modals contribute both quantification over possible worlds but also an additional speech act of "proffering". Portner (2009) goes beyond epistemics claiming further that deontics carry a speech act of a command and roots contribute an assertion which accounts for actuality entailments. It is therefore interesting that modals should have such similarities to performatives. The section above noted that shall gains its present meaning through performative modality but ended by noting that finding utterances with shall which are not performative on that view seems to be difficult. This thesis notes that it is not reasonable to assume all modals are performative unless all modality is to be thought to be ultimately circumstantial modality, with the other modal flavors being results of either positional or pragmatic interpretation. Further interest for investigating the interaction between performativity and modality comes from the similarities between the two. Epistemic modals in particular like performative utterances resist embedding. They do not generally scope under tense, negation, or quantifiers, nor can they be used in the formation of questions, conditional antecedents, or attitude verb complements Hacquard (2010), Eckardt (2012). A side effect of necessarily scoping over tense means that performatives, just as epistemics, are always evaluated at the speech time. He might have left is an expression of what the speaker can say given their current knowledge, not their knowledge at the moment the individual in question left. In the same sense an utterance of You hereby have been healed! cannot describe that someone was healed before the utterance, despite the fact that use of the present perfect tense allows such a time interval to be the reference. Rather a performance is understood to at the moment of utterance healed the individual. Responding to the observations above Hacquard proposes an events-based account of modality. As opposed to Kratzer’s account which positions modals as relative to a world of evaluation, Hacquard’s account relates modals, specifically modal bases, to an event of evaluation. Modal bases in her account are locally bound to an event pronoun (e) and otherwise keep their Kratzerian lexical entries (universal or existential quantification). Hacquard handles the differing modal behaviors by proposing that modals can bind either to a default speech event λ e0 or to aspect. This characterization accounts for the differing syntactic behaviors of root and epistemic modals and largely corresponds with the Cinque (1999) proposed hierarchy. These two event binders yield three events which modals can stand in relation to: the speech event e0 , attitude events e1 , and VP events e2 . The default speech event functions as an assertion in declarative sentences. (Hacquard 2010) Hacquard’s claim that there is a second, high scoping, event involved in binding nonroot modals is not uncontroversial. This theory that the speech act is represented in the 40 syntax is known as the performative hypothesis and has been proposed and supported by Ross (1970), Rizzi (1997), Ambar (1999), Ginzburg & Sag (2000), Krifka (1999), and Tenny & Speas (2003). It holds that every sentence contains an implicit "I declare../I assert.." type structure. Although she recognizes that the proposal is controversial (particularly among syntacticians) Hacquard (2010) says the performative hypothesis is regaining popularity among semanticists. Aside from the generative pitfall syntacticians note, claiming that the default speech event is associated with the force of the assertion would correctly predict that epistemic modals alter the force of the assertion (I assert, based on what I know, that..) and at the same time the fact that modals modify the force of the assertion would support the links proposed between epistemic modals and evidential marking by Westmoreland (1998) and Drubig (2001). Hacquard adopts a framework which she derives from the performative hypothesis in which there is a speech event in the topmost syntactic position whose role is to determine the type of utterance, be it assertion, question, or command (Hacquard 2010). The advantage of Hacquard’s theory is that through the different syntactic positions of the binding events, the syntactic behaviors of the flavors of modality can be modeled without resorting to positing homophony. Hacquard accounts for the lack of epistemic modals in embedded contexts by reformulating the epistemic modal base as providing the set of worlds which are compatible with the ‘content’ of the event which binds it, rather than being connected to the speaker’s knowledge. She stipulates that because non-attitude VP events are content-less they cannot therefore be an argument of an epistemic base. It is in fact precisely this interpretation which we see in the modality in performatives in magical and ritual speech. 4.3 Integrating the two accounts Both Eckardt’s and Hacquard’s theories therefore incorporate an idea of a speech event which is additional to the root event of the explicit verb of the sentence. This, uniquely to other theories of modality, provides a point at which performative utterances and modals can interface. While Hacquard proposes that the default speech event scopes over the root event, Eckardt does not make this explicit for her "ongoing transfer of information" but assumes that it exists in the context set as a salient event which can, either through hereby or pragmatic inference, be identified with the event parameter of the root verb. From this discrepancy there arise two possibilities for this current effort of combining the theories. The first possibility identifies Hacquard’s default speech event with Eckardt’s ongoing transfer of information, placing the default speech event into the pragmatic domain where it is ready to be identified with the root event if necessary, as in a performative utterance. This approach lacks the binding facts of Hacquard’s which account for the syntactic differences between epistemic and root modals. It is further unclear how this account would help explain how modality helps sentences with quantified subject DPs become performative. To make this account work a mechanism would have to be devised in which the modal introduces quantification over events during the pragmatic identification of the speaking event with the root event (the g(e/ε 0 ) assignment function step from the derivations above). No such mechanism exists, nor would introducing another layer of quantification solve the agent uniqueness problem which was caused by the quantification in the subject DP. Rather this thesis takes the other direction, identifying in performatives the ongoing transfer of information of Eckardt’s account with Hacquard’s default speech event; a high scoping event variable (above TP) present in the compositional semantics of the utterance, rather than in the pragmatics. What this thesis will show through the examples below is that by shifting the identification of the ongoing transfer of information from the root verb to the default speech event, the agent uniqueness problems presented in performative utterances with quantified subject DPs may be circumvented. In this account the presence of a high scoping default 42 speech event (e0 ) in utterances with modals allows an alternative performative association to occur. Instead of the utterance becoming performative by associating the ongoing transfer of information with the root verb, the performative association is between the default speech event (i.e. assert) and the ongoing transfer of information. Since the agent of the speech event is never quantified (it is always the speaker (I assert..)), the quantification which may exist over the agent of the root verb cannot effect the association. In this view the default speech event, which is modified by the modal, is in turn identified as the performative event of the utterance (I hereby assert...). The only downside of this approach is that it loses the attractive feature of Eckardt’s view in which, in a performative utterance, the speaking event is itself the root event (i.e. this ongoing transfer of information is a promising). This, however, is actually desirable for magical utterances and ritual speech because we would potentially like to say, if we don’t find the heal* theory convincing, that in "May you be protected" the speaking is not itself a protecting. It is rather a blessing which in turn grants protection. It is still possible to save the performative association to the main verb if we posit, like in the application of the assignment function (e/ε 0 ), that the events can still be pragmatically identified with each other with the assignment function. In this way the speaker can make the inference, just as under Eckardt’s view, that the present ongoing transfer of information, as an assertion that there is a promising/protecting, may at the same time be such a promising/protecting. In such a way, if permitted under the constraint of agent uniqueness, the ongoing transfer of information may be performatively identified both as an assertion and as a promising. This happens to capture both the view of Austin (1962) that performative utterances do not assert their content and the view of Szabolcsi (1982) and Jary (2007), that they do. Alternatively, extending the logic of shall above, this thesis argues that the performative use of may creates an obligation to the truth of the proposition through the circumstantial modality. Namely the performance of the circumstantial possibility modal says that the world is now potentially compatible with the circumstances of this utterance, with that circumstance being the proposition that you are protected, if the world is now potentially compatible with circumstances of the utterance. In this way may does not necessarily create the eventuality but does change the world such that the eventuality is now possible. This effect does not seem strong enough at first glance, but the world changing effect they claim can still be quite strong, only preserving some agency for the ritual subject. For example, in the meaning of a ritual utterance with may the world is now changed such that the wind is able to be at your back, however if one stays inside then such an effect does not take place. Similarly a healer may through an utterance change the world such that you are now able to walk, if you do not exercise that new ability then the event does not happen. This is why may is still used despite the stronger shall existing, the weaker may still enacts 43 a change in the world but. As an illustration consider a healing ritual that someone such as Jesus could have performed for someone previously unable to walk. Should Jesus say "From this moment forth, you may walk!" That seems like quite a gracious blessing. On the other hand if Jesus said "You shall walk from this moment forth!", taken literally, the subject is now obligated to walk continuously from that moment, or with some pragmatic leniency, obligated to walk wherever there is a choice of motion. Despite healing the subject’s condition, the strength of shall seems to be able to present unintended obligations due to it strength. It is perhaps for this reason that may should be used frequently in ritual and magic speech despite contributing possibility modality rather than necessity modality. 4.4 An anthropological discussion There is perhaps an interesting reason why a speaker should want to employ circumstantial modality in their spells and blessings. The deontic interpretation of this modal stems not from a deontic modal base, in which the accessible worlds are those in which all rules are followed. Instead deontic is a second order or phantasmal relation which arises from the performance of circumstantial modality. This has the benefit of more narrowly selecting the relevant worlds, only those which are compatible with circumstances of the utterance need be accessible, rather than the worlds in which all obligations are upheld. This approach reduces need for an ordering source, which Kratzer (1981), posits to account for the apparently conflicting obligations in deontic modality under her analysis. If the relevant worlds are those in which all obligations are upheld, then there must be some way to order the worlds so that the more relevant obligations are prioritized over other obligations which may hold. Her evidence for this is the fact that if, in a world where all obligations are upheld, a law which says a murderer shall serve twenty years prison applies vacuously for the law which forbids murder is also upheld and there are no murderers to speak of. Kratzer’s ordering source deals with the conflicting obligations by ordering them. But if, instead of talking about worlds in every obligation is upheld, the modality of a particular law is connected to those worlds which are compatible 59 with the circumstances of that utterance then the existence of other laws does not conflict with the apparent obligation to follow that one. This is because the obligation is a secondary effect of the performance of the circumstantial modal. The performative makes it now true that in order for this world to be compatible with the circumstance of the utterance, the eventuality described must hold. Persons subject to the law understand that, if they believe the speaker has the authority to make them agents of the law through the performative, then, if they are to act in accordance with the law spoken, then they must work to make the world one in which is compatible with the circumstance of the law. With that circumstance being that the proposition is made true through the utterance of the law, they must work to uphold the truth of the proposition. In this way obligations are held virtually and to every individual law rather than all laws at once, so that the law which forbids murder applies first but when an individual fails to uphold the state which the law has performatively made them an agent of, the next law applies, for they are now the agent of the law that a murderer shall get twenty years imprisonment. The application of the law involves taking such individuals who fail to uphold primary states and forcibly making them individuals the agents/experiencers of the secondary states namely, the experiencers of prison or the payers of fines. Even a prison sentence, in just places, is performative (You are hereby sentenced to twenty years in prison), the caveat being that the guards help the prisoner fulfill their agent-hood if they are reluctant to do so. Therefore obligation in modality is not derived from ordering worlds in which all obligations hold, but from each individual’s understanding that, lest they face becoming the agents of further, more restricting, utterances, they must act to make the world such it is compatible with the utterances of the laws by acting as the agents of the utterances of which laws apply to them. The conclusion which this thesis has reached on performative utterances with shall may be of interest in the study of metaethics. This thesis has derived the effect of a duty or obligation without reference to right or wrong, or an ideal world. Rather this thesis shows that laws are states which are asserted as truly holding at the point of ratification. Obligations are derived from each individual’s understanding that they are agents of a certain set of laws and if they fail to be the agent of those laws they may be forced to be the agents of more restrictive laws. Interestingly breaking the law on this view amounts to an exercise of agency. As a corollary each law abiding individual sacrifices some degree of agency. Of course the reason this sacrifice is made is not because of a sense of duty but because individuals understand that they will be forced to sacrifice more agency if they fail to maintain the state which the authority has made them an agent of. Laws are only one arm of duties and obligations. This approach can also apply to 60 uncodified norms and obligations. For example, "Everyone should vote" may be seen as a duty which is not an instance of a state performatively making individuals the agents of an eventuality. This, however, is not so different. Someone said the utterance above and in doing so, expressed that if the world is compatible with their utterance then each person in the world votes. An individual who respects the authority of the speaker positions themselves as an agent of that eventuality and acts in correspondingly. Should a political pundit assert "British citizens must accept austerity measures" those individuals who do not act as agents of that utterance, do so, not because they are not part of the group which the speaker performatively asserts are the agents of the action, but because they do not cooperate with their inclusion as agents of that eventuality. The question this leaves open is why do individuals make such statements or think of such propositions which place themselves and others as agents of eventualities. Surely this points to a non-performative origin of ethics duty or obligation. It could be reasoned that each moral statement is made to further the goal of another which the speaker is an agent of. So that someone who see themselves as an agent of the utterance "Everyone should vote" may also see themselves as the agent of the utterances "people should spread awareness about voter registration". In this way each obligation which is created or concluded can be thought of as being created or concluded as a result of acting as the agent of another prior utterance. This reflects the reality of many norms, which may exist simply because they are spoken. For example, a superstition such as avoiding stepping on cracks in pavement is followed not because of an larger ethical principle, or because of tangible consequences, but simply because people (particularly children) regularly state that it is bad to do so. Deriving obligation from the way performatives purport to change the facts of the world, and the way individuals accommodate or reject their roles as agents of those new states, is advantageous to those who are skeptical about the concept of duties. Duties this way are illusory, they are in reality a linguistic behavior-asserting that others or oneself are now the agents of a particular eventuality and a resulting behavior of the supposed agents, either in accordance with the state asserted or against it. Hume (1739) observed a gap in reasoning often presented in ethics. He observed that moral arguments often seamlessly flow from statements of what is to statements of what ought to be, when upon close inspection there is no logical connection between what is and what ought to be. For Hume if an individual only has access to factual premises, they cannot infer the truth of moral statements. More broadly, this can be construed as part of the fact-value distinction which claims that it is not possible to infer evaluative statements from non-evaluative statements. (Cohon 2018) This observation can be accounted for in the above derived account of deontic statements. The truth of a deontic statement is not inferred, it is asserted, and it is up to the hearer to decide if they shall act to uphold the truth 61 of the sate they may have performatively been made the subject of. Insights which followed from Hume’s observation led to what Anscombe (1958) describes as a considerable service of showing that the notion of "morally ought" has no content. She laments that the same philosophers have attempted to supply more dubious content to "ought" in an attempt to retain the "psychological force of the term" It is her claim that the notion of "morally ought" ought to be dropped in favor of more descriptive categorization such as "untruthful" "unjust" or "unchaste". She notes that it is hardly possible of course to speak at all without regular use of ought, must, and should, something which might encourage someone to think that the terms do have content and quite important content at that. However she argues that the terms used represent "brute relatives". In Anscombe’s example, he supplied me with potatoes has the relative "brute facts" of he had potatoes carted to my house and they were left there. In the same way she finds statements of obligation, though themselves contentless, to describe the overall character of a set of processes. This thesis provides a mechanism that underpins this "brute relative" of statements of obligation. It is not as robust moral realism has it that "Moral propositions are true when actions and other objects of moral assessment have the relevant moral properties (so that the relevant moral facts obtain)" Sturgeon (1984). It is rather that moral propositions are performatively asserted to be true and they purport to make a change of the facts of the world. This is not exactly what cognitivists would call "truth-apt" because it is not that the proposition is true only that it is asserted to now be such.
