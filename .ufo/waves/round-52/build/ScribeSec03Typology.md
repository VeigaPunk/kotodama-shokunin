## The boundary rule the section states: what makes a code NOT a surrogate

The section gives the designer two questions and one caution.

1. **Iconic link.** Does the sign track the sound of the language — tone, syllable, vowel
   quality? Whistled and drummed speech do: the whistle reproduces lexical tone on the
   pitch and segments in the amplitude envelope in tonal languages
   (`NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-3`), and the drum reproduces syllable timing and tone
   (`NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-1`). Lexical-ideogram systems do not: their motifs stand for
   concepts with no phonological relation to the speech
   (`NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-2`).
2. **Openness.** Can new sentences be composed, or is the repertoire a closed list of named
   events? French hunting-horn signals were named hunt events — for the dog, retreat,
   capture, death — kept as an orally transmitted teaching repertoire
   (`NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-7`); the Kwoma and Malekula slit-log codes work the same way
   (`NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-2`).
3. **Caution: arbitrariness is not the test.** Enphrased phonemic messages also become
   conventional and arbitrary in part, so "arbitrary = code" is a false test
   (`NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-2`). The section says so explicitly, and also says that where
   the line falls is a declared convention, not a consensus: the literature splits (Nketia
   1971 excludes signal drumming; Niles 2010 includes it, as reported in
   `NEW-ScribeSec03Typology-2` §2.2).

Bugle calls and bells are named as members of the code class, but the specific instances
are marked `(to-verify)` — no source for a bugle or bell code was opened, and the hunting
horn is the sourced instance of the class. The typical failure the design consequence
column names is a designer who gives a culture a closed call list and then lets characters
say sentences on it.
