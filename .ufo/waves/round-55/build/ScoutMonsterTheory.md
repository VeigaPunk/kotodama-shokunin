### NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-1

citation: Cohen, Jeffrey Jerome. 1996. "Monster Culture (Seven Theses)." In *Monster Theory: Reading Culture*, ed. Jeffrey Jerome Cohen, 3–25. Minneapolis: University of Minnesota Press. No DOI verified for this chapter; stable id: the 1996 Minnesota volume, chapter pp. 3–25 (the copy's own head note carries exactly this citation line).

url: https://web.archive.org/web/20260914204030id_/https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/20578/files/2022/05/FYW-Sample-Reading-B.pdf

route: The same file requested directly from CUNY Academic Commons (`https://files.commons.gc.cuny.edu/wp-content/blogs.dir/20578/files/2022/05/FYW-Sample-Reading-B.pdf`) answered HTTP 403 with an HTML block page: challenge met, not passed. Read instead from Wayback capture `20260914204030` (HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 2,941,677 bytes, scanned facing-page spreads of printed pp. 3–25), accessed 2026-10-22. The PDF has no text layer; quotes were OCRed and then verified against the 300 dpi page images. Quotation marks inside quotes are the printed typographic marks.

kind: book (chapter)

tier: A — the author's own chapter text, primary for the seven theses it states. Not a survey and not a history: it argues about Western, mainly medieval and early modern, material, and is not evidence for any other culture's monsters.

scope: the seven theses as a method of reading monsters from the outside; specifically the *monstrum* gloss (reveal/warn), the mixed-category argument, the prohibition-and-border argument, and the claim that inherited maps and travel accounts populated the far edges of the world with creatures. It carries no bestiary, no roster of beings, no dates, and no non-European material.

quotes:
- Thesis I, second paragraph (p. 4): "the monstrum is etymologically “that which reveals,” “that which warns,” a glyph that seeks a hierophant."
- Thesis III (p. 7): "A mixed category, the monster resists any classification built on hierarchy or a merely binary opposition,"
- Thesis V, first paragraph (p. 12): "To step outside this official geography is to risk attack by some monstrous border patrol or (worse) to become monstrous oneself."
- Thesis VI (p. 18): "Maps and travel accounts inherited from antiquity invented whole geographies of the mind and peopled them with exotic and fantastic creatures;"

teaches: Read a culture's monsters as readable signs: the word warns, mixed bodies defeat classification, and the border punishes those who cross it.

draft sentences:
- Take the culture's own word for the being as a statement of function, because the Latin *monstrum* is glossed as that which reveals and that which warns before any question of shape is asked (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-1, Thesis I, second paragraph, p. 4).
- Decide which classification the body defeats, since a mixed category resists any classification built on hierarchy or on a merely binary opposition (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-1, Thesis III, p. 7).
- Place the being on the map the culture actually used, because inherited maps and travel accounts peopled whole geographies of the mind with exotic creatures, and the entry should name that map and its edge (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-1, Thesis VI, p. 18).
- Choose whether the being warns, punishes or converts the traveller, since stepping outside the official geography risks attack by a monstrous border patrol or becoming monstrous oneself (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-1, Thesis V, first paragraph, p. 12).
- Keep each period's version of the being separate, because Cohen's second thesis is that the monster always escapes and returns in another form (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-1, Thesis II heading).

### NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-2

citation: Erle, Sibylle; Hendry, Helen. 2020. "Monsters: interdisciplinary explorations in monstrosity." *Palgrave Communications* (the journal now runs as *Humanities and Social Sciences Communications*) 6: 53. doi 10.1057/s41599-020-0428-1. Open access, CC BY 4.0.

url: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41599-020-0428-1

route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, `text/html`, full article text served (372,261 bytes, abstract through references); no paywall, no challenge, no JavaScript needed. Accessed 2026-10-22.

kind: paper (the article's own type is Comment / Brief Communication: a field overview, not a systematic review)

tier: B — peer-reviewed open-access comment. It summarises the field, including Cohen, and its own worked case is *Frankenstein* reception and teaching; authoritative for how monster studies describes its own scope, not for any culture's monsters and not primary for the authors it cites.

scope: what monster studies takes itself to cover and what it inherits from Cohen; the field's treatment of Greek myth, Ovid's *Metamorphoses* and fairy tales as the familiar homes of monsters; the fight-and-relief pattern; the monster as measure of the human; and the argument that monsters defeat classification. It is not a survey of any tradition's beings.

quotes:
- Introduction, paragraph beginning "For our purposes": "Monsters are also familiar figures from our consumption of Greek myths, Ovid’s Metamorphoses and, of course, Fairy Tales."
- Introduction, paragraph beginning "For our purposes": "Monsters in all these stories must be fought and victory tends to be conquest followed by relief or emotional stillness."
- Introduction, paragraph beginning "For our purposes": "the figure of the monster has been used to measure the status of ‘the human’ both in terms of appearance and ethical choice or agency."
- § The afterlife and legacy of Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*, paragraph beginning "Botting, like Foucault before him": "monsters—by their very nature—confront us with excess, a circumstance, which strictly speaking should make classification impossible."

teaches: Survey the field's working premises: monsters are signs of societal problems, measure the human, and arrive through myth, metamorphosis and fairy tale.

draft sentences:
- Name the genre of the record, because the field meets monsters through Greek myth, Ovid's *Metamorphoses* and fairy tales rather than through one kind of book (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-2, Introduction, paragraph beginning "For our purposes").
- Decide who fights the being and what the victory settles, since in these stories monsters must be fought and victory tends to be conquest followed by relief (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-2, Introduction, paragraph beginning "For our purposes").
- Say what boundary the being measures, because the monster has been used to measure the status of the human in appearance and in ethical choice or agency (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-2, Introduction, paragraph beginning "For our purposes").
- Expect the bestiary to fail as a list: monsters confront classification with excess, so record where the entry's own categories break (NEW-ScoutMonsterTheory-2, § The afterlife and legacy of Mary Shelley's *Frankenstein*, paragraph beginning "Botting, like Foucault before him").

## Gaps and attacks

- The seven theses are theses: one author's 1996 argument, not findings about any culture. A draft sentence may report what Cohen argues; it may not report what a culture believed.
- Cohen quotes Ovid, Homer, Gerald of Wales and Mandeville inside the chapter. None of those was opened at this seat, so the entry must not quote them through Cohen or treat his summary of them as a source row.
- Erle and Hendry quote Foucault, Mittman, Weinstock and McCormack inside their survey. Those are quotations inside a quotation and cannot become rows without opening the originals.
- The chapter's bestiary-adjacent material (wonder catalogues, marvels) is only touched through "maps and travel accounts"; a real bestiary or wonder-book row belongs to another seat. If no seat opened one, the entry's bestiary section will lean on this map claim alone, which is thin.
- Not checked at this seat: whether the corpus already uses Cohen anywhere under another spelling or through a secondary citation. `grep -i cohen corpus/SOURCES.md` finds no row for *Monster Theory* (S244's row is a different Cohen, an editor of a puppetry volume), so the row is new rather than a duplicate.
