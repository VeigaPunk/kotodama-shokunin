### NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1

citation: Foster, Michael Dylan. 1998. "The Metamorphosis of the Kappa: Transformation of Folklore to Folklorism in Japan." *Asian Folklore Studies* 57(1): 1–24. DOI 10.2307/1178994 (confirmed against Crossref: author Foster, container *Asian Folklore Studies*, 57/1, first page 1, 1998). Open archive of the journal's successor title *Asian Ethnology*, Nanzan University.

url: https://asianethnology.org/article/149194.pdf

route: direct plain request (curl), HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 461330 bytes, access date 2026-10-22; landing page `https://asianethnology.org/article/149194-the-metamorphosis-of-the-kappa-transformation-of-folklore-to-folklorism-in-japan` also HTTP 200. The former host `nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp` returned HTTP 503 with a system-maintenance notice, so the publisher's current archive was read instead.

kind: paper

tier: A — peer-reviewed article that is itself the study of this exact transformation; the quotes are its own analysis and body text, not a publisher description or abstract summary. (The abstract restates the same claims; the row's quotes are taken from the article's body and abstract only where the sentence is the article's own.)

scope: what this article documents about the kappa's name stock, body, habitat, the recorders of its image, and the twentieth-century shift from a lethal water creature to a commercial mascot. Says nothing about yokai as a legal or scholarly category, nothing about bestiaries as a genre, and nothing about any tradition outside Japan; the article's own frame is one creature's history, not a comparative typology.

quotes:
- abstract, p. 1: "The kappa is a mischievous water goblin of Japanese folklore."
- p. 3, opening of the section on the kappa in folklore: "The wide distribution of the kappa is reflected in its many regional types as well as in the numerous dialectal variations of its name."
- p. 4, on the body: "Almost always the kappa has a carapace on its back, and its face is sharp with a beak-like mouth."
- p. 19, closing page: "has never been static; rather, it responds to the dynamic concerns of the people who are continuously reinventing it"

teaches: A water being can be built from borrowed animal parts, named in more than eighty local dialects, and re-made as a mascot without losing its name.

draft sentences:
- Give the being a cover-name and a crowd of local ones: Foster counts more than eighty regional variants of the kappa's name, and reports that some describe the body while others describe a habit, such as the horse-puller (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1, pp. 3–4)
- Compose the body from several animals and refuse a single ancestor: the kappa has a carapace, a beak-like face and a child's height, and Foster says its image is not exclusively derived from any one existing animal (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1, p. 4)
- Put the creature's life in one named, losable part: the dish of water on the kappa's head is its life force, and if it spills the kappa loses its power and in some accounts dies (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1, p. 4)
- Place it on the settlement's water and give it a second address: Foster puts the kappa in slow-moving freshwater rivers and pools, and records it going between mountain and river twice a year as mountain deity and as water deity (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1, pp. 3, 9)
- Decide which record your culture trusts: Foster says the image most commonly associated with the creature comes from material gathered by folklorists and amateur collectors in the early twentieth century, while an illustrated encyclopedia of the early 1700s already described its arms (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1, pp. 3, 6)
- Expect the image to follow its sponsors: Foster traces three twentieth-century kappa booms — Akutagawa's 1927 novel, postwar cartoon characters, and 1970s–80s village tourism — to a mascot that now advertises clean rivers, an inversion of the creature that once endangered children at the water (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-1, pp. 13–18)

### NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2

citation: Reider, Noriko T. 2003. "Transformation of the Oni: From the Frightening and Diabolical to the Cute and Sexy." *Asian Folklore Studies* 62(1): 133–157. Open archive of the journal's successor title *Asian Ethnology*, Nanzan University; the publisher's article page prints no DOI, and none was verified, so the stable id used here is the archive's article number 149377.

url: https://asianethnology.org/article/149377.pdf

route: direct plain request (curl), HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 496471 bytes, access date 2026-10-22; the PDF's 25 pages carry the printed page numbers 133–157. Landing page `https://asianethnology.org/article/149377-transformation-of-the-oni-from-the-frightening-and-diabolical-to-the-cute-and-sexy` also HTTP 200.

kind: paper

tier: A — peer-reviewed article and the study itself for the oni's iconographic and semantic transformation; quotes are its body text, plus one passage where it quotes a named scholar, which is marked as such below.

scope: what this article documents about the Japanese oni — the semantic range of the word and its Chinese character, the medieval iconographic set (horns, third eye, skin colours, iron mace, tiger skin), the extension of the label to living people outside imperial control, cannibalism in the tales, the Otsu-e print as a moralising souvenir, and the early modern and modern domestication of the figure. Says nothing about yokai as a whole, nothing about non-Japanese monsters, and gives no bestiary or catalogue genre.

quotes:
- p. 135, on the character: "which in Chinese means invisible soul/spirit, both ancestral and evil, of the dead"
- p. 135, on the medieval image: "Oni were customarily portrayed with one or more horns protruding from their scalps."
- p. 145, quoting McArthur 1999, 30 on the Otsu-e prints: "Some of the inscriptions to the paintings warn against the superficial appearance of goodness"
- p. 145, opening the section on modern oni: "The modern era has witnessed the birth of a new type of oni—the utterly kind, benevolent oni."

teaches: The word for a Japanese ogre covers ancestral spirits and people beyond imperial control, and the same figure is man-eater, souvenir print and benevolent children's-story character.

draft sentences:
- Let the word keep its older breadth: the character used for oni means an invisible soul or spirit of the dead, ancestral as well as evil, and the first Japanese dictionary glosses an oni as something hiding behind things and not wishing to appear (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2, p. 135)
- Build the body as a readable diagram: horns, sometimes a third eye and a fixed set of skin colours, and a tiger-skin loincloth that Reider's note derives from the ominous ox-tiger direction called the oni's gate (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2, p. 135, and note 2, p. 153)
- Let the label reach living people: Reider reports that people with different customs, or living beyond the reach of the emperor's control, were considered some form of oni (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2, p. 135)
- Pick what the being does to bodies and let ritual answer it: oni eat a person in one gulp in the tales Reider cites, and by the early modern period they are the ones driven out with thrown beans on the night of setsubun (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2, pp. 135, 143, 153)
- Put the being on cheap travelling objects and let the caption moralise: the Otsu-e print of a praying oni in a priest's garb was a souvenir sold to travellers on the Tōkaidō, and its inscriptions warn against the appearance of goodness (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2, p. 145)
- Plan for a reversal: Reider dates an utterly kind oni to Hamada Hirosuke's 1933 children's story, and finds the same figure by 1978 as a cute alien ogress in a weekly manga and its television anime (NEW-ScoutYokaiScholarship-2, pp. 145, 149–151)

Not covered by these rows, and not to be inferred from them:

- **Bestiaries and wonder catalogues as a genre.** Neither article treats a bestiary, a manuscript catalogue, or a wonder book. Foster mentions the *Wakan sansai zue* only as an illustrated encyclopedia describing the kappa's arms. This entry's bestiary material must come from another seat's rows.
- **Restricted or sacred beings.** Neither row speaks to restricted knowledge, custodianship, or what a community allows to be published. The entry's restricted-being section cannot cite these rows.
- **Any tradition outside Japan.** Neither row supports a comparative claim about monsters elsewhere.
- **Yokai as a scholarly category in general.** Reider opens from "popular thought" and treats one figure; Foster treats one figure. Neither defines yōkai as a class, so the entry should not attribute a definition of yokai to either row.
