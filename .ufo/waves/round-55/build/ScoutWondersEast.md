teaches: The Library's own account supplies the catalogue's working rules — four facts per being, bodies built from listed deformities, and every race placed beyond the known world.
draft sentences:
- Decide whether the catalogue names its beings: the Library's account finds many creatures in the *Marvels* unnamed, with *panotii* and *blemmyae* labels supplied later and only the donestre named in the text itself (NEW-ScoutWondersEast-2, paragraph beginning "The Marvels provides us with a number of these creatures").

- Make the encounter a social test as well as a bodily one: the donestre speaks every human language, beguiles strangers, eats them below the neck, then sits and weeps over the head (NEW-ScoutWondersEast-2, paragraph beginning "This race of people is called the donestre").
- Treat the book as a miscellany item with a period series: three illustrated copies survive, of c. 1000, the first half of the eleventh century and c. 1120–1140, all bound in miscellanies (NEW-ScoutWondersEast-2, paragraph beginning "Copies of the Marvels were apparently produced").

## Findings for the entry's method (beyond the three rows)

- The category the Library itself uses is "monstrous races of human beings", not monsters
  as a separate order of being; the account's only universal trait is distance. This is the
  cleanest support for the entry's framing sentence that a culture's monster list is a
  geography of the outside, not a biology ([NEW-ScoutWondersEast-2], paragraph beginning
  "Belief in the existence of monstrous races").
- The Library's own description supplies a ready-made typology axis for bodies (size,
  extremities, multiplication, hybridity) and a separate axis for behaviour (language,
  diet, dress, social structure), which are independent and can be tabulated separately
  ([NEW-ScoutWondersEast-2], paragraphs "Belief in the existence" and "Physical deformity").
- Naming is layered: text-level names (donestre) versus later scholarly or Latin-tradition
  labels (panotii, blemmyae). Any entry table needs a column for "named in the source /
  named later", or the worldbuilder will import a modern label as if it were period usage.
- The three copies give a period-change series at c. 1000, first half of the eleventh
  century, and c. 1120–1140, all illustrated, all in miscellanies — good evidence that the
  illustrated wonder catalogue is a recurring book type rather than a single artefact.
