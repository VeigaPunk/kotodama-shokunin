## What the sources answer, by design question

- What the culture calls the being, and what the word means: every being is named inside a fixed entry formula
  ("其名曰 X" / "名曰 X"); names can be toponyms, so a being is called after the mountain that produces it, and the
  book's own front matter claims the book's purpose is to settle exactly such an identification.
- How its body is composed: by likeness to a familiar animal plus the parts that break the likeness — a sheep with
  nine tails, four ears and eyes on its back; a fox with nine tails; a fish with a human face; a turtle with a bird
  head and snake tail; a one-body three-head being; a human with boar bristles.
- Where it lives relative to the known world: inside a numbered mountain route, placed by direction and distance from
  the mountain before it; overseas, by a chain of "to its east" steps running from the southwest corner to the
  southeast corner. Location is relational, not coordinate.
- Whether it warns, guards, punishes, teaches or devours: all of these are in the same book and often in the same
  entry — the nine-tailed fox eats people and its flesh wards off harm; the appearance of some beings means flood or
  forced labour in the county or commandery; each route closes with its tutelary spirit's shape and the sacrifice owed.
- Who records or depicts it, and in what kind of book or object: the court memorial that opens the book, and the
  Zuozhuan nine-tripod passage in which cast bronze lets people distinguish the local spirits and walk safely.
- What changes across periods: the mountain chapters keep the route and the distances, the sea-and-overseas chapters
  drop the distances and turn to far countries; the later Korean atlases reuse the same zones as world maps.

draft sentences:
- The first southern route names its beings one by one as the route advances, each beast fixed to one mountain with a stated distance from the last, so a worldbuilder who wants a monster census must lay out the road first. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-1, juan 1, 招搖之山, image n15)

- Danger and use share one entry, because the nine-tailed fox of 青丘 eats people while its flesh protects the eater from 蠱 harm, so decide per being whether it kills, heals, or both. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-1, juan 1, 青丘之山, image n18)
- Each route closes with a count of mountains and li and with the shape of the spirit that governs the whole stretch — bird body, dragon head — followed by the sacrifice owed, so guardians belong to a road, not to one peak. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-1, juan 1, route summary after 箕尾之山, image n18)

### NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-4

citation: Dorofeeva-Lichtmann, Vera V., 1995, "Conception of Terrestrial Organization in the Shan hai jing", Bulletin de l'École française d'Extrême-Orient 82: 57–110, doi 10.3406/befeo.1995.2297
url: https://uva.theopenscholar.com/files/geometries-of-polity/files/1995dorofeeva-conceptions_0336-1519_1995_num_82_1_2297.1995.pdf (open PDF of the journal article; also on Persée, http://www.persee.fr/doc/befeo_0336-1519_1995_num_82_1_2297)
route: direct, HTTP 200 application/pdf (6.35 MB, 54 article pages); quotes verified against the extracted text and located by the printed page numbers in the running heads. No challenge.
kind: paper (peer-reviewed journal article, open access copy)
tier: A — peer-reviewed specialist study of exactly this text's organisation of terrestrial space, read in full text.
scope: how the mountain and sea chapters organise beings and places — route lists, relational location, the beings' location/appearance/function triad, the merging of creatures with divinities, the spiritual-landscape reading, and the nine-tripod object tradition.
quotes:
- p. 59: "It includes 26 lists of mountains. Each list is perceived as forming a route."
- p. 59: "The text specifies the location, appearance and function of these beings and things"
- p. 86: "These sections constitute a spiritual landscape, or a geography of divine influences."
- p. 87: "the main purpose for the casting of the tripods was that they enabled people to distinguish the local spirits"
teaches: Beings are filed by route, described by a fixed triad, and read as marks of the divine powers holding each stretch of land.
draft sentences:
- The mountain section is 26 lists of mountains, each list read as a route in which a mountain is placed by direction and distance from the one before, so a being's address is relational. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-4, p. 59)
- The text gives each being's location, appearance and function and then the sacrifice owed to the powers there, and its creatures and magical objects are not clearly separated from divinities, so a worldbuilder should decide whether monsters and gods share one list. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-4, p. 59)
- Because each section submits to one group of deities and the sections together form a geography of divine influences, a being's place on the map is also a statement about which power holds that ground. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-4, p. 86)
- The Zuozhuan passage on the nine tripods says the cast vessels let people distinguish the local spirits and walk without danger of confusing them, so a catalogue of beings can live on an object as well as in a book. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-4, p. 87)

### NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-5

citation: Dorofeeva-Lichtmann, Vera V., 2020 (dated 1/25/2020), "Spatial Models in the Yugong and Shanhaijing: A Review", author's provisional draft for a workshop talk, hosted by the Geometries of Polity project (University of Virginia)
url: https://uva.theopenscholar.com/files/geometries-of-polity/files/4dorofeeva_spatial-models_yg-shj_5.pdf
route: direct, HTTP 200 application/pdf (1.14 MB, 7 pages). No challenge.
kind: paper (author's provisional conference draft, not peer reviewed)
tier: B — same specialist author, but the file is explicitly a work-in-progress draft ("long abstract", "provisional draft of her talk"), so it carries orientation and counts, not settled claims.
scope: the route/guardian definition, the count of 26 itineraries and 446 mountains, the land-and-sea zone model, the purpose of the model, and later reception of the zones in Korean atlases.
quotes:
- §1.1: "An itinerary includes mountains governed by the same group of local spirits."
- §1.1: "The central area of the world is mapped by means of a complex cardinally-oriented system of twenty-six land itineraries marked by 446 mountains."
- §2: "The goal of the complex spatial model underlying the Shanhaijing is to map the deployment of sacred powers over terrestrial space"
teaches: Route boundaries and cult boundaries coincide, and the same zone scheme reappears later as a picture in atlases.
draft sentences:
- An itinerary is defined as the mountains governed by the same group of local spirits, so a route boundary in this book is a cult boundary, not a landform boundary. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-5, §1.1)
- The central area of the world is mapped by twenty-six land itineraries marked by 446 mountains, and the whole scheme alternates concentric land and sea zones, so a worldbuilder copying it must copy the zoning as well as the list. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-5, §1.1)
- The stated goal of that model is to map the deployment of sacred powers over terrestrial space, which makes a being's position a claim about jurisdiction rather than habitat. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-5, §2)
- The same zones are used as key world maps in popular Korean atlases of the 18th and 19th centuries, so the catalogue's geography outlived the text as an image. (NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-5, §1.1, MAP 1)

## Remaining unknowns

- The 1995 article's own tables of the mountain lists and their counts were not transcribed; the numbers quoted here (26 routes, 446 mountains) come from NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-5, which is a draft. A settled count needs the article's tables or another peer-reviewed statement.
- No source opened this session documents illustrated Ming or Qing Shanhaijing editions, or the Shanhaijing's use in a specific museum object; the only object evidence is the nine-tripod passage as reported by NEW-ScoutShanhaijing-4, p. 87.
- The 海外南經 transcription carries editorial variant templates (for example 六合之{{另|閒|間}}) that mark text-critical choices; the quotes above avoid the affected characters except where the variant is noted.
- Whether any later Chinese commentary assigns a being's name from the mountain name (Guo Pu's note "以山出此獸，因以名之" appears in the Wikisource transcription at 長右之山) was not verified on the printed leaf images, so it is not quoted here.
