## Findings for the entry (claim C)

**What these three rows can carry.** Four design decisions, and only four. (1) *What the culture calls the being and what the word means*: a monster-word can be built from the verb for showing, and the Latin set is four overlapping nouns, not a taxonomy (row 1, row 2). (2) *How its body is composed*: not by composite anatomy. The Roman notice's monsters are category violations — an infant speaking a ritual cry, an ox on a staircase, human-shaped figures that cannot be approached, a ship-shape in the sky (row 3). If the entry has a "composite anatomy" section, it cannot be sourced from these rows; the chimera material must come from another seat's rows. (3) *Where it lives*: at named places inside and at the edge of the state's territory, plus the sky over the city, while the reading skill is attributed to one region (rows 1 and 3). (4) *Whether it warns, guards, punishes, teaches or devours*: in these texts it warns only, and the response is administrative — consultation of a book, a proclaimed rite, purification, vows (row 3). Nothing here supports a guardian, a punisher, a teacher or a devourer.
