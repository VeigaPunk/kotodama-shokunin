teaches: Polesye field records put leshy, vodyanoy and field spirits among natural loci and the domovik among house spirits, where one name covers several beings and the evidence is thin.
draft sentences:
- Classify a being by the locus it owns before giving it a body: the article separates "spirits of home space" from "spirits of natural space" and names leshy, vodyanoy and polevik as the wood, water and field spirits of the second group (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-1, p. 193).
- Let one name cover several kinds of being and let one of them be necessary: in Polesye domovik covers several types of house spirits, is also used for a dead man who returns at night and for an enrichment spirit, and the house master must live in every house or household affairs fail (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-1, pp. 182, 193).
- Make the name remember a death: the Polesye water spirit is called vodjanyk or vodjanyj, but also toplenyk, "the drowned one", and the same figure is called the unclean spirit, the devil, or the water devil (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-1, p. 185).
- Let the water being wear several bodies and have no fixed species form: in Polesye it appears as a man, a child or a waterfowl, lures people in, drowns bathers and sits on the drowned, yet the "master" of the reservoir and its fish is practically absent there though developed in Russian mythological prose (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-1, pp. 185–186).
- Give the forest being a changing body and a territory: the eastern Polesye lesovik looks like a man whose height shifts from large to small, guards the forest as space belonging to him, and gathers all the snakes at the Exaltation (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-1, p. 186).
- Record from questionnaires and archives, not from fiction: the article's sources are the four-volume *Народная демонология Полесья* (2010–2019) and Belarusian regional field editions, and it points to the Polesye Archive in Moscow as the region's field record (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-1, pp. 180, 182).

### NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2

citation: Dynda, Jiří. 2017. "Rusalki: Anthropology of Time, Death, and Sexuality in Slavic Folklore." *Studia Mythologica Slavica* 20: 83–109. DOI 10.3986/sms.v20i0.6662.
url: https://ojs.zrc-sazu.si/sms/article/view/6662 (article page) and https://ojs.zrc-sazu.si/sms/article/download/6662/6320 (PDF)
route: direct plain request; DOI resolved with HTTP 200 to the article page; PDF HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 384,826 bytes, 27 pages covering journal pp. 83–109, 2026-10-22
kind: paper
tier: B — peer-reviewed argument in an international folklore journal, re-evaluating Zelenin against published and archival ethnography; it interprets other scholars' records rather than reporting new fieldwork.
scope: the rusalka as one of the unclean dead, her appearance in Rusalia Week, her actions toward men, her link to crop fertility, and the modern history of the fish-tailed mermaid image in scholarship and literature. Not a survey of leshy or vodyanoy, and not the primary archive.
quotes:
- Abstract, p. 83: "those who died by unnatural or untimely death, were not allowed to be buried properly and became revenants"
- p. 84: "“Russian mermaids” were for the most part a literary chimaera"
- p. 84: "They are deadly and dangerous for men, whom they seduce, tickle and kill"
- p. 84, n. 3: "was gradually imposed, by the literary influence, to the traditional culture and thus “re-folklorized”"
teaches: Rusalki are the unquiet dead of improper burials, appearing in Rusalia Week to seduce and kill men, while the fish-tailed mermaid is a nineteenth-century literary image later re-folklorised.
draft sentences:
- Begin with the death, not the body: rusalki are the unclean dead — women, girls and infants who died untimely or unjustly and were not buried properly — so their danger follows from a funeral rule before any anatomy (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2, p. 83).
- Time the appearance: rusalki are said to appear only in a fixed season, the week before or after Pentecost, the Rusalia Week, and they move between rivers, branches, fields, woods and village houses (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2, p. 84).
- Do not copy the mermaid: the fish-tailed "Russian mermaid" is, in Dynda's words, for the most part a literary chimera, and the ethnographers who looked for that zoological being in East Slavic folklore did not find it (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2, p. 84).
- Make the encounter social and sexual: rusalki are deadly to men, whom they seduce, tickle and kill, while the same beings are tied to flowering crops and field fertility rather than to water as such (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2, p. 84).
- Model the loop between books and belief: the romantic mermaid image was imposed by literary influence in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, re-folklorised, and then recorded by ethnographers as living tradition, so a late "source" may be quoting a poem back into custom (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2, p. 84 n. 3).
- Keep a calendar function if the being must matter to farmers: Dynda reads rusalki as a representation of eternal unripeness revived each year to carry the community through the liminal phase of the agricultural and liturgical cycle (NEW-ScoutSlavicSpirits-2, Abstract, p. 83).

## Where the "monster" label misfits — observed, then inference

Observed in the opened pages:

- Vinogradova's units of analysis are "spirits of home space", "spirits of natural space",
  and "demonic characters from among the deceased" (p. 193). Beings are indexed by the place
  they belong to and by the dead they come from. Neither article uses the English word
  "monster" or proposes a monster taxon. Vinogradova's frame is "folk demonology" and "lower
  mythology"; Dynda calls rusalki "mythological beings" and works from the same Polesye
  ethnographic corpus.
- The household case is a required presence, not an adversary: the domovik must live in every
  house and without him household affairs fail, and the same name also covers a returning dead
  man and an enrichment spirit (p. 182). A being that a household needs is badly described by a
  word that implies something to be killed.
- The one composite body in this material is the mermaid: green hair, human upper part, fish
  tail instead of legs (p. 84). Dynda identifies that body as a dictionary and literary
  construction, "for the most part a literary chimaera", and notes it was later re-folklorised
  and recorded as tradition (p. 84 and n. 3). The folk rusalka is a revenant of the unclean
  dead with a calendar, not a hybrid species.
- The forestry and water beings have no fixed anatomy either: the lesovik is a man whose height
  changes, the water spirit can be a man, a child or a waterfowl (pp. 185–186). Shape repertoire
  replaces hybrid anatomy in this record.

[INFERENCE] The "monster" label is therefore a reception category, not an emic one, for this
material. A worldbuilder who starts from "monster" buys a body-first, adversary-first frame
that these sources do not support; starting from locus, death and calendar reproduces what the
records actually show. Confidence: high for the classification facts (both pages read in
full); the label argument is my inference from the two papers' categories, not a sentence
either author writes.

## What each source is good for, and what it is not

- Good for: a locus taxonomy that names leshy, vodyanoy and polevik together (1); one name
  covering several house beings, including a necessary house master (1); a water being named
  after drowning and merged with the devil (1); the water being's actions and the absence of an
  "owner of the reservoir" motif in Polesye (1); the leshy's changing height, forest guard and
  snake gathering (1); the questionnaire, archive and multi-volume print base of the record
  (1); the rusalka's etiology in improper burial, her Rusalia Week calendar, her actions toward
  men, her fertility link, and the literary history of her mermaid body (2).
- Not good for: any pan-Slavic synthesis. Vinogradova finds natural-locus beliefs "scant,
  isolated evidence" in Polesye and explicitly contrasts North Russian material; Dynda is about
  rusalki alone. Neither supports treating "demon" as a doctrinal Christian species: in
  Vinogradova it is partly the informants' own word (нечистый, чорт) and partly the scholars'
  category. Neither gives a composite anatomy for leshy
  or vodyanoy. Do not use the mermaid body from 19th-century dictionaries as folk evidence —
  source 2 exists partly to refute that use.
