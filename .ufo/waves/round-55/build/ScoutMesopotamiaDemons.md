Two sources are filed below; both were opened in this session (2026-10-22) and read in full.
The British Museum's live record returned HTTP 403 to a plain request, so the record was read
from a Wayback capture of the same URL (capture id given in the route); the Met's live essay
returned HTTP 429, so it was read from a 2017 Wayback capture. Both are recorded as "challenge
met, not passed" and were reached by an ordinary archive copy, not by any bypass. No browser,
no captcha solving, no paywall, no login. No tool installs and no pacman.

Finding in one line: the two institutions give the two halves of the apotropaic composite — the
Met's curator says Pazuzu's frightening body was *worn* as a shield against Lamashtu and that
such beings were "largely amoral" and could be aimed by ritual, while the British Museum's label
says its human-headed winged bull was a protective spirit *fixed* at a palace doorway; together
they give the entry the portable amulet versus the architectural guardian, the composite parts,
and the historical rise and near-disappearance of one such figure.

draft sentences:
- Name the being by office as well as by name: the Met's curator calls Pazuzu "the son of Hanbu and king of the wind demons", so decide whether a monster's title names a species, a rank, or a territory it rules (NEW-ScoutMesopotamiaDemons-1, § beginning "The exception seems to be Pazuzu").
- Compose the body from ranked parts rather than random hybridity: the essay lists human arms ending in claws, two pairs of wings, a scorpion's tail and a horned, bearded head with bulging eyes and a snarling canine mouth, so decide which animal contributes which power (NEW-ScoutMesopotamiaDemons-1, § beginning "The exception seems to be Pazuzu").
- Make the frightening body do the work of protection: the Met says Pazuzu was "frequently set up as a shield against another supernatural terror: Lamashtu", so decide whether your monster repels by being more terrifying than what threatens the bearer (NEW-ScoutMesopotamiaDemons-1, § beginning "To answer this question").
- Treat the category as amoral and negotiable rather than good or evil: the essay says such beings "seem to be presented as largely amoral" and could be appeased with offerings and incantations, so decide whether your monsters are moral agents or forces that a specialist can feed, bind and aim (NEW-ScoutMesopotamiaDemons-1, § beginning "To answer this question").
- Decide who carries the monster: amulets were worn on the body and a gold fibula with his head was buried in a royal woman's grave at Nimrud, so the same being can be private jewellery and grave goods rather than a temple statue (NEW-ScoutMesopotamiaDemons-1, § beginning "Pazuzu was most popular").
- Give the figure a lifespan and an end: the essay says that by the time of the Parthians Pazuzu's image was almost out of use, so decide what makes a monster cult go obsolete while its image survives in later art (NEW-ScoutMesopotamiaDemons-1, § beginning "The grand buildings on the screen").

## What the pair is good for, and what it is not

- Good for: the naming of a being by rank and parentage (1); a worked composite anatomy with each part named (1); the amoral, negotiable category of supernatural being and the practice of aiming one demon at another (1); the portable amulet and the worn head as the ordinary object type, plus a royal grave as a find context (1); the Neo-Assyrian to Parthian arc of one figure's use and obsolescence (1); the fixed architectural guardian, its composite of human, bull and wings, its doorway findspot, its pair and its date under a named king (2); the museum dispersal that separates a pair (2).
- Not good for: any Mesopotamian "word for monster" or a native category term — neither page gives one, and the Met essay uses the English "demon"; any first-person inscription, ritual text or incantation series; any statement that Pazuzu was always protective (the Met essay says the opposite of the modern film reading and stresses complexity); any claim that the lamassu was called *lamassu* in its own time (the BM record does not use the word; the Met's object title does, but that record is not filed here); any findspot for the Pazuzu objects; and any date for the Met pendant beyond the Met's own "ca. 8th–7th century BCE" object record, which is a separate page from the essay.
- The two rows are deliberately one portable and one fixed: do not merge them into a single "Mesopotamian guardian" claim. The Met row is a curator's synthesis; the BM row is an object label. Cite each for what it actually says.
