draft sentences:
- Wengrow drops "monsters" for "composites" because the older word carries "broader connotations that are unwarranted here", and he says of his own title that it is "purely for effect" (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-1, p. 154).
- A composite is defined by its parts, as images "combining incongruous elements of human and/or animal anatomy", so the worldbuilder must decide which heads, limbs or skins are borrowed and from which species (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-1, p. 154).
- Scarcity is itself evidence: Wengrow says the prominence given to composite figures in accounts of Palaeolithic art is "out of all proportion to their quantity", and finds a comparable paucity in later Neolithic pictorial art, so settle how rare the being is before settling what it means (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-1, pp. 155-157).
- For Palaeolithic and Mesolithic societies the deliberate combination of human and animal parts survives more often in burials than in pictures, so decide whether the being belongs to the mortuary record or to the image repertoire (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-1, p. 155).
- Unambiguous composites "first become widely visible in the archaeological record" with the transition from village to urban life in the Near East, in the late fifth and fourth millennia BC, which lets a worldbuilder peg the being's first appearance to a settlement's growth (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-1, p. 160).
- Sealing "allowed the faithful transmission of images that were often highly complex and ‘counter-intuitive’" while giving the specialists who held seal production new means of controlling what circulated, so decide whether the being is copied by a specialist or performed by anyone (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-1, pp. 160-161).

draft sentences:
- The frame is urban Mesopotamia: "The first age of mechanical reproduction belongs to Mesopotamia", in the efflorescence of urban life there some six thousand years ago, so tie the being's proliferation to cities and their institutions rather than to untouched nature (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-2, Introduction, p. 1).
- The book replaces "monsters" with "composites", a term it says "more accurately describes" the anatomical experiment at issue, so define the culture's own word for such a being before borrowing the English one (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-2, p. 1).
- The body is assembled from parts: the experiment lay in "isolating the limbs or other features of diverse species and recombining them to form images of beings that have no counterparts in the visible world" (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-2, p. 1).
- Do not assume the culture separates fauna from fable: Wengrow quotes an Egyptologist who insists "An ancient Egyptian would have reckoned with the possibility of really encountering a griffin", with the area near the Red Sea coast given as an example of where one might be met (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-2, p. 5).
- Distribution follows networks: once established, composite images "often led remarkably cosmopolitan lives", their distributions in the archaeological record far outstripping those of other figural images and tracking political and commercial expansion (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-2, p. 2).
- The contents page names three modes of image transfer, "transformative", "integrative" and "protective" (ch. 6, p. 88), which gives the entry a vocabulary for how a being's image changes as it travels, though the chapter text itself was not read (NEW-ScoutWengrowComposite-2, Contents, ch. 6).
