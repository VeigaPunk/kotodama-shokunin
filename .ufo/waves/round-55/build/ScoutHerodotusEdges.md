## Findings for the entry (design points the L1 can use)

1. **Naming and word meaning.** 4.27 is the load-bearing sentence: Herodotus takes the name from the Scythians and glosses it (`ἄριμα` one, `σποῦ` eye). A worldbuilder deciding "what the culture calls the being" gets a model of an exonym that is a literal body description in a neighbour's language, adopted without translation into the narrator's own usage.
2. **Composite anatomy is absent.** Across 3.116, 4.13 and 4.27 the griffin is named and given a function (`χρυσοφύλακας`, "that guard gold") but never described. The lion-eagle body is supplied by depiction, not by this text; the entry should say the text supplies habitat and duty, images supply anatomy.
3. **Placement on the map.** 4.13 gives a linear sequence outward from the Issedones: Arimaspians, then griffins, then Hyperboreans whose land reaches the sea. 4.27 calls it the country north of the Issedones. 3.116 puts the gold "in the north of Europe" and generalises in 3.116.3 that the outermost lands hold the finest and rarest things. That is a portable rule for edge-of-map beings, independent of any one culture.
4. **Guardian, not omen or judge.** The griffins' only stated role is guarding gold against the Arimaspians; nothing in these chapters makes them omens, punishers or teachers. The moral or allegorical reading is later and not in these pages.
5. **Report versus belief, explicitly marked.** 3.116.1 `λέγεται`; 3.116.2 `πείθομαι δὲ οὐδὲ τοῦτο` (disbelief in one-eyed men specifically, not in griffins); 4.13 attributes the whole sequence to Aristeas's poem and then objects that Aristeas disagrees with the Scythian account; 4.27 uses `νενομίκαμεν` for received custom. A worldbuilder can copy this three-way stance: received, doubted, asserted.
6. **Monsters as a cause of movement.** 4.13.2 has the Arimaspians push the Issedones, who push the Scythians, who press the Cimmerians. The monster is not only at the edge; it is the reason the edge moves.
