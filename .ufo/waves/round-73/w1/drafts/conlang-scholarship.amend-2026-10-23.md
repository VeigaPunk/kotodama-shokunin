## 1. The structural dichotomy is argued down to a scale

Novikov's article states its method in the abstract: "the author examines the existing classifications of constructed languages and attempts to establish a connection between purposes of their creation and their linguistic features" — 24 words, Abstract, p. 455 (S878).

The dichotomy he works against is the a priori / a posteriori split: the same article calls it one of the most important classifications of constructed languages, then argues it apart (S878).

His ruling on it: "this division should be seen as a scale rather than a binary system", because every a priori language carries its creator's linguistic worldview — Balaibalan, conventionally a priori, leans on natural languages more than SolReSol does, which in turn sits differently from aUI (S878).

On the pragmatic axis he adopts Halley's split into interlangs and artlangs, and places under the interlang side the zonal languages (Križanić's Common Slavonic) and, at the highest degree of a posteriority, the controlled natural languages — Basic English, Globish, E-Prime (S878).

The artlang side is not a class but a range: the Star Wars films use 68 languages with no formal description, while Klingon served as one of Wikipedia's official languages until that was abolished (S878).

His conclusion generalizes the move: the findings "challenge the integrity of the seemingly well-established dichotomies of a priori and a posteriori, auxiliary and artistic languages" — 18 words, Conclusion, p. 464 (S878).

Calibration: the scale reading is the article's own argument against a dichotomy its own survey calls one of the most important — a contested refinement, not a replacement, and the entry cites it as one author's position (S878).

The Eurocentrism finding is the article's own hypothesis-test, not a survey result: Standard Average European features appear even in a priori languages, because the creator's linguistic worldview cannot be fully escaped (S878).

## 2. Three Russian-language typologies, and one reviewer's comparison

Шувалова and Сидорова (2006), as this article restates them, divide artificial languages in two: "1. Auxlangs. Вспомогательные языки", then "2. Вымышленные языки (ВЯ) в широком смысле", whose 2.1 Artlangs split into 2.1.1 languages that left their fictional worlds (Tolkien's languages, Klingon, the Э'П language of the game *Myst*) and 2.1.2 languages that exist only inside the work (Arëndron) (S879).

Their 2.2 is "Personal languages" — languages individual enthusiasts build for their own pleasure; the same authors propose calling these «авторскими языками», author languages (S879).

Piperski (2017) offers a three-way split instead: philosophical or logical languages (loglan, lojban), international auxiliary languages, and artistic languages (S879).

Peterson's taxonomy, delivered as a 2015 podcast episode, sorts by purpose of creation into personal, secret or stealthlang, auxiliary, fictional, artistic, and engineered languages, with Vorlin, Awolang, Esperanto/Novial, Quenya/Sindarin/Klingon/Verdurian, Okuna, and ro/Ithkuil as the respective instances (S879).

The comparison's verdict: "Наиболее детализированной классификацией, по всей видимости, является типология Д. Питерсона" — 10 tokens, in the comparison section's paragraph on differences between the classifications (S879).

The same verdict cuts the other way, and this is the part worth carrying: Peterson's set is the most detailed yet systematizes worse than the Russian typologies, which the article attributes to its oral podcast form (S879).

Calibration: that ranking is one author's comparison inside one article, not a field consensus, and Peterson's taxonomy is a practitioner framework delivered as a podcast episode rather than a published taxonomy (S879).

The comparison table's Peterson column carries three categories the article's prose does not list — parody, sign and graphic languages — so the taxonomy's full extent is a table entry, not an argued class (S879).

The article's own subject is Peterson's classification and its advantages over the Russian typologies, not the reverse: Шувалова/Сидорова is one of the compared typologies (S879).

The article's own footnote calls Lojban's placement among auxlangs disputable, noting that in Piperski's work Lojban belongs to the philosophical or logical group (S879).

## 3. What the classroom study consisted of

The companion article's one-line description of the study: "Sara Sowers-Wills assesses students’ command of concepts in phonology through their creation of a constructed language" — 16 words, §3, p. 5 (S880).

The course design, verbatim: "In the course, examples of phonological systems in natural languages and constructed languages were presented along with instruction in language universals and typology" — 23 words, same section and page (S880).

The claimed novelty, verbatim: "One innovation of this work is that it suggests that a multi-part, creative project can be part of a course even at the introductory level" — 25 words, same section and page (S880).

The course is characterized as "an active learning approach in a project-based phonology course", which is the only description of its format the companion article gives (S880).

## 4. What the lane does with it

Classify a world's constructed language by the purpose it serves in that world first, then by structure: every taxonomy the review article compares sorts on purpose before it sorts on form (S879).

Place the language on the a priori / a posteriori scale rather than assigning a label: the scale is that article's operative claim, and a label invites exactly the false binary it argues against (S878).

Expect Eurocentrism as a default, not an accident: a constructed language built by one creator from one language background will carry that background's features, so a world that wants an alien system must design the departure explicitly (S878).

Do not upgrade the classroom evidence into a pedagogy claim: what these rows support is that creating a language assesses a student's command of phonological concepts, at introductory level and inside a project-based course — nothing here compares it with lectures (S880).
