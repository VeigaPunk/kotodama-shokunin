<h2 class="heading-1">
<a name="1._Introduction"></a>1. Introduction</h2>

<p class="Textbody">Though these values seem straightforward, in practice there are various problems. On the one hand, constructions are sometimes called “optative” which we would call something else. The construction called “optative” in <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_sla" title="view language details">Slave</a> by <a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Rice-1989" title="view reference details">Rice (1989</a>: 1111), for instance, has a very wide range of uses, and the expression of a wish does not seem to be particularly central. Whatever this is, it is not our optative. Very striking in this respect is also the tradition in Turkology to apply the term “optative” to what we would treat as a first person imperative or hortative (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Tenisev-et-al-1997" title="view reference details">Tenišev et al. 1997</a>: 212, 364, 402). A tendency to refer to certain imperative categories with the label “optative” is found outside <a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/altaic#turkic" title="Turkic">Turkic</a> grammars as well. Thus, in <a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Fortescue-1984" title="view reference details">Fortescue’s (1984</a>: 24) description of <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_grw" title="view language details">West Greenlandic</a>, the set of what we take to be 1st person singular, 1st person plural exclusive and 3rd person imperatives or hortatives is distinguished from other imperative forms on formal grounds and called “optative”. On the other hand, the semantic category which fits our notion of optative is often listed under another label, such as “desiderative” in <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_aym" title="view language details">Aymara</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Hardman-de-Bautista-1974" title="view reference details">Hardman de Bautista 1974</a>: 227), <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_gua" title="view language details">Guaraní</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Gregores-and-Suarez-1967" title="view reference details">Gregores and Suarez 1967</a>: 132), and <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_mar" title="view language details">Maricopa</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Gordon-1986" title="view reference details">Gordon 1986</a>: 110), and “benedictive” in <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_lad" title="view language details">Ladakhi</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Koshal-1979" title="view reference details">Koshal 1979</a>: 226).</p>
<p class="Firstlineindent">It is important to note that the first value is assigned to languages that have the optative as an <i>inflectional</i>  category. This means that it is not sufficient for the language to exhibit a dedicated optative construction. Consider <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_nma" title="view language details">Mao Naga</a> (Tibeto-Burman; <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/IN" title="view languages for country">India</a>). Its “blessing and cursing” optatives involve combining the particle <i>peno</i>  with an imperative verb form.</p>
<blockquote class="example"><p class="example-start">(4) <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_nma" title="view language details">Mao Naga</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Giridhar-1994" title="view reference details">Giridhar 1994</a>: 314) </p>

<p class="Textbody">The strategy is analytic, but we would nevertheless say that <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_kuq" title="view language details">Kumyk</a> has optative morphology: the suffix <i>-gaj/-γaj</i>  is fully dedicated to the expression of optatives — it is indeed the optative marker most typical for <a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/altaic#turkic" title="Turkic">Turkic</a> in general. Until recently, the <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_kuq" title="view language details">Kumyk</a> optative occurred without an auxiliary, but nowadays the pattern without auxiliary sounds obsolete. Even so, the need for the auxiliary does not change the fact that there is morphology that is specifically optative in meaning.</p>
<p class="Firstlineindent">Another restriction is that we only consider forms that are available for all persons. For instance, although <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_bsk" title="view language details">Bashkir</a> (<a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/altaic#turkic" title="Turkic">Turkic</a>; <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/RU" title="view languages for country">Russia</a>) has a 2nd person optative to express a wish that something bad should happen (curse) (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Juldasev-1981" title="view reference details">Juldašev 1981</a>: 288), it is shown on the map as lacking an optative. </p>
<h2 class="heading-1">
<a name="3._Related_categories"></a>3. Related categories</h2>
<p class="Textbody">It is important also to distinguish the optative from related categories. We will here discuss subjunctives, desideratives and imperatives (hortatives). </p>
<h3 class="heading-2">
<a name="section3.1."></a>3.1. Subjunctive</h3>
<p class="Textbody">The notion of <b>subjunctive</b>, first of all, is itself a problematic notion. One can choose to define it as a mood that is typical for subordinate clauses, or at least some subtypes, or one can associate it with non-actuality and thereby bring it closer to notions like “irrealis” and “potentialis”. Either way, there is a link with the expression of wishes. First, with <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_rus" title="view language details">Russian</a> we have just illustrated in (3) the fact that the optative may use subordination strategies, and the very combination of a past tense and the particle <i>by</i>  seen in (3) has been called “subjunctive”. Of course, this <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_rus" title="view language details">Russian</a> subjunctive is not a morphological category, and the <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_rus" title="view language details">Russian</a> facts pose no problem for us. The link between the expression of wishes and the subjunctive category is, however, independent of whether the subjunctive is realized by a syntactic pattern as in <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_rus" title="view language details">Russian</a> or by morphology only, as in <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_arm" title="view language details">Eastern Armenian</a>.</p>
<blockquote class="example"><p class="example-start">(6) <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_arm" title="view language details">Eastern Armenian</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Kozintseva-2001" title="view reference details">Kozintseva 2001</a>: 247) </p>

<p class="Textbody">To distinguish the morphological optative – the topic of this chapter – from the wish-expressing morphological subjunctive, such as that of <a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/indoeuropean#armenian" title="Armenian">Armenian</a> (6), we stipulate that the expression of the wish must be the main function. This is sometimes clear enough; but when the category called “optative” has other uses, too, the difference between a multifunctional optative and a multifunctional subjunctive may become very small, and grammarians may disagree. <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_swa" title="view language details">Swahili</a>, for instance, is commonly taken to have a multifunctional subjunctive – <a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Wilson-1970" title="view reference details">Wilson (1970</a>: 118-121) lists as many as seven functions – but <a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Loogman-1965" title="view reference details">Loogman (1965</a>: 206-209) speaks of the “Wishing-Form”. Multifunctional optatives used in non-wishing contexts are illustrated by (7) from <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_arc" title="view language details">Archi</a> (<a class="Family" href="https://wals.info/languoid/family/nakhdaghestanian" title="Nakh-Daghestanian">Nakh-Daghestanian</a>; eastern Caucasus) (a conditional use) and by (8) from <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_mar" title="view language details">Maricopa</a> (<a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/hokan#yuman" title="Yuman">Yuman</a>; Arizona) (what <a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Palmer-1986" title="view reference details">Palmer (1986</a>: 110) calls a “deliberative question” use). </p>
<blockquote class="example"><p class="example-start">(7) <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_arc" title="view language details">Archi</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Kibrik-et-al-1977" title="view reference details">Kibrik et al. 1977</a>: 227) </p>

<h3 class="heading-2">
<a name="section3.2."></a>3.2. Desiderative</h3>
<p class="Textbody">Another notion related to optative is <b>desiderative</b> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Plungian-2000" title="view reference details">Plungian 2000</a>: 317) (see also Chapter <a class="Feature" href="https://wals.info/chapter/124">124</a>). Both categories involve the expression of a wish, but whereas the optative is used for the wish of the speaker, the desiderative is used for the wish of a participant in the state of affairs referred to in the utterance. A language with a dedicated inflectional strategy for the desiderative is <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_nga" title="view language details">Nganasan</a> (<a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/nilosaharan#songhay" title="Songhay">Songhay</a>, <a class="Family" href="https://wals.info/languoid/family/uralic" title="Uralic">Uralic</a>; northern <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/RU" title="view languages for country">Russia</a>).</p>

<h3 class="heading-2">
<a name="section3.3."></a>3.3. Imperative</h3>
<p class="Textbody">The third related notion is the <b>imperative</b>(see also Chapter <a class="Feature" href="https://wals.info/chapter/72">72</a>). Both the imperative and the optative refer to a wish of the speaker. With the optative, the state of affairs wished for is typically outside the sphere of influence of the speaker; see (10a) from <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_agl" title="view language details">Aghul</a> (<a class="Family" href="https://wals.info/languoid/family/nakhdaghestanian" title="Nakh-Daghestanian">Nakh-Daghestanian</a>). With an imperative, however, the speaker launches an appeal to the hearer to fulfill the wish. In <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_agl" title="view language details">Aghul</a>, the optative simply cannot be used in this situation, see (10b).</p>

<h2 class="heading-1">
<a name="5._Geographical_distribution"></a>5. Geographical distribution</h2>
<p class="Textbody">The map documents 319 languages. Worldwide, so it seems, morphological optatives are a fairly infrequent phenomenon (48 languages in our sample). Our sample does not contain any languages with inflectional optatives from Australia or from Africa; we have just two cases in Europe (<a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_alb" title="view language details">Albanian</a> and <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_krm" title="view language details">Karaim</a>), one in New Guinea, and only a few in the Americas and Asia outside of the Caucasus and the area of northern <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/IN" title="view languages for country">India</a> and <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/NP" title="view languages for country">Nepal</a>.</p>
<p class="Firstlineindent">As far as the Caucasus is concerned, the optative was first of all found in all of the North-Caucasian languages for which we were able to find reliable information. <a class="Family" href="https://wals.info/languoid/family/northwestcaucasian" title="Northwest Caucasian">Northwest Caucasian</a> languages, furthermore, seem highly unusual in that they have two optatives; <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_ing" title="view language details">Ingush</a> and <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_chc" title="view language details">Chechen</a>, which are Northeast Caucasian (= <a class="Family" href="https://wals.info/languoid/family/nakhdaghestanian" title="Nakh-Daghestanian">Nakh-Daghestanian</a>), have two optatives too, though one of them is unproductive (Johanna Nichols, p.c.). Unfortunately, data from the South Caucasian (= <a class="Family" href="https://wals.info/languoid/family/kartvelian" title="Kartvelian">Kartvelian</a>) languages proved less accessible. <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_geo" title="view language details">Georgian</a>, at least, does have a morphological optative. Whether the Caucasian optative is a phenomenon due to areal contact or to a common origin is not clear, in part because the genetic affinity of the Northwest and Northeast languages is still under discussion. Interestingly, both <a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/altaic#turkic" title="Turkic">Turkic</a> languages of the Caucasus, <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_krc" title="view language details">Karachay-Balkar</a> and <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_kuq" title="view language details">Kumyk</a>, have a full personal paradigm of a morphologically dedicated optative, which is untypical for <a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/altaic#turkic" title="Turkic">Turkic</a>. </p>
<p class="Firstlineindent">A second area that seems to be characterized by the morphological optative is northern <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/IN" title="view languages for country">India</a> and <a class="Country" href="https://wals.info/country/NP" title="view languages for country">Nepal</a>. The optative occurs in some of the Tibeto-Burman and <a class="Genus" href="https://wals.info/family/austroasiatic#munda" title="Munda">Munda</a> languages, and interestingly, the only Indo-Iranian language we found to have a morphological optative is <a class="Language" href="https://wals.info/languoid/lect/wals_code_nep" title="view language details">Nepali</a> (<a class="Reference" href="https://wals.info/refdb/record/Korolev-1965" title="view reference details">Korolev 1965</a>: 80), probably as a result of the influence of neighboring Tibeto-Burman languages. </p>
