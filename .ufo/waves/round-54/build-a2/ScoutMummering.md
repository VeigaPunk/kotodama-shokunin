Reading note: the two rows are different registers. The 2011 designation describes the recognised contemporary form as friendly and conditional; Fraser's court record documents fear, violence and legal suppression in the 19th-century towns. Keep them separate: the designation's tone does not erase Fraser's evidence, and Fraser's urban record does not stand for the whole custom. Neither row supports a pagan-survival origin or a spirit-portrait reading of the mask; both describe concealment, embodied change and identification.

kind: paper

tier: A — peer-reviewed archival study of the primary court records for this exact period and place; use only within that scope.

scope: The 1861 licensing act and 1862 ban, the Isaac Mercer case, and what the Conception Bay and St. John's trial records show about disguise, gender and venue. Not authority for outport house-visit norms, for the modern revival, or for mummering outside these towns.

quotes:
- p.70, Introduction: "conceal their identities by adopting various disguises and by modifying their speech, posture and behaviour"
- p.75, quoting Consolidated Acts of Newfoundland 1861: 10: "any Person who shall be found… without a written Licence from a Magistrate, dressed as a Mummer, masked, or otherwise disguised"
- p.79, trial-record summary: "some kind of mask (D, H, T) or other facial disguise"
- p.80, trial-record summary: "in female dress as mummers usually disguise themselves"

teaches: A mask licence can be legal as well as social: the state can require a magistrate's licence, then ban the disguise outright, and the court record shows what wearers actually did under it.

draft sentences:
- Modern Newfoundland house-visit mummers conceal identity through disguise and by changing speech, posture and behaviour, then travel in groups from house to house while hosts guess who they are and afterwards give food and drink (NEW-ScoutMummering-1, p.70).
- The paper warns that the revival's picture is selective and idealised, and that the house-visit form has overshadowed other forms and the custom's history of fear and violence (NEW-ScoutMummering-1, p.71).
- In June 1861 a statute made it a public nuisance to be found masked or otherwise disguised without a magistrate's written licence, with a fine of up to twenty shillings or seven days' imprisonment (NEW-ScoutMummering-1, p.75).
- In March 1862 the legislature replaced licensing with an outright ban that remained in force for over a hundred years (NEW-ScoutMummering-1, p.76).
- The court record shows masks, veils or hats, blacked or covered faces and cross-dressing as ordinary mummer disguise, while the accused in these urban cases were exclusively male, unlike the rural house visit that usually involved both men and women (NEW-ScoutMummering-1, p.80).
- Most prosecuted incidents occurred outdoors, unlike the rural house visit where mummers knocked and asked permission, and mummers carried combinations of hatchets, sticks, ropes and whips (NEW-ScoutMummering-1, p.80).

### NEW-ScoutMummering-2

citation: Heritage Foundation of Newfoundland and Labrador. 2011. "Mummering." Provincial Historic Commemorations Program designation, nominated by Dale Jarvis. Stable id: https://heritagenl.ca/discover/provincial-historic-commemorations-program-designations/mummering/ (accessed 2026-10-22).

tier: A — primary statement of the 2011 provincial designation, authoritative for what the designation says about the recognised tradition; not a fieldwork source for historical prevalence or regional variation.

scope: The officially designated description of Newfoundland and Labrador mummering: house visiting, disguise pieced together from household goods, identity guessing, unmasking, conditional hospitality, and the modern forms the designation lists. It carries no 19th-century legal record and no detailed regional typology.

quotes:
- body paragraph 2: "the hosts try to guess the identities of the mummers hidden behind some kind of mask"
- body paragraph 2: "With the lifting of the veil, the stranger becomes the friend"
- body paragraph 2: "a host would not offer a drink until they guess the mummer’s identity"
- body paragraph 2: "They might change their walk, talk, shape, or size-whatever it takes to make them unrecognizable"

teaches: The mask is a relational cover: the host must name the wearer before hospitality flows, and the unmasking turns stranger back into friend.

draft sentences:
- The designation describes mummering as visiting several homes in an evening in disguise, with groups of friends or family piecing disguises together from whatever they have at home (NEW-ScoutMummering-2, body paragraphs 1-2).
- Mummers change walk, talk, shape or size to be unrecognizable, and hosts guess the identities hidden behind "some kind of mask" (NEW-ScoutMummering-2, body paragraph 2).
- The mask comes off when the host names the wearer; the page's formula is that with "the lifting of the veil, the stranger becomes the friend" (NEW-ScoutMummering-2, body paragraph 2).
- Hospitality is conditional on identification: in many homes the host withholds a drink until the mummer's identity is guessed (NEW-ScoutMummering-2, body paragraph 2).
- The designation lists a Christmas house visit, a summertime Come Home Year performance, a song, and art and craft as current forms of the same tradition (NEW-ScoutMummering-2, body paragraph 3).
- The province designated mummering in 2011 as a Distinctive Cultural Tradition and Practice (NEW-ScoutMummering-2, designation line).
