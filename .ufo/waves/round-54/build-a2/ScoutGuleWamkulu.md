# ScoutGuleWamkulu — Chewa Nyau / Gule Wamkulu mask evidence for claim C

Seat: leaf scout, no delegation, no background jobs. Question asked: who may make, wear, see or
speak through a mask; what the mask is said to be; when and where it appears; what it licenses;
what is restricted. All four sources below were opened in this session (2026-10-22); the
attempt-1 scratch was not used. Lane HEAD at reading time: `260c68d`.

No code-contract tracing was involved (the question is bibliographic), so the `lsp` device was not
used and no semantic claim rests on text search. No tool was installed or upgraded; `tesseract 5.5.3`
and `pdftoppm` were already on the host and were used locally for page images only (see `unopened:`).

Cross-source calibration the entry must carry: three of these sources are secondary. UNESCO's
descriptive text is an unsigned summary compiled from the nomination file; Mbewe is a literature
synthesis that itself reports Breugel 2001, Lwanda 2005, Phiri 1974 and Longwe 2006; Zubieta's
statements about Nyau structure are partly hers from 2006 fieldwork in central Malawi and partly
reported from Rangeley 1949, van Breugel 2001 and Boucher 2012; Mtuta reports Kamlongera 1992,
Katan 2004 and Gondwe 2015. Any of them may carry a rule; none of them alone may carry a figure,
a date or a prohibition as fact. The entry should say "as reported by …" for every rule.

### NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1

citation: UNESCO, "Gule Wamkulu" (Malawi, Mozambique, Zambia), element No. 00142, Representative List of the Intangible Cultural Heritage of Humanity, inscribed 2008 (3.COM), originally proclaimed 2005.

url: https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/gule-wamkulu-00142

route: The live page and the live `doc/src/00142-EN.pdf` both returned an F5/TSPD JavaScript challenge ("Please enable JavaScript to view the page content … What code is in the image?") — **challenge met, not passed**; no browser, captcha or token workaround was attempted. The newer capture `20260922182713` holds the challenge page itself and was discarded. Access date 2026-10-22.

kind: web (official inscription record)

tier: B — the listing facts (2008 inscription, 3.COM, 2005 proclamation, the three states parties) are authoritative here, but the descriptive paragraphs are an unsigned nomination-derived summary, so the ethnography is strong secondary only.

scope: what the inscription record itself states about the element: its practitioners, the occasions it appears at, the costume and mask materials, the character types, and UNESCO's own statement of its decline.

quotes:
- element description, opening sentences: "Gule Wamkulu was a secret cult, involving a ritual dance practiced among the Chewa in Malawi, Zambia, and Mozambique."
- element description, opening sentences: "It was performed by members of the Nyau brotherhood, a secret society of initiated men."
- element description, costume-and-characters sentence: "the Nyau dancers wear costumes and masks made of wood and straw, representing a great variety of characters"
- element description, costume-and-characters sentence: "Each of these figures plays a particular, often evil, character expressing a form of misbehavior, teaching the audience moral and social values."

teaches: the inscribed element is a masked dance of an initiated men's society whose figures are typed characters — wild animals, spirits of the dead, slave traders, and recent figures such as the helicopter — each enacting a fault to teach; and UNESCO's own record says the practice is being reduced to tourist and political entertainment.

draft sentences:
- The record's character list is a design menu in the order a worldbuilder needs it: animals, spirits of the dead, slave traders, and then figures from the present, so a mask set is a dated inventory and not a timeless pantheon (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1, element description, costume-and-characters sentence).
- Decide whether the mask is a person or a type: the record lists its figures as characters — animals, spirits of the dead, slave traders, the helicopter — not as named individuals (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1, element description, costume-and-characters sentence).
- Put the appearance on the calendar before putting it on the map: the dance belongs to the season after the July harvest and is also staged at weddings, funerals, and a chief's installation or death (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1, element description, occasions sentence).
- Make wearing an office: performance belongs to members of an initiated men's society, so the mask is a rank artifact and the entry question is membership, not costume (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1, element description, opening sentences).
- Write decline as a live option rather than an ending: the record says the performances are being reduced to entertainment for tourists and for political purposes, which is what a hollowed-out mask society looks like from inside (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1, element description, closing sentence on decline).
- Give every figure a fault to carry: the character teaches by doing the wrong thing in front of the audience, which makes the mask an enforcement instrument and not decoration (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-1, element description, costume-and-characters sentence).

### NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2

citation: Zubieta, Leslie F. 2016, "Animals' Role in Proper Behaviour: Cheŵa Women's Instructions in South-Central Africa", Conservation and Society 14(4): 406-415, doi 10.4103/0972-4923.197606, CC BY.

url: https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/2195/5ae7e11a237042a419dabbcf30c44368fe72.pdf

route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 3,286,180 bytes, 10 pages; the PDF is the publisher's open-access file (each page footer reads "Downloaded free from http://www.conservationandsociety.org on Monday, January 16, 2017"). Journal site not requested. Access date 2026-10-22.

kind: paper

tier: B — peer-reviewed open-access article by a researcher who attended and was initiated into the dambwe in central Malawi in 2006, but its statements about Nyau structure are partly hers and partly reported from Rangeley 1949, van Breugel 2001 and Boucher 2012, and several Chewa consultants are named only by role.

scope: the Nyau/dambwe access rules as she witnesses and reports them — where masks are made, who may approach them, the mask as ancestral spirit, the seniority grades, and the named character types.

quotes:
- p. 407: "Nyau constitutes its own closed circle of knowledge exclusive to initiated men"
- p. 410: "The dambwe is a sacred place inside the graveyard (manda), which is where the structures and masks are made"
- p. 410: "Women must stay away from them because masks are ancestral spirits (mizimu) and, as such, are feared."
- p. 410: "Only a few elder women, and women who have been initiated in Nyau, can approach the masks"

teaches: mask-making is a place-based, graded privilege — the workshop is inside the graveyard, the masks are held to be the ancestral spirits themselves, women keep their distance except a few elder women and women initiated in Nyau, and the highest grade's mask is made only by old men who have graduated from the ordinary grade.

draft sentences:
- Fix the ontology first: these masks are held to be the ancestral spirits themselves (mizimu) and are feared, so a wearer is a presence the living must manage and not an actor in costume (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2, p. 410).
- Locate the workshop: masks and the large theriomorphic structures are made at the dambwe, a sacred place inside the graveyard where firewood collection and hunting are prohibited — mask-making is a mortuary-adjacent craft, and the entry should not put it in a craft quarter (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2, p. 410).
- Grade the makers by grade of mask: the elephant and the two hunter figures that always accompany it are made only at the senior dambwe, whose membership is open only to old men who have graduated from the ordinary dambwe (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2, p. 410).
- Write the exemption into the prohibition: women must stay away from the masks, yet a few elder women and women initiated in Nyau may come close and tease them — a blanket ban produces no scenes, a ban with named exceptions produces them all (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2, p. 410).
- Let the figure carry a kin term: the elephant stands for the father ancestor and the chief and personifies dead chiefs, while the eland headdress is called the mother of all Nyau masks and, literally, "the one who accompanies the corpse to the graveyard" (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2, pp. 409-410).
- Keep the two closed circles distinct: the same page reports Chinamwali, the girls' and women's initiation, as exclusive to women with teachings passed on in secrecy, so a men's mask society is half of a pair of closed institutions and not the whole ritual field (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-2, p. 407).

### NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3

citation: Mbewe, Mastone L. K. 2024, "Assessment of the Gule Wamkulu as a Rite of Passage among the Chewas of the Central Region in Malawi", in L. Ndekha, J. Bachmann, R. Munyenyembe & J. Kügler (eds.), *African Traditional Religions Revisited*, Bamberg: University of Bamberg Press, pp. 131-147, doi 10.20378/irb-106013, CC BY.

url: https://fis.uni-bamberg.de/bitstreams/7e782cb4-cda8-4e8f-9f91-8b268f57c5db/download

route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 387,760 bytes, 18 PDF pages = printed pp. 131-147. Access date 2026-10-22.

kind: book chapter

tier: B — a chapter in an edited, peer-reviewed volume, but it is explicitly a synthesis "from an analysis of available literature and older scholarly data", so every rule below is a report of Breugel 2001, Lwanda 2005, Phiri 1974 or Longwe 2006 and second hand in this row.

scope: the licensing of performance (the mzinda right and its fee economy), the historical enforcement of distance (the red-flag announcer and the colonial ban on beatings), the seasonal ban, and the exclusion of the uninitiated.

quotes:
- p. 135: "a person carrying a red flag walked ahead of the Nyau masquerades"
- p. 136: "except for the purpose of a funeral, the Nyau ceremonies should not be conducted during the rainy season"
- p. 138: "For a chief to conduct the Nyau, he or she had to get a bundle of rights from a Senior Chief"
- p. 138: "Any village chief conducting initiation ceremonies without receiving Mzinda from his or her Senior Chief was severely punished."

teaches: staging the masquerade is a licensed right held downward from a senior chief and enforced by punishment, the right and the initiation fees are one economy, the approach of the masquerade was once cleared by a flag-bearer, and the ritual calendar has been set by the state as well as by the harvest.

draft sentences:
- Make the right to stage a masquerade a licensed office: a chief could perform Nyau only with *mzinda*, a bundle of rights granted by a senior chief, and performing without it was severely punished (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3, p. 138).
- Follow the fee to see the incentive: without *mzinda* a chief could neither conduct initiations nor collect the fees paid in money, chickens, mats, cattle and goats, so the mask society is also a revenue system with an owner (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3, p. 138).
- Decide how the street is cleared: in the pre-colonial period a person carrying a red flag walked ahead of the masquerades and called on travellers to run away and hide — masking needs a traffic rule, and the colonial order that the masquerades stop beating people on the roads is the moment the rule changes (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3, p. 135).
- Choose what the mask licenses and who is shut out: uninitiated boys and girls were excluded from important community functions, so the visible mask marks a membership boundary rather than offering entertainment (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3, p. 132).
- Set ritual duration as a negotiated quantity: a Presidential order barred Nyau ceremonies in the rainy season except for funerals, and school terms and that order compressed initiation from about a month to three or five days (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3, p. 136).
- Let belief move: the chapter reports that at present young people consider the performers to be human beings, not spirits or wild beasts as was the case in the past — so the entry can hold a culture's own account of what the mask is alongside its own account of what changed (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-3, p. 133).

### NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-4

citation: Mtuta, Anthony Gilbert 2023, "Gulewamkulu institutional and organizational factors influencing community development projects in the area of T.A. Chauma in Dedza District, Malawi", International Journal of Anthropology and Ethnology 7: 21, doi 10.1186/s41257-023-00100-4, open access.

url: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s41257-023-00100-4

route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, `text/html`, 307,162 bytes, full open-access text (no challenge). Access date 2026-10-22.

kind: paper

tier: B — a peer-reviewed open-access article, but its type is "Review", its organizational claims are cited from Kamlongera 1992, Katan 2004 and Gondwe 2015, and its own evidence is a binary logit model on a questionnaire in one Traditional Authority area, so it may support no rule on its own.

scope: only two things — the article's statements that chiefly ownership governs the society and that concealment licensed speech and retaliation, plus its glossary definitions of *gule* and *nyau*.

quotes:
- section "What is Gulewamkulu?": "the performer wearing the mask is thought to be the ghost of a deceased ancestor"
- section "Origin of Gulewamkulu": "Gulewamkulu became a venue for these guys to air their concerns and exact retribution on abuses"
- section "Findings and discussion": "Gulewamkulu is owned and ruled by Chewa chiefs."
- section "Findings and discussion": "Every member of the organization is required to abide by the severe norms of Gulewamkulu."

teaches: the mask is the licence — concealment let men speak grievances and take revenge they could not otherwise, and the society that owns that licence is chiefly property with severe internal norms.

draft sentences:
- Treat the mask as a licence to speak: the reported origin story has men taking up the dance and, once their identities were hidden, using it to air grievances and exact retribution for abuses (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-4, section "Origin of Gulewamkulu").
- Name the owner of the licence: the article states the society is owned and ruled by Chewa chiefs, which is the same fact Mbewe's *mzinda* rule states procedurally (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-4, section "Findings and discussion").
- Write the internal penalty: members are required to abide by severe norms, and breaches carry severe penalties, so an unmasked disclosure is the crime the mask system exists to define (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-4, section "Findings and discussion").
- Fix the vocabulary before using it: this article's glossary gives *gule* as "big dance" and *nyau* as "another name for Gulewamkulu … the attire that the mask dancers put on", while the same article and Zubieta use *Nyau* for the society and its structures, so the entry must declare which sense it uses (NEW-ScoutGuleWamkulu-4, glossary).

Two cautions for the entry text. First, the sources disagree on how closed the society is: UNESCO calls it a secret society of initiated men and Mbewe/Zubieta describe a men's closed circle, while Zubieta also reports *Chinamwali* as a women's closed ceremony with its own secrecy (p. 407) — write the pair, not one half. Second, "Gule Wamkulu" and "Nyau" are not used alike in these rows: UNESCO uses Nyau for the society and Gule Wamkulu for the dance, Mtuta's glossary calls Nyau "another name for Gulewamkulu" tied to "the attire that the mask dancers put on", and Zubieta uses Nyau for the society, the structures and the masks (p. 410), so the entry should fix its own usage in one line rather than inherit the slippage.

## Remaining unknowns (not resolved by these four rows)

- No opened row states the consequence of revealing a mask's secret to an outsider; "severe penalties" (4) and "severely punished" (3, for performing without *mzinda*) are the only enforcement statements, and they cover different offences. The entry must not merge them.
- No opened row gives a mask's manufacture (wood species, straw, binding, paint, repair, retirement or burial of a worn mask). UNESCO says only "wood and straw"; the entry should mark manufacture detail as open.
- No opened row dates the tradition. The 2017 record repeats the seventeenth-century Chewa Empire claim unsigned, and Mbewe's origin section lists conflicting legends (Zaire/DRC, Makewana at Msinja, a women's dance taken over by men, Nyanda's famine dance) without adjudicating. Anything earlier than the 2005 proclamation should be marked (to-verify).
- Outside the scope of the question and not pursued: Zambia's and Mozambique's own Nyau variants (the rows are Malawi-centred; UNESCO's page is the only three-state source here).
