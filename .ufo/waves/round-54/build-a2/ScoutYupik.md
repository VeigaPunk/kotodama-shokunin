### NEW-ScoutYupik-1

citation: Johnson, Emily. 1997. "Yup'ik Dance: Old and New." Journal for the Anthropological Study of Human Movement 9(3), Spring 1997: 132–149 (journal index; PDF pp. 131–149). Open PDF on the journal's own site.
url: https://jashm.press.uillinois.edu/9.3/9-3Yupik_Johnson131-149.pdf
route: direct, HTTP 200, application/pdf, 354,012 bytes, 19 PDF pages (curl status check 2026-10-22); text extracted locally with pdftotext 26.08.0. The issue's index page https://jashm.press.uillinois.edu/9.3/ (HTTP 200) supplied the volume, number, season, page range and ISSN (1940-7610).
kind: paper
tier: B — a peer-reviewed article by an author of Yup'ik descent who reports her own family interviews, but its mask ethnography is largely attributed to Fienup-Riordan (1990, 1991, 1996) and to elder testimony it quotes; it is authoritative for "how a 1997 Yup'ik author described mask practice", not for Yup'ik practice as such. Tier A would need Fienup-Riordan's own text or a community statement.
scope: the mask's name and gloss, the vision-to-carver sequence and the carver's latitude, the ringed "model cosmos" design, the masked dance (kelek) as invitation and as the shaman's road, the dancers' downcast eyes, where masks were carved and who witnessed ceremonies, and the missionary removal and later revival of masked dancing. Not: post-ceremonial disposal (this article does not state it), named mask types, or any rule about who may not see a mask.
quotes:
- p. 141: "The mask (kegginaquq - literally 'thing that is like a face') was a central element in Yup'ik ceremonial dancing."
- p. 141: "The image of a mask was first seen in a shaman's dream which was related to a mask-carver"
- p. 141: "but relied mostly on his own interpretation and the natural shape of the wood"
- p. 140: "The people dancing behind the masks were the 'road' by which the shaman traveled to other worlds."
teaches: the mask is a likeness whose form is negotiated between a visionary and a carver, and the dancer who wears it is a route for someone else's travel.
draft sentences:
- Start from what the mask is said to be: kegginaquq is glossed as "thing that is like a face", a likeness of a being rather than the being, so decide whether donning it makes the wearer that being or only its image (NEW-ScoutYupik-1, p. 141).
- Split authority over the object: the image comes from a shaman's dream, but the carver works mostly from his own interpretation and the natural shape of the wood, which gives the craftsman real licence and a real quarrel with the visionary (NEW-ScoutYupik-1, p. 141).
- Separate the wearer from the beneficiary: the dancers behind the masks are the road by which the shaman travels to other worlds, so the person carrying the mask need not be the person it serves (NEW-ScoutYupik-1, p. 140).
- Build the mask as a model cosmos and ration its sight: a central face is ringed by ellanguat ("pretend or model cosmos"), the eyes are treated as doors between worlds, and the dancers keep their own eyes downcast to stay in the physical world (NEW-ScoutYupik-1, p. 141).
- Let the masked dance be an invitation with a name: kelek derives from keleg, "to invite to one's house", which tells you whom the ceremony is bringing in and who is obliged to receive them (NEW-ScoutYupik-1, p. 140).
- A mask licence can be revoked and restored: the drive to eliminate dancing gained a headquarters when the Moravian Church was established at Bethel in 1885, masked dances and the kelek were among the first practices removed, and later pages record school dancing and a festival mask exhibit as revival (NEW-ScoutYupik-1, pp. 144, 146–147).

tier: A — the exhibition/catalogue is co-curated by the community in Quinhagak with the excavating team, and these are its own records of its own excavated objects; the tier is scoped to what the catalogue says about the Nunalleq finds and their deposition, not to Yup'ik ritual rules in general.

Not: who may wear a mask today, named mask owners, or any prohibition.
quotes:
- object record GDN-248:78007, Description: "Some fire damage exists in the form of charring around the left eye, which may have been intentional damage before disposal."
- object record GDN-248:78007, Description: "This mask was found tucked down into a post hole in the earlier house at the site"
- object record GDN-248:23893, Description: "There is a burned, round ownership mark on the backside of the mask."
- section "In the Winter: Dancing and Drumming in the Qasgiq": "The beautifully made kegginaqut [masks] and ceremonial regalia found at Nunalleq are very similar to those used in Yupiit winter ceremonies"
teaches: a mask can be a short-lived, deliberately damaged, sometimes owned object deposited in a house rather than an heirloom, and a community-co-curated catalogue is itself a design for who authors a mask's meaning.
draft sentences:
- Give the mask a lifespan and a place of deposit instead of an archive: at Nunalleq masks were left in post holes and trash pits, and one seal mask's charring is read as possible intentional damage before disposal (NEW-ScoutYupik-2, GDN-248:78007, Description; GDN-248:78013, Description).
- Decide who owns a mask between uses: a full-sized bird mask carries a burned, round ownership mark on its back (NEW-ScoutYupik-2, GDN-248:23893, Description).
- Fix how the mask attaches to the body: suspension holes survive on the reverse of several masks, so the mask was tied on and the dancer's hands stayed free for gesture (NEW-ScoutYupik-2, GDN-248:78007, Description; GDN-248:23886, Description).
- Make the mask a composite of the story rather than a portrait of one animal: attachments representing other animals and characters were added to complete a layered narration (NEW-ScoutYupik-2, section "Qirussiq: Seal flipper attachment", Description).
- Borrow a 16th-century find for later practice only with the continuity claim stated: the catalogue says the Nunalleq masks are very similar to those used in Yup'ik winter ceremonies four centuries later (NEW-ScoutYupik-2, section "In the Winter: Dancing and Drumming in the Qasgiq").
- Decide who writes a mask's meaning: the catalogue is co-curated by the community in Quinhagak with the excavating team, which makes its descriptions community-accountable rather than an outside ethnographer's (NEW-ScoutYupik-2, section "Welcome to the Nunalleq Digital Museum & Catalogue").

## What this adds to the entry, and what it does not settle

Design decisions these three rows can carry:

| decision | what the opened sources say |
|---|---|
| what the mask is | a likeness ("thing that is like a face"), a model cosmos, a composite of story attachments, a being's image that may or may not be the being |
| who may make it | the shaman supplies the vision, the carver supplies interpretation and the wood's grain; carving happened in the men's house |
| who wears it, who it serves | the dancer is the road for the shaman's travel; the wearer is not necessarily the agent |
| what it licenses | invitation (keleg), sight (eyes as doors), a journey between worlds; and it can be revoked by religious authority and restored by festival |
| how long it lives | short: deposited in post holes and pits, sometimes charred or broken before disposal, sometimes marked as someone's property |
| who sees it | the whole community gathered in the men's house to witness ceremonies; later the mask is shown in galleries and festival exhibits |

Gaps this seat could not close (do not state these in the entry as if sourced):

- **No opened source states a single-use rule.** The Nunalleq records show deposition in post
  holes and trash pits and possible deliberate damage before disposal; they do not say every
  ceremonial mask was made for exactly one ceremony. The claim "made for one ceremony, then
  destroyed" is a common summary of Fienup-Riordan's account, and I could not open her text
  this session, so a draft sentence must stay at "deposited and damaged before disposal" or be
  marked `(to-verify)`.
- **No opened source states a gender or age prohibition on seeing or wearing masks.** Johnson
  gives the men's house as the carving place and the community as witnesses; she does not
  report that women were barred from masked dances. A worldbuilder can design such a rule, but
  it must not be attributed to Yup'ik practice on this evidence.
- **Restricted/sacred material:** both opened Yup'ik-facing sources are public and
  community-co-curated; nothing here required handling restricted knowledge, and nothing here
  should be extended into one.

Confidence: high for the three rows' quotes and for the exhibition facts; medium for the
"single-use" reading (supported only as deposition and damage, not as a stated rule).
