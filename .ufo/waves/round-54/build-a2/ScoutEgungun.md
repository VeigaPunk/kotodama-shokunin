## Findings the entry can build on

1. **The costume is the mechanism, not the ornament.** All three substantive sources describe a
   full-body cloth assemblage whose stated purpose is that the wearer not be seen as a person:
   èkú/agò at Òkèigbó, èkú at Ọ̀yọ́. The mask is not a face-plate; it is a volume of cloth that
   replaces a body. The Cleveland record adds the museum's own claim that the panels are a
   "dwelling place" for ancestral spirits.
2. **Access is organised around a place, not only around the object.** At Òkèigbó the masker
   dresses in the igbó-ìgbàlè grove; at Ọ̀yọ́ the costume is sewn and repaired in the ìgbàlè. Both
   sources record a bar on women entering that room or grove, and an overlapping set of female titles appears at
   both sites: NEW-ScoutEgungun-2 names the post-menopausal Iya Àgan and Iya Mojè who accompany the
   egúngún, while NEW-ScoutEgungun-1's own informant list includes the three female Òjè̀ title
   holders of Òkèigbó, among them the Ìyá-Àgan (p.19 note 10). Neither source says a titled woman
   may enter the grove, so a worldbuilder should decide whether the ban is on seeing the mask, on
   entering the place, or on knowing the craft — these sources separate them.
3. **The wearer may be a hired outsider.** At Ọ̀yọ́ the Jeńjù masker must be a stranger from
   another town, paid in money and cloth, and Ọ̀yọ́ indigenes are barred from the role. Ownership
   of the mask and the identity of the man inside are deliberately kept in different hands.
4. **The voice question has two answers in the sources, and they are not the same answer.**
   NEW-ScoutEgungun-1 treats the talking drum's surrogate language as the speech the masked
   performer must decode and answer in dance. NEW-ScoutEgungun-4 gives the
   masker an altered voice (a "low fumble", a high falsetto, a piping treble) that preserves the illusion. The
   entry should present these as two designs, not merge them.
5. **What the mask licenses is bounded and auditable.** At Òkèigbó spectators may blame the human
   family for a poor or tattered costume but may not criticise the egúngún; an attendant
   (atọ́kùn egúngún) keeps dangerous emblems away from a warrior egúngún when the drum incites it.
   At Ogidi-Ijumu the night masquerades' warnings and orders "must not be violated". Licence is
   granted to the presence, and the cost of a bad performance is carried by the lineage.

### NEW-ScoutEgungun-1

citation: Fámúlè, Oláwọlé. 2018. "Èdè Àyàn: The Language of Àyàn in Yorùbá Art and Ritual of Egúngún." Yoruba Studies Review 2(2): 1–50. doi 10.32473/ysr.v2i2.129886, ISSN 2578-692X.

route: direct plain request, HTTP 200 application/pdf, 10,280,213 bytes, 51 PDF pages, CC BY-NC 4.0; landing page HTTP 200. No access control met.
kind: paper
tier: A — a 50-page peer-reviewed study whose author reports 25 years of research at Òkèigbó with named cult office-holders and family custodians; primary for the Òkèigbó practice he describes. The mythic material he quotes (Odù Ifá, Bascom 1969) is second-hand in this row and is not what the draft sentences use.
scope: the èkú/agò shroud-costume and its wearer (arèkú), the igbó-ìgbàlè dressing place and the secrecy obligation, the Ọdún Egúngún calendar at Òkèigbó (Òndó State, Nigeria), and the talking drum as the masquerade's speech channel.
quotes:
- p.12, §"Egúngún: A Physical manifestation of the Yorùbá ancestor spirits", ¶1: "Egúngún is thought of as a physical manifestation of the ancestor spirit(s)."
- p.17, same section, ¶5: "The primary purpose of èkú/agò is to totally obliterate the identity of the human agent that dons it"
- p.26, §"Performance Context of Ọdún Egúngún", translation of an Èsà Ògbín oríkì: "I asked why then are females not allowed therein (at the forest-grove of egúngún)?"
- p.42, dance-criticism passage: "they cannot criticize the egúngún to which the èkú/agò gave form."
teaches: the mask is a shroud assemblage whose stated job is to erase the wearer so that an ancestor is present; dressing happens in a cult grove before the masker emerges, and a quoted oríkì asks why women are not allowed there; cult members who know what happens in the grove are forbidden to reveal it; the mask's speech is the talking drum's surrogate language, which the performer must decode and answer in dance; criticism of a costume is aimed at the human lineage, never at the ancestor; at the time of writing only one egúngún still appeared from the grove while the rest emerged from family compounds called "houses in heaven" (ilérun); the annual festival at Òkèigbó runs a fixed seven-day sequence from 25 July, except for the founding lineage that starts a day early.
draft sentences:
1. Decide whether the mask is a costume worn by a person or a presence that replaces the person: at Òkèigbó the shroud is said to exist to obliterate the wearer's identity (NEW-ScoutEgungun-1, p.17).
2. Decide where dressing happens and who may watch: the masker dresses in a forest-grove, and the cult members who see it are bound never to reveal it, under threat of a deadly consequence (NEW-ScoutEgungun-1, p.17–18).
3. Give an exclusion a stated reason rather than a mood: an oríkì the author translates asks why women are not allowed in the egúngún grove, and answers that whoever tills that ground with a hoe has struck his father with it (NEW-ScoutEgungun-1, p.26).
4. If the mask must speak, decide whether the throat or an instrument is its mouth: here the drum's surrogate speech addresses the egúngún and the performer must decode it and answer in dance (NEW-ScoutEgungun-1, p.42–43).
5. Decide what criticism the mask may receive and who carries the shame: spectators may blame the family for a tattered or ugly costume but may not criticise the egúngún (NEW-ScoutEgungun-1, p.42).
6. Decide whether every mask in the system is an ancestor: the article separates entertainment masquerades (egúngún aláré, onídán, agbégijó, alárìnjó) from the Ọ̀yọ́ type that bears the ancestor, so masking is a genre with a non-ancestral branch (NEW-ScoutEgungun-1, p.15).
