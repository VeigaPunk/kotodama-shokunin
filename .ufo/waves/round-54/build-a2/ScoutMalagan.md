### NEW-ScoutMalagan-1
citation: Albert, Steven M. 1987. "Tubuan: Masks and Men in Southern New Ireland, Papua New Guinea". Expedition Magazine 29(1), Penn Museum (the Penn Museum's own magazine; the article's objects are the museum's axes).
url: https://www.penn.museum/sites/expedition/tubuan/
route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, text/html, access date 2026-10-22
kind: paper
tier: A — a first-hand field account by the researcher himself, published by the museum that holds the objects, and explicitly limited by him to the public dimension of the masking system.
scope: what a southern New Ireland (Lak) masking society does in public — who is initiated, how sight is bought, how figures are owned and disposed of, and how secrecy is enforced. It is not a statement about Tolai practice, although it reports the Lak borrowing of Tolai masks.
quotes:
- opening section, paragraph beginning "I did not know of firam": "in some villages, every Lak male, from age 5 or 6 on, was an initiate in the secret society"
- opening section, same paragraph: "a hidden, virtually alternative society, which revolved around the masks and rigidly excluded women"
- opening section, paragraph on the author's initiation: "buying the right to see it in the triun, the otherwise forbidden spot where seniors construct the masks"
- "Conclusion: Masks, Secrecy and Belief", first paragraph: "men say it is a spirit but clearly know that it is they who make the mask and don it"
teaches: the mask is a spirit men know they make; sight is a purchased grade; the society is near-universal for men and closed to women; each figure is destroyed after its appearance while its authority continues in a planted bush; masks are clan property that may not be alienated, but masks bought from Tolai or Duke of York owners are personal property that can be sold; producing a clan figure at a mortuary feast is an overt claim of leadership, and unauthorized disclosure (kilung) is fined in pigs.
draft sentences:
- Among the Lak of southern New Ireland the masking society takes in nearly every male, in some villages from about five or six years old, and it revolves around the masks while rigidly excluding women (NEW-ScoutMalagan-1, opening section).
- Seeing the mask is a purchased grade, not a public right: the ethnographer's lowest initiation bought the right to see it where the seniors build it, so access to sight is itself property (NEW-ScoutMalagan-1, opening section).
- Lak men say the tubuan is a spirit while knowing they make and wear it, and the account argues that revealing the trick does not dissolve the figure's mystery but deepens it (NEW-ScoutMalagan-1, "Conclusion: Masks, Secrecy and Belief").
- Each figure is made for an appearance and destroyed afterwards, yet its authority is carried on by a planted bush said to be the tubuan, so destruction ends the object without ending the office (NEW-ScoutMalagan-1, "Ownership and Use of the Tubuan").
- Lak masks are clan possessions with sanctions against alienation, while masks bought from Tolai or Duke of York Island owners become personal property that can be sold — two ownership regimes inside one system, and a direct model for a world that separates inherited and purchased masks (NEW-ScoutMalagan-1, "General Features of the Tubuan System").

### NEW-ScoutMalagan-2
citation: musée du quai Branly – Jacques Chirac, "Malagan head", collection record and curatorial essay for object 70.2017.66.28 (Tabar Islands, New Ireland, 20th century; gift of Marc Ladreit de Lacharrière).
url: https://collection-lacharriere.quaibranly.fr/en/malagan-head
route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, text/html, access date 2026-10-22
kind: record
tier: A — the holding museum's own record and essay for this object; the ethnographic sentences it quotes from Gunn, Peltier and Küchler are second hand in this row and are not used as primary evidence here.
scope: the malagan object family of New Ireland and the Tabar Islands, the single-use disposal of most works, and the marada rain heads' making, restricted enclosure and inheritance.
quotes:
- "Malagan traditions", paragraph after the Michael Gunn attribution: "these highly elaborate objects were only used once"
- "Malagan traditions", same paragraph: "they were then destroyed, abandoned or sold once the ceremonies were over"
- "Calling for rain", paragraph on the marada heads: "wrongly called ‘masks’ (because of their size, they could not be worn)"
- "Calling for rain", same paragraph: "they were displayed in special enclosures that the uninitiated were not allowed to enter"
teaches: malagan is a family of ritual objects across New Ireland and the Tabar Islands; most were spent in one ceremony and then destroyed, abandoned or sold, a few excepted as kept heirlooms; the marada heads were not wearable and topped plant-fibre figures; rain-making was an inherited office passed from father to son; rights over designs and production sat with clans and clan leaders, and sculptors worked to detailed instructions with offerings at each stage.
draft sentences:
- In New Ireland and the Tabar Islands one word names a whole family of ritual objects, so a world should not assume one object type behind a single term (NEW-ScoutMalagan-2, "Malagan traditions").
- Most of these works were used once: when the ceremony had spent them they were destroyed, abandoned or sold, and only a few were kept and handed down for reuse (NEW-ScoutMalagan-2, "Malagan traditions").
- The marada heads of the Tabar Islands were "wrongly called masks" by the museum's own account, because their size kept them from being worn, and they topped plant-fibre figures instead (NEW-ScoutMalagan-2, "Calling for rain").
- Rain-making was inherited office work: the heads stood in enclosures the uninitiated could not enter, and the magicians' responsibility passed from father to son (NEW-ScoutMalagan-2, "Calling for rain").

### NEW-ScoutMalagan-3
citation: Küchler, Susanne. 2002. Malanggan: Art, Memory and Sacrifice. Oxford: Berg (Materializing Culture). Publisher's preview PDF, front matter and Introduction, printed pp. 1–10.
url: https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781000184075_A40249304/preview-9781000184075_A40249304.pdf
route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, application/pdf, converted by the corpus reading tool; preview only (Introduction pp. 1–10), access date 2026-10-22
kind: book
tier: B — a publisher's preview of a scholarly monograph; the quoted words are the author's own introduction, but the book's body was not opened.
scope: how a malanggan effigy is animated, displayed and allowed to die; the disposal of the remains; the argument that the image, not the carving, is the durable property whose access defines rank and land rights.
quotes:
- Introduction, printed p. 1: "When installed in a structure that is specifically erected for its display, the effigy is thought to be alive"
- Introduction, printed p. 1: "the ‘empty’ ‘remains’ of effigies being taken to the forest to be left to decompose or to the mission to be sold to collectors"
- Introduction, printed p. 2: "The figure is thus an instance of a polity of images, whose access defines rank, authority and rights to land."
- Introduction, printed p. 4: "Malanggan thus capture a form of property that is not material, but intellectual in kind."
teaches: the effigy is animated during production, installed, treated as alive, then ritually killed; months of preparation end in an hour; the remains go to the forest to rot or to a mission to be sold; what survives is a named image, and personal names are names of malanggan images passed down as a finite set.
draft sentences:
- An effigy is put in a structure built only for its display, treated as alive because it was animated while being made, and then allowed to die (NEW-ScoutMalagan-3, Introduction, p. 1).
- Months of preparation end in an hour, and the emptied remains go to the forest to rot or to a mission to be sold to collectors, so disposal belongs to the rite rather than marking a failure of preservation (NEW-ScoutMalagan-3, Introduction, p. 1).
- What outlasts the carving is an image, and the effigy belongs to a polity of images whose access defines rank, authority and rights to land (NEW-ScoutMalagan-3, Introduction, p. 2).
- Malanggan is property that is intellectual rather than material, so a world should let rights in a named design outlive every object made from it (NEW-ScoutMalagan-3, Introduction, p. 4).

### NEW-ScoutMalagan-4
citation: Tassignon, Isabelle. 2018. "Under the Scrutiny of the Mosquito Spirit: A Baining Kavat". Fondation Gandur pour l'Art, Geneva, collection essay for FGA-ETH-OC-0009 (curator of the ethnology collection, December 2018; cites Corbin, Fajans and Küster–Corbin).
url: https://www.fg-art.org/users_uploads/oeuvre_oeuvre_documents/5c331646addcf.pdf
route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, application/pdf, converted by the corpus reading tool, access date 2026-10-22
kind: record
tier: B — the holding foundation's curatorial essay for one object: a museum synthesis citing published ethnography, not a peer-reviewed study.
scope: Central Baining (Kairak and Uramot) kavat masking on the Gazelle Peninsula, New Britain: gender exclusion and its origin story, the dream commission, destruction by fire after the dance, and the day/night dance division.
quotes:
- "Men and masks", first paragraph: "though the original myth relates that women created the first masks, men stole the technique from them and then barred women from their production"
- "Happy is he who dreams of forest spirits", final paragraph: "these spirits only reveal themselves to the initiated"
- same paragraph: "By doing the latter the honour of entering their dreams, the spirits ask the initiated to bring them to life in a mask"
- "Watch out for the Atutki!", final paragraph: "Burned in the same fire after use, the masks must not survive these performances."
teaches: masks are men's business by a myth of stolen technique; the art passes from father to son among the initiated; spirits appear in dreams and commission their own masks; masks are destroyed in the fire after use, so collected examples are rarities; the day dance is for the dead and fertility and is danced by women every two or three years, the night dance belongs to men and forest spirits; kavat chase and frighten the uninitiated and children, and sick children are pushed between a dancer's legs to be healed.
draft sentences:
- Baining kavat masks belong to men through a story of theft: the myth says women made the first masks, men took the technique and barred women from production, and the art passes from father to son among the initiated (NEW-ScoutMalagan-4, "Men and masks").
- The spirits are not on general view: they reveal themselves only to the initiated, and then ask in dreams to be brought to life in a mask, so the initiative for a mask is the spirit's rather than the carver's (NEW-ScoutMalagan-4, "Happy is he who dreams of forest spirits").
- The mask must not survive the dance: burned in the same fire after use, kavat are meant to leave no object behind, which is why collected examples are described as rarities (NEW-ScoutMalagan-4, "Watch out for the Atutki!").
- The Baining calendar divides audiences by dance: the day dance for the dead and for fertility is danced by women every two or three years, while the night dance is men's and is dedicated to forest spirits (NEW-ScoutMalagan-4, "Watch out for the Atutki!").

### NEW-ScoutMalagan-5
citation: Horniman Museum and Gardens, object record 1997.2, "Mask", commentary text (Night Dance Mask, Kavat, Baining people, New Britain; bamboo, barkcloth, pigment, late 20th century).
url: https://www.horniman.ac.uk/object/1997.2/
route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, text/html, access date 2026-10-22
kind: record
tier: B — a short museum object commentary: usable as a label and as a counter-case, not as ethnography.
scope: one kavat and its label's account of the dance, the making place, the spirit identity and the disposal of masks. Its disposal sentence disagrees with NEW-ScoutMalagan-4 and should be reported as a disagreement, not a rule.
quotes:
- Commentary, first paragraph: "produced for use in the miaus or langani male night dances"
- Commentary, third paragraph: "constructed and painted before the ceremony inside a sacred shelter (kanamitki) in the bush"
- Commentary, fourth paragraph: "The kavat mask depicts a bird-like leaf-spirit called a rengit"
- Commentary, final paragraph: "At the end of the night dance, all of the masks except the kavat are either burned in the fires, or discarded to decompose."
teaches: kavat are made for male night dances in which masked spirits come out of the bush and chase women and children; making happens in a sacred shelter; the eyes carry the power and the triangles beneath them are tears; the dancer's gender is marked by the shape of the barkcloth penis-sheath; and this label says the kavat is the mask that is *not* burned, which the Gandur essay contradicts.
draft sentences:
- The Horniman describes kavat as made for the miaus or langani male night dances, the dances that celebrate senior men's connection with bush spirits (NEW-ScoutMalagan-5, Commentary).
- Masks are built and painted before the ceremony in a sacred shelter in the bush, so the making happens in sacred ground away from the village, not at the dance ground (NEW-ScoutMalagan-5, Commentary).
- The kavat is a bird-like leaf-spirit called a rengit that lives in trees and comes down to dance (NEW-ScoutMalagan-5, Commentary).
- This label contradicts the simple rule, saying that all masks except the kavat are burned or discarded after the night dance, so a worldbuilder should decide per mask type what is destroyed, kept or sold instead of giving every mask one fate (NEW-ScoutMalagan-5, Commentary).

### NEW-ScoutMalagan-6
citation: Museum der Universität Tübingen MUT, Ethnologische Sammlung, object record AOI-Es-A921, "Maske für den 'Tatanua'-Tanz" (New Ireland, Papua New Guinea, around 1900).
url: https://www.emuseum.uni-tuebingen.de/objects/30944/maske-fur-den-tatanuatanz
route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, text/html, access date 2026-10-22
kind: record
tier: B — a museum label text for one object: authoritative for that object's record, thin for the ceremony.
scope: the double sense of malanggan in central and northern New Ireland, and the inter-clan request that brings a tatanua dance into a malanggan festival.
quotes:
- Description field: "sowohl die Schnitzwerke als auch die Totengedenkfeiern, für die sie hergestellt werden"
- Description field: "wenn die Festveranstalter einen anderen Clan, zu dem in der Regel eine verwandtschaftliche Beziehung besteht, um die Aufführung eines solchen Tanzes bitten"
teaches: malanggan names both the carvings and the mortuary feasts they are made for; a tatanua dance joins a festival when the hosts ask another clan, usually kin, to perform it, so performance is arranged between groups; the object itself is wood painted white, yellow, red and black with a yellow caterpillar-fibre crest, patterned cloth and snail-shell eyes.
draft sentences:
- In central and northern New Ireland malanggan names both the carvings and the mortuary feasts for which they are made, so the object and the ceremony share one term (NEW-ScoutMalagan-6, Description).
- A tatanua dance becomes part of a malanggan festival when the hosts ask another clan, usually one related by kinship, to perform it, so who dances is settled between groups (NEW-ScoutMalagan-6, Description).
- The museum's own tatanua is dated about 1900 and is painted white, yellow, red and black, with a yellow caterpillar-fibre crest, patterned cloth and snail-shell eyes (NEW-ScoutMalagan-6, object record AOI-Es-A921).
