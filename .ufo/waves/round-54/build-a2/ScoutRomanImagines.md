## What the evidence fixes, axis by axis

- **Who may make.** No classical text says who made the mask. Smith's entry reconstructs it: the *pollinctor*
  (the embalmer who prepared the body) probably took a mould from the dead man's face, the first cast went into
  the ground with the body, and a fresh cast from the same mould went to the atrium. Smith marks this as
  "probable supposition"; it is a reconstruction, not testimony. Design consequence: the mask maker is a
  death-trade specialist, not a sculptor, and the mould — not the mask — is the master object.
- **Who may wear.** Not the family: men chosen because they resemble the dead man in stature and carriage.
  Resemblance is a casting decision about the body. Rank is then added by costume: purple-bordered toga for
  consul or praetor, all purple for censor, gold-embroidered for a triumphator, with fasces and axes carried
  ahead and ivory chairs at the rostra.
- **What the mask is said to be.** A likeness worked to fidelity of features and complexion; in Greek a
  πρόσωπον, the word for a face (and, per LSJ, for the stage mask, for a bust or portrait, and for a person).
  Polybius's encomium is that the ancestors look "as if alive and breathing" — presence, but a presence produced
  by accurate portraiture, not by indwelling spirit.
- **Who may see.** The household always (the mask stands in the most conspicuous place in the house); the public
  on two occasions — public sacrifices, when the shrines are opened and decorated, and the funeral, when the
  masks come out in procession and sit at the rostra.
- **What it licenses.** The right to be represented at the funerals of one's descendants (*ius imaginum*),
  confined to holders of curule and higher office; Pliny shows the set also functioning as a pedigree diagram and
  as a claim that can be refused by the family (Messala and the Laevini) or forged by adoption (the Salvitti and
  the Scipios).
- **What is restricted.** Office, not wealth, admits a mask. The right can be cancelled after death: the masks of
  Brutus and Cassius were not permitted at their family's funerals. Access to the shrines is a door opened on
  stated occasions.
- **The voice asymmetry (the sharpest design point).** The masked ancestors do not speak. At the funeral the
  living orator recites the dead man's virtues and then the exploits of the ancestors whose images are present,
  beginning with the most ancient. The mask authorizes speech by another person; it does not ventriloquize.

## Integration notes for the L1

- **Rows 1 and 2 are one witness.** They are the same passage in translation and in Greek. The entry may cite both
  where the Greek word matters, but must not present them as two independent testimonies of Roman practice.
- **Calibration to write into the entry.** The imagines complex (wax portrait masks kept in the atrium, displayed at
  festivals, worn or carried at funerals) is mainstream in Roman social history. The details of mounting (worn on a
  face versus fitted to a bust) and of manufacture are reconstructions; Smith marks them so. Pliny writes about
  ancestral custom from the 1st century CE; Polybius writes in the 2nd century BCE about practice among the
  office-holding nobility. No claim of continuity into the Empire should rest on Polybius alone.
