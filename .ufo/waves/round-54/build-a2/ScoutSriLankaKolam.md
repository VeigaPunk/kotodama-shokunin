### NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-1

citation: Bailey, Mark S. and H. Janaka de Silva, 2006, "Sri Lankan sanni masks: an ancient classification of disease", *BMJ* 333(7582):1327–1328, doi 10.1136/bmj.39055.445417.BE, PMCID PMC1761180, PMID 17185730.
url: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1761180/
route: direct, HTTP 200 (PMC full text), 2026-10-22
kind: paper
tier: B — peer-reviewed review article; it synthesises published sources and two museum collections rather than reporting fieldwork, so it is authoritative for the state of the classification but not primary for any single ritual.
scope: the sanni demon roster and its variability, what the masks depict, how a Sanni Yakuma performance is ordered around the chief demon, and the distinction between kolam and tovil.
quotes:
- Abstract, ¶1: "Sri Lanka has a rich culture of theatre called kolam and exorcism called tovil, which make use of actors, exorcists, masks, music, and dance."
- Abstract, ¶1: "Occasionally the full complement of 18 possible disease demons is represented in the Daha Ata Sanniya (18 diseases) ritual"
- Abstract, ¶1: "The exorcism ends with the appearance of an exorcist wearing the mask of the chief demon called Maha Kola"
- Body, ¶ on figs 1–2: "the sanni demons do seem to represent disease syndromes, and their masks show clinical features that are familiar to clinicians today"
teaches: the mask is a diagnostic portrait of a condition, the set of conditions is conventionally 18 but actually open, and the chief mask closes the performance while containing all the others.
draft sentences:
- Separate the two institutions at the start: kolam is theatre, tovil is exorcism, and both use masks, actors, drumming and dance, so a world that merges them loses the line between entertainment and cure (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-1, Abstract, ¶1).
- Decide whether your mask set is a closed canon or an open list: this tradition fixes the number at 18 while more than thirty names circulate, and only the demons thought to be causing a given patient's illness are staged (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-1, Abstract ¶1 and body ¶ on variation).
- If the mask portraits a condition rather than a character, its features become readable symptoms — the vomiting demon's green face and protruding tongue, the pale face read as anaemia from hookworm (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-1, body ¶ on figs 1–2).
- Let the chief mask hold the whole taxonomy: the Maha Kola mask usually carries miniature representations of the other eighteen, and its wearer's appearance is the last event of the rite (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-1, Abstract, ¶1).

### NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-2

citation: Amarasinghe, Jayantha and Saman M. Kariyakarawana, 2014, "Immigrated National Identity: Karava and Sinhalese Mask Drama (Kolam)", *Journal of Social Review* 3(1):56–68, Department of Social Sciences, South Eastern University of Sri Lanka.
url: https://www.seu.ac.lk/researchandpublications/social_review_fac/V3N1/5%20Article%20pages%2056%20-%2068.pdf
route: direct, HTTP 200 (PDF, 406,652 bytes), 2026-10-22
kind: paper
tier: B — peer-reviewed journal article built on literature plus eight interviews; its origin thesis (a Kerala-derived kolam carried by Karava migrants) is offered as probable and is contested, so use it for the organisation of the craft, not for the migration story.
scope: kolam's definition and stock features, the division of carving and performance between Karava and Berava households around Ambalangoda and Bentota, a reported caste restriction on wearing one mask, and the variation in character counts between manuscript copies.
quotes:
- Introduction, p. 57: "Mask drama or Kôlam (mockery) is a kind of folk-drama solely native to maritime regions of south and west lowland"
- Introduction, p. 57: "performance of all men's and women's parts exclusively by men"
- p. 64, reporting the mask carver Norbert: "only people belonging to Oli caste was allowed to make performance wearing the mask of the (Demon) Garâyakâ."
- Abstract, p. 56: "those who belonged to Karava caste took part in singing and playing where as that of Berava practiced carving masks."
teaches: making and wearing are separate rights that can be held by different communities and later merge, cross-dressing is a standing rule of the form, and one mask carried a caste-specific right to wear it.
draft sentences:
- Split the right to make from the right to wear, then let the split erode: Karava households carved and Berava ritual dancers supplied verse, song and drumming, and the paper's own interviews find a Berava artist who both beats the drum and carves (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-2, Abstract p. 56 and p. 64).
- Give at least one mask a restricted wearer: a carver recalls that only people of the Oli caste were once allowed to perform in the Garayaka mask, and that many other castes do so now (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-2, p. 64).
- Make voice disguise part of the mask's job if you keep an all-male cast, since every male and female part is played by men (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-2, p. 57).
- Name the form after what it licenses: kôlam glosses as mockery, and its stock parts include a police constable, a Mudaliyar, a washerman and a king and queen, which is a licence to lampoon officials in masked dance (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-2, Introduction p. 57 and Notes p. 65).
- Fix the cast list as a recension, not a canon: documented kolam copies carry 7, 18, 19 and 51 characters, and the paper says the number varies from copy to copy (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-2, Notes, p. 65).

### NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-3

citation: Museum of Cultural History, University of Oslo, "From Ceylon to Norway: Sinhalese Ritual Dance Masks and Objects at the Museum of Cultural History", collection presentation, with contributions by Dumith Kulasekara and an interview with L. Bentharage (2025).
url: https://www.khm.uio.no/english/collections/srilanka/
route: direct, HTTP 200, 2026-10-22
kind: record
tier: A for the museum's own holdings, acquisition history and object identifications (the holding museum writing about its own collection); B for the ritual summaries, which rest partly on a single interviewed artist.
scope: which rituals the collected masks belong to, how Sanniyakuma and kolam are staged in time and place, the two dances in which a dancer removes the mask, the effects attributed to the Garā Yakā's presence, regional variation, and how the masks reached Europe.
quotes:
- Sanniyäkuma section, ¶ on Kola Sanniya: "there are only two masked dances in which the dancers reveal their actual face by removing the mask in front of the patient"
- Kōlam Theatre section, closing ¶: "The presence of Garā Yakā is believed to bring success, purification, and prosperity to the humans present."
- Sanniyäkuma section, ¶2: "Representations of these Yaǩku in masks do not happen in all regional traditions in the same way."
- Hagenbeck section, ¶1: "The presence of many of the earliest Sinhalese ritual dance masks in European collections originated from the business ventures of Carl Hagenbeck."
teaches: mask removal is a rare, scripted revelation rather than stage business; the masked figure is treated as a presence with effects on the room; no mask design is uniform across regions; and masks have a documented second life as commodities.
draft sentences:
- Decide whether a mask may ever come off: the museum states that of all Sinhalese masked dances only two end with the dancer removing the mask in front of the patient, Kola Sanniya and the Garā Yakā, so unmasking is the scripted climax and not a stage convention (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-3, Sanniyäkuma section).
- Treat the worn mask as a presence acting on the room: the Garā Yakā's presence is said to bring success, purification and prosperity to the humans present (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-3, Kōlam Theatre section).
- Refuse a single canonical mask design for a whole culture, because regional traditions do not represent the same demons in the same way (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-3, Sanniyäkuma section).
- Write the exit of the objects as a decision: most of the earliest Sinhalese ritual masks in European collections came through Carl Hagenbeck's commercial ventures, whose shows the same museum page names as "Ethnic Shows" and the "Human Zoo" (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-3, Hagenbeck section).
- Stage the two rites at the same hour and in opposite spaces: Sanniyakuma runs in a prepared open-air space at the patient's house until the next morning, and kolam runs from evening to morning as well, so the calendar, not the clock, separates them (NEW-ScoutSriLankaKolam-3, Sanniyäkuma and Kōlam Theatre sections).

## What each source is good for, and what it is not

- Good for: mask as diagnostic portrait of a condition (1); masks as lampoon of named officials with a fixed stock cast (2); who carves versus who wears, and one caste-bound wearing right (2); night-long performance at the patient's house, unmasking as climax, presence-language for the mask (3); object scale, weight and museum reclassification (4).
- Not good for: any statement that the Kerala origin story is established (paper 2 hedges it as "probable"; do not repeat it as fact); any claim that all sanni masks are worn by exorcists of one caste; any claim about a fixed roster of exactly eighteen conditions (paper 1 explicitly finds more than thirty names); any statement about women's present-day participation, which none of these sources settles.
