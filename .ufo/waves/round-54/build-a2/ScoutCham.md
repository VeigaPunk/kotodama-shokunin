Vol. 19 (June 2014), published on `ichcourier.unesco-ichcap.org` (the opened page's own

teaches: a masked form can be a community's bounded property with a named patron, an
unbroken transmission chain, and an access rule phrased as fortune rather than law.
draft sentences:
- A masked dance can be property before it is repertoire: the Drametse Ngacham stayed inside one community and one monastery's patronage until the late nineteenth century, so a worldbuilder must date the gate at which a masked form becomes public (NEW-ScoutCham-1, Introduction).
- Make the right to learn and to perform a threshold the tradition names out loud: the Drametse account holds that only the fortunate get the chance to learn the dance and perform it, which gives characters an access rule to aspire to and to resent rather than a licence office (NEW-ScoutCham-1, Introduction).
- Decide what the mask depicts and who is behind it: Drametse dancers wear masks of real and mythical animal faces, and the dance is registered as a meditative art form in which the dancer visualises the outer world as pure land, so the wearer is a practitioner executing a technique rather than an actor playing a part (NEW-ScoutCham-1, The Symbolic Meaning of the Dance).
- License the mask to teach: the dance is described as a didactic way of imparting esoteric teaching in dance form, so the masked figure may say in public what a priest would have to explain in private (NEW-ScoutCham-1, The Symbolic Meaning of the Dance).
- Give transmission a keeper and a break condition: the dance is said to come down an unbroken chain of lineage holders, so an interregnum is a plot event — nobody can legitimately dance the mask until the chain is repaired (NEW-ScoutCham-1, Conclusion).

### NEW-ScoutCham-2

citation: Mareike Wulff, 2019, "Putting the Festival Participants Back into the Festival:
Rethinking Communal Identity Formation in Buddhist Cham Festivals in Bhutan", Journal of
Bhutan Studies Vol. 40 (Summer 2019), pp. 9–61 (article at pp. 9–61, DOI none; open PDF).
url: https://d1i1jdw69xsqx0.cloudfront.net/digitalhimalaya/collections/journals/jbs/pdf/JBS_40_02.pdf
route: direct, HTTP 200, 465 972 bytes, `application/pdf`, no challenge (Digital Himalaya
copy of the journal issue).
kind: paper (research article reporting the author's own long-term fieldwork).
tier: A for the Korphu Drub observations, which are the author's own participant observation
in Korphu, Trongsa District, 2011–2019 (stated in her note 2, p. 11) — primary for what she
recorded there; B for any generalisation to cham elsewhere in Bhutan, which the article
itself does not claim.
scope: who performs which masked role and how that changes with age and gender at one
Bhutanese cham festival; what a mask is held to be (kuten, a body emanation); how roles are
cast and masks maintained; the festival positions that hold the dance. Not evidence for
Drametse practice, not evidence for a formal prohibition on women dancing, not evidence for
mask materials or prices.
quotes:
- p. 11, n. 3: "They are performed by monks or lay men as parts of complex Buddhist festivals carried out annually at important dates of the liturgical calendar."
- p. 12, n. 3: "The masks itself are considered to be kuten (sku rten), ‘body emanations’ of the deities they depict."
- p. 33 (subsection "Becoming visible. Entering the stage"): "Shielded by the small masks in front of their faces, they contest with each other trying to attract the attention of the audience."
- p. 41 (subsection "Mastery. Passing on knowledge"): "champon Sonam had inspected all masks and costumes, making a list of the pieces which had to be repaired"
- p. 41 (same subsection): "Only he has the honour to wear and perform the most respected and venerated mask of the community, the Rakhsha Lango"
- p. 41 (same subsection): "the champon assigns participation in the different dances according to the abilities of the individual boys and their participation during the last years’ festivals."
teaches: the mask is an indwelt body with a ranked owner, a maintenance officer and an
apprentice grade — presence, office and upkeep are three separate rules.
draft sentences:
- Decide whether the mask is a costume or a body: cham masks are held to be kuten (sku rten), body emanations of the deity depicted, so a worn mask is a claim of presence and a broken mask is a broken presence rather than damaged property (NEW-ScoutCham-2, p. 12, n. 3).
- Split the masked body between two institutions: cham is danced by monks or by lay men at fixed dates of the liturgical calendar, so the calendar owns the mask on those days and someone else keeps it the rest of the year (NEW-ScoutCham-2, p. 11, n. 3).
- Rank the masks instead of issuing them equally: at Korphu only the dance master wears the community's most venerated mask, the ox-headed Rakhsha Lango, so one mask is an office that a career must reach (NEW-ScoutCham-2, p. 41).
- Cast by demonstrated skill and past service, not by birth alone: the dance master assigns each dance by a boy's ability and by his record at previous festivals, which puts a merit ladder inside an inherited repertoire (NEW-ScoutCham-2, p. 41).
- Stage the first wearing as a small mask: boys enter as junior jesters shielded by small masks in front of their faces, clowning with the audience before they are trusted with a deity's mask, so masking has apprentice grades (NEW-ScoutCham-2, p. 33).
- Make upkeep an authorised act: the dance master inspects every mask and costume before rehearsal and lists what must be repaired or replaced, so repair is an office, not a hobby (NEW-ScoutCham-2, p. 41).

### NEW-ScoutCham-3 (optional third row — funerary register)

citation: Françoise Pommaret, 2006, "Dances in Bhutan: A Traditional Medium of Information",
Journal of Bhutan Studies Vol. 14 (Summer 2006), pp. 26–35, Centre for Bhutan Studies,
Thimphu.
url: https://bhutanstudies.org.bt/wp-content/uploads/2026/06/JBSVOL14.pdf
route: direct, HTTP 200, 1 342 976 bytes, `application/pdf`, no challenge (whole-issue PDF
from the publisher's own site; the article is pp. 26–35 of the file, page numbers in the PDF
body match the printed ones).
kind: paper (scholarly essay/note, no peer review stated).
tier: B — a scholarly synthesis by the leading academic specialist on Bhutanese dance, in a
research-centre journal, but a short essay without new fieldwork and without stated peer
review.
scope: the classification of Bhutanese cham into subjugation, victory and didactic dances;
the Drametse history and the see-it-once belief; the funerary Raksha mar [mang] cham as an
enactment of the Bardo thos grol; the outsider's misreading of the licensed fool. The word
"mask" appears in this article only in its bibliography — it carries **no** claims about
masks as objects, so do not cite it for mask material, mask ownership or mask handling.
quotes:
- p. 27: "Bhutanese religious dances, ‘cham’, can broadly fall into three categories: the subjugation dances, the victory dances and the didactic moral dances."
- p. 29: "Bhutanese believe that a person has to see the Drametse Ngacham of Drametse at least once in one’s life time"
- p. 29: "In the dance of the Raksha mar [mang] cham, the main performers represent the deities who appear after death."
- p. 33: "for two centuries, it did not go out of the confines of the Drametse Thegchog Ogyen Namdrol Choeling monastery courtyard."
- p. 33: "foreigners the atsara are just clowns and their religious function is not apparent."
teaches: genre fixes what a mask is licensed to do, and the audience can hold a duty that
pays off only at death.
draft sentences:
- Classify masked dances by what they are licensed to do: Bhutanese cham falls into subjugation dances, victory dances and didactic moral dances, so a mask's licence follows its genre rather than its wearer (NEW-ScoutCham-3, p. 27).
- Give the audience a duty with a deferred payoff: seeing the Drametse Ngacham once in a lifetime is held to let a person recognise the deities in the bardo, so attendance is an obligation rather than entertainment (NEW-ScoutCham-3, p. 29).
- Put the death-god mask in front of mourners who cannot read the text: the Raksha mar [mang] cham shows the deities who appear after death, which is why the masked figure, not the manuscript, is the teaching (NEW-ScoutCham-3, p. 29).
- Give the origin claim to the tradition's own authorities: the dance is said to have been taught in the sixteenth century after its founder's vision of Guru Rinpoche's palace at Drametse, so the mask's warrant is a vision report, not a charter (NEW-ScoutCham-3, p. 33).
- Decide which part of the licence a visitor can read: for foreigners the atsara are just clowns and their religious function is not apparent, so the same masked figure can be sacred to insiders and comic to guests (NEW-ScoutCham-3, p. 33).

## Cautions for the L1 (things these rows do NOT license)

1. **The 2008 inscription is not sourced here.** The opened ICHC page carries the November
   2005 Third Proclamation only. If the entry states 2008 (Representative List inscription),
   it needs a new row; otherwise keep it to 2005 and mark the second date `(to-verify)`.
2. **The founder's name conflicts between two opened pages.** ICHC: "Khedup Kuenga Wangpo,
   son of Terton Pema Lingpa". Pommaret p. 33: "Khedup Kuenga Gyeltshen, a descendant of the
   treasure discoverer Padma gling pa". Do not harmonise them; either give both or stay at
   "a descendant of Pema Lingpa taught it in the sixteenth century" and mark the name
   contested.
3. **Do not write "women are forbidden to dance cham".** Wulff supports only that cham is
   performed by monks or lay men (p. 11 n. 3) and that at Korphu boys and men are the mask
   dancers while girls and women hold the singing-leader (sedpem), household-head (maili
   ama) and organiser's-assistant (dodampa) positions (pp. 24–25, 36, 51). Her paper states
   no prohibition.
4. **Wulff is one festival.** Korphu Drub in Trongsa District, Khengkha-speaking community;
   do not present its age grades as pan-Bhutanese.
5. **Pommaret 2006 says nothing about masks as objects** ("mask" occurs only in its
   bibliography). Use it for genre, history, the bardo belief and outsider misreading only.
6. **No cost, material or carving claims** are available from these rows. Mask carving,
   wood, consecration rites and who may sell a mask are unopened questions; if the entry
   needs them, mark `(to-verify)` or route to another seat.
7. Tier caution: row 1 is magazine-published (tier B) even though its author directs the
   institute that documented the dance; row 2's tier A is scoped to Korphu fieldwork only.
