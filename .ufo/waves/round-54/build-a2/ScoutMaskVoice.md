# ScoutMaskVoice — the masker's voice (claim C, language coupling)

Seat: KotodamaR54a2.ScoutMaskVoice. Assigned unknown: how a masked speaker sounds, who
speaks through the mask, who translates it, and what the mask's speech is not allowed to be.
Three sources opened this session by plain request (all HTTP 200, access date 2026-10-22); two
are peer-reviewed open-access articles, one is an author's circulation draft read from a public
document mirror (abstract and section 1 only; its download endpoint presented a human check that
was met and not passed). No headless browser, captcha, login or paywall was used.

The material supports the entry's voice coupling at four decision points: the device (mirliton /
vibrating membrane, hidden in the hand or behind the mask), the register (buzzy, nasal, shrill,
lisping, "another tongue"), the access rule (who may see the headpiece versus who may only hear
it), and the office of interpreter (an unmasked speaker who renders the mask's utterance). It does
**not** contain an opened source stating a rule in the form "the masker must not speak in his own
voice": the Tarok and Kòmò material shows the practice (disguised voice plus translation or
initiatory framing), not a quoted prohibition. Flagged under gaps.

## NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1

citation: Potter, Cher 2020, "West African Voice Disguisers and Audible Ghosts: a case for expanding the fluency of global design history", Design and Culture 12(3): 309–329, doi 10.1080/17547075.2020.1741912. Author's accepted manuscript, CC BY-NC-ND, UAL Research Online eprint 14774.
url: https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/14774/1/UAL%20Research_06.08.19_D%26C_BokiVoiceDisguiser.pdf
route: direct plain GET, HTTP 200, application/pdf, body read through the conclusion and references this session (the last figure-list lines were not re-read); repository record https://ualresearchonline.arts.ac.uk/id/eprint/14774/ (HTTP 200) supplied the citation and refereed status; access date 2026-10-22.
kind: paper
tier: A for the article's own argument and its handling of the Boki object (peer-reviewed, open repository copy of the accepted manuscript); the ethnographic reports it quotes (Lifschitz 1988, Isichei 1988, Balfour 1948, Lo-Bamijoko 1987, Peek 1994) are second hand in this row.
scope: the Boki voice-disguiser (Pitt Rivers Museum 1931.46.1, bone tube with spider-silk membrane, south-eastern Nigeria, c. 1925) as an object that produces a spirit presence by sound; the article's "acoustic mask" framing; the gendered and concentric organisation of the soundscape; Lo-Bamijoko's Igbo context-based classification of the same device. Not a general theory of African masking, and not a statement about Boki belief beyond what the article reports.
quotes:
- §"The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?", paragraph beginning "It is clear that the primary source of the sound": "the human speaks and a spirit is heard"
- §"The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?", paragraph beginning "While many spirits are perceived", quoting Peek 1994: 487: "a bombilating base-line pierced with irregular bleats in shrill pitches and the lisping vocal articulations characteristic of the voice-disguiser"
- §"The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?", paragraph beginning "The texture of this acoustic presence": "the soundscape was gendered concentrically and served to reinforce secret bodies of knowledge and the patriarchal structure of a community"
- §"The Boki voice-disguiser – an object of design?", paragraph on Lo-Bamijoko's Igbo classification: "they are at once musical instruments, social tools and metaphysical manifestations"
teaches: a hand-sized membrane tube lets one speaker sound like a spirit, and the same device is named by the social occasion it serves (summoning gods, ancestral dance, theatre) rather than by its shape.
draft sentences:
- Decide whether the mask is a face or a voice: the Pitt Rivers Boki voice-disguiser is a 5.6 cm bone tube capped with a spider-silk membrane, and the article's formulation of what it does is that "the human speaks and a spirit is heard" (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1, §The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?).
- Give the spirit a timbre the audience can name: presence is recognised by "a bombilating base-line pierced with irregular bleats in shrill pitches" plus lisping articulation, so the mask's sound is a describable acoustic signature rather than a generic boom (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1, §The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?, quoting Peek 1994: 487).
- Decide who may hear what, and from where: in the Boki account "the soundscape was gendered concentrically", with women kept to the outskirts during a spirit visitation, so audibility itself carries rank and sex (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1, §The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?).
- Name the instrument by occasion, not by shape: Igbo classification distinguishes the same membrane device as igba-alusi for summoning gods, igba-mmonwu for ancestral dance and igba-egwu for theatre, because "they are at once musical instruments, social tools and metaphysical manifestations" (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1, §The Boki voice-disguiser – an object of design?).
- Make the voice a jurisdiction: in the night law-making (kykware) that Potter reports from Lifschitz, men spoke disguisers in sequence around a village so that non-initiates heard one spirit flying over and around it, which made the court's authority an acoustic effect before it was a judgment (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1, §The Boki voice-disguiser – a metaphysical tool?).
- Keep the source of the voice invisible: the tube is held inside wrapped fingers and in public performance is hidden behind the mask, so design the audience to see a face and hear a voice with no visible origin (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-1, §The Boki voice-disguiser – an object of design?; §The Boki voice-disguiser – a metaphysical tool?).
unopened for this row: the Pitt Rivers Museum's own catalogue record for 1931.46.1 was not located this session; object data here is Potter's, not the holding museum's record.

## NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2

citation: Driver, Merlyn 2017, "The Buzz Aesthetic and Mande Music: Acoustic Masks and the Technology of Enchantment", African Music: Journal of the International Library of African Music 10(3): 95–117, doi 10.21504/amj.v10i3.2198.
url: https://journal.ru.ac.za/index.php/africanmusic/article/download/2198/1183/1251
route: direct plain GET, HTTP 200, application/pdf, read through the conclusion and references this session (about ten lines of figure captions between printed pages 15 and 17 were not re-read); journal record https://journal.ru.ac.za/index.php/africanmusic/article/view/2198 (HTTP 200) supplied volume, issue, pages and DOI; open access; access date 2026-10-22.
kind: paper
tier: A for the article's own review statements about mirliton voice disguisers and the buzz aesthetic (peer-reviewed, open-access journal); the Kòmò description is Driver quoting Jespers 1995: 37–56 (second hand in this row), and the Balfour 1948 and Lifschitz 1988 reports it relays are also second hand.
scope: vibrating-membrane voice disguisers as the most widespread device for spirit voice in sub-Saharan Africa; the Kòmò (Minyanka, Mali) warada kazoo, its material, its register, the ban on non-initiates seeing the headpieces, the after-sunset season, and the distributed roar of the jarawara; the article's argument that buzzy timbre is tied to spirit presence. Not a survey of every Mande mask, and not a claim about Minyanka belief beyond what Driver reports from Jespers.
quotes:
- §"The buzz aesthetic in Africa – possible explanations", paragraph beginning "Possibly the oldest method": "Possibly the oldest method of establishing a spirit presence via sound is through the distortion or disguise of the human voice."
- §"The buzz aesthetic in Africa – possible explanations", next paragraph: "The most widespread devices used for disguising the human voice for spiritual purposes in sub-Saharan Africa are those that employ a vibrating membrane."
- §"Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic", paragraph on jow masks: "non-initiates (normally women and children) who are forbidden from seeing the headpieces"
- §"Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic", paragraph on the waraw, quoting Jespers 1995: 49: "Shrill little nasal voices [using 'kazoo'-like voice disguisers] announce that the 'catamounts' have arrived at the gates of the village"
teaches: the kazoo-like membrane is the standard technology of the acoustic mask, and Kòmò practice wraps one disguised voice in an access rule, a night licence and a register meant to sound like another tongue.
draft sentences:
- Treat the vibrating membrane as the default technology: "the most widespread devices used for disguising the human voice for spiritual purposes in sub-Saharan Africa" are mirlitons, so a mask culture needs no electronics to sound non-human (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2, §The buzz aesthetic in Africa – possible explanations).
- Let the mask's substance be its voice: in the Kòmò association the warada, "mouth of catamount", is a reed closed at both ends with spider-egg membrane, and Jespers describes the headpieces as having a substance that is "first of all a voice" (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2, §Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic, quoting Jespers 1995: 45, 51).
- Separate seeing from hearing by rule: Kòmò headpieces may not be seen by "non-initiates (normally women and children)", so for most of the audience the spirit exists only as sound (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2, §Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic).
- Make the register unmistakably other: the warada produces "shrill little nasal voices" at the village gates, and the transformation makes a young initiate think he hears another tongue (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2, §Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic, quoting Jespers 1995: 49, 51).
- Distribute one voice across many bodies: the jarawara roar is made by several friction drums carried by the mask bearers, producing the perception of a single omnipresent voice, which is a design for sounding like one spirit while many men work (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2, §Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic).
- Set the hour and the season: the Kòmò masks come out after sunset in a seasonal "coming out", so the acoustic mask's licence belongs to darkness and the calendar, not to the object alone (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-2, §Sources of power within the Mande buzz aesthetic).

## NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3

citation: Blench, Roger 2006, "The coded language of the orìm, the ancestral spirits of the Tarok of Central Nigeria", paper for the conference "Spirit, Languages, Silence and Secrecy" (Cologne, 2–3 December 2005), circulation draft revised post-conference, dated 4 January 2006.
url: https://docslib.org/doc/3708411/the-coded-language-of-the-orim-the-ancestral-spirits-of-the-tarok-of-central-nigeria
route: plain GET, HTTP 200, public copy on docslib.org (a document mirror reproducing the author's draft); access date 2026-10-22. The mirror's "Download full-text PDF" endpoint presents a human-verification check: challenge met, not passed. Only the title page, abstract and section 1 rendered on the opened page; nothing past section 1 was read.
kind: web (unpublished conference draft)
tier: B — the author's own report of Tarok orìm practice (primary for what Blench reports), but an unpublished, non-peer-reviewed circulation draft read from a third-party mirror, and only its abstract and section 1 were opened. The Tarok lexical data and the paper's wider comparative claims were not read and are not scoped here.
scope: who represents the orìm (initiated males, post-menopausal women), that orìm are mostly heard and only sometimes seen as masked figures, the licensed occasions (disciplining "stubborn" women, prophecy), the register (voice disguisers plus code words inside normal Tarok syntax), and the unmasked interpreter. Not evidence for any specific code word, and not a general model of Nigerian masking.
quotes:
- Abstract: "The ancestors, orìm, are represented by initiated males and post-menopausal women."
- Abstract: "Orìm are mostly heard, but emerge as masked figures under some circumstances, especially for the disciplining of 'stubborn' women and for making prophecies."
- Abstract: "speak through voice disguisers in a language dotted with code words although framed in normal Tarok syntax"
- Abstract: "their utterances are interpreted by unmasked figures"
teaches: a spirit register can be ordinary grammar with a substituted lexicon, spoken through a disguiser and rendered into public speech by an unmasked interpreter.
draft sentences:
- Decide whether the spirit is normally heard and only rarely seen: Blench reports that Tarok orìm "are mostly heard, but emerge as masked figures under some circumstances", which makes the voice the default channel and the mask the exception (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3, Abstract).
- Tie the right to represent the ancestors to initiation and age, not simply to sex: orìm "are represented by initiated males and post-menopausal women" (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3, Abstract).
- Build the spirit register as code inside ordinary grammar: they "speak through voice disguisers in a language dotted with code words although framed in normal Tarok syntax", so the audience hears its own language with a substituted lexicon (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3, Abstract).
- Give the interpretation an office: "their utterances are interpreted by unmasked figures", so decide who may stand between the mask and the public, and what that mediator is owed (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3, Abstract).
- Choose the mechanism deliberately: Blench separates substitution languages, where normal syntax carries replaced key words, from replacement languages and surrogate tone-speech, so a fictional spirit language can pick one device instead of inventing a whole tongue (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3, §1).
- Keep the licence narrow and political: the masks emerge "especially for the disciplining of 'stubborn' women and for making prophecies", a policing and prophecy brief rather than general entertainment (NEW-ScoutMaskVoice-3, Abstract).

## Use in the entry (for the L1)

- Decision table rows this supports: device (mirliton / membrane tube, hidden); register (buzzy,
  nasal, shrill, lisping; "another tongue"); who may see (initiates; non-initiates hear only);
  who may speak (initiated men, post-menopausal women in the Tarok case); who translates (an
  unmasked interpreter); when (night, seasonal coming-out); what it licenses (law, prophecy,
  discipline, entertainment); what it costs/restricts (sight of the headpiece, ordinary voice).
- The strongest single formulation for the entry's thesis is Potter's "the human speaks and a
  spirit is heard", with Driver's Kòmò case as the access-rule illustration and Blench's Tarok
  case as the interpreter-and-code illustration.
- Do not generalise the mirliton to all masking. Driver says it is the most widespread device
  for voice disguise in sub-Saharan Africa; neither source says every mask uses one, and the
  jarawara roar in the same article is made by friction drums, not a membrane.
- Do not describe restricted objects beyond what the communities' published statements allow.
  The Tarok and Kòmò material here is published ethnographic description; the entry should say
  that the headpiece is barred from non-initiates' sight, not inventory what it contains.
