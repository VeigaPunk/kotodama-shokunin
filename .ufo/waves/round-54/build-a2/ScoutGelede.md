draft sentences:
- Decide when a community stages its masquerade: UNESCO lists every year after the harvests, at important events, and during drought or epidemics, with mask carving and costume making counted among the preparatory craftwork (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, second paragraph, sentences 1 and 5).
- Decide whether the masked figure enters a space already made public: the ceremony "usually takes place at night on a public square", the dancers prepare in a nearby house, and singers and drummers appear before the masked dancers (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, second paragraph, sentences 2-3).
- Decide who holds authority over the masquerade: UNESCO describes the community as divided into groups of men and women led by a male and a female head, and calls Gelede "the only known masked society, which is also governed by women" (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, second paragraph, sentences 8-9) — a superlative that is UNESCO's own and must be marked as such.
- Decide what a mask depicts: animal figures are often used, the serpent as "a symbol of power" and the bird as "the messenger of the “mothers”", so two of the motifs are given stated meanings on the record (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, second paragraph, sentence 7).
- Decide what the mask licenses: the verses are sung in Yoruba and "employ a good deal of irony and mockery, supported by satirical masks" (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, second paragraph, sentence 6).
- Decide how far the record may be trusted on origins: UNESCO calls the dances "a testimony of the former matriarchal order" while also saying Gelede "has nowadays adapted to a more patriarchal society" — a dated, contested framing to be quoted as UNESCO's, never adopted as fact (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, second paragraph, sentence 10).

### NEW-ScoutGelede-2

citation: Drewal, Henry John (compiler, translator, annotator) 2008, "Homage to Thomas Moulero: Private Historian of Gèlèdé", The University of African Art Press, Ile Ife and Denver, Colorado; contains an annotated transcription of Moulero's French/Yoruba manuscript "Le Guelede" (written ca. 1969–70) and the Drewals' interview transcript of 15 May 1971, Porto-Novo.

url: https://universityofafricanart.org/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/Drewal-Moulero-on-University-of-African-Art.pdf

route: direct plain request 2026-10-22, HTTP 200, `application/pdf`, 873781 bytes, 35 pages; read with `pdftotext -layout`. Page locators below are the PDF's own pages; Moulero's manuscript page numbers (p.1-p.18 of his text) are given where the passage sits inside his manuscript.

kind: book

tier: A — for what Thomas Moulero wrote and said as this edition prints it: a Ketu-born Catholic priest and historian's own account, transcribed and translated by the scholar who interviewed him. Scoped tightly: the English of the interview is the interviewer's translation, the testimony is one man's, and it was recorded in 1971.

scope: one insider account of Ketu / Porto-Novo Gelede — who the honoured mothers are said to be, what the great mask is said to be, sanctions said to attach to a mask's removal, viewing as a granted exception, and the anonymous licensed voice. Not a survey of all Yoruba-Nago communities, not current practice, and not a community's collective statement.

quotes:
- interview transcript, PDF p.5 (answer to the question about Ulli Beier): "they can prevent the mask from being removed after the festival."
- interview transcript, PDF p.7: "The orisa are gods, but these prayers are addressed to living persons."
- interview transcript, PDF p.8 (on the Obatala mask at Odogbo): "It was only for me that they brought it out so it could be photographed."
- manuscript p.5, printed at PDF p.18: "L’enorme masque qui prelude la danse du soir est la personnification de la nature creatrice" (the text layer prints this sentence without the accents standard French would carry; none added here).

teaches: Gelede as a stage play compared by its author to the Roman theatre and to Noh; the honoured "mothers" (iya wa, aje) distinguished from the orisa as living persons; the great mask that opens the evening dance as a personification of creative nature; the mothers' reported power to prevent a mask from being removed after the festival and to stop a singer mid-song; viewing of a mask as something granted for a purpose; dancers who perform without knowing the deeper meaning; and, quoting Cambell Flavier, Èfè as the anonymous figure who mocks wrongdoing and praises the deserving without being stung in return.

draft sentences:
- Decide whether the honoured "mothers" are deities or living people: Moulero separates them from the gods — "The orisa are gods, but these prayers are addressed to living persons" (NEW-ScoutGelede-2, interview transcript, PDF p.7) — while UNESCO's record frames the same ceremony as tribute to the primordial mother Iyà Nlà, so the entry should carry the disagreement rather than resolve it (NEW-ScoutGelede-1, Description, first paragraph).
- Decide what a mask is said to be: the enormous mask that opens the evening dance is "la personnification de la nature creatrice", a personification of creative nature (NEW-ScoutGelede-2, manuscript p.5, PDF p.18).
- Decide who controls the mask after the performance: Moulero reports that the mothers "can prevent the mask from being removed after the festival", and that they can stop a singer from continuing, so removing the mask is something the mothers are said to be able to halt (NEW-ScoutGelede-2, interview transcript, PDF p.5).
- Decide what counts as permission to see a mask: Moulero saw an Obatala mask at Odogbo only because "they brought it out" for him to photograph, at night, and he could not describe the other masks he had seen (NEW-ScoutGelede-2, interview transcript, PDF p.8).
- Decide what anonymity buys: Moulero, quoting Cambell Flavier, calls Èfè "le personnage anonyme qui raille les mauvais actes", the anonymous figure who, in the same passage, mocks bad acts, praises the deserving and is not the victim of criticism's venomous stings (NEW-ScoutGelede-2, manuscript p.7, PDF p.19).
- Decide who may speak through the mask: Moulero says there are dancers who know how to dance but not the deeper meaning, and that "There aren’t many who know the real meaning" (NEW-ScoutGelede-2, interview transcript, PDF p.5).

## Cautions for integration

- Two UNESCO sentences must be handled as UNESCO's own claims, not as findings
  (criterion 2): "the only known masked society, which is also governed by women"
  (a superlative no cited study backs on the page) and "a testimony of the former
  matriarchal order" (a dated, contested frame; UNESCO itself says the practice has
  adapted to a more patriarchal society).
- Moulero's testimony is 1969–71, printed 2008. Date it in the entry; do not write it
  in the ethnographic present, and do not generalize it from Ketu/Porto-Novo to all
  Yoruba-Nago communities.
- The two sources disagree on who is honoured (primordial mother Iyà Nlà versus living
  women called iya wa). Keep both attributed; the disagreement is itself usable.
- The Èfè line is French in the manuscript, in the same text layer that prints
  "addresse" and "pards" for what is probably "dards"; quote it only as printed, or
  paraphrase with the locator.
- Do not describe restricted or sacred procedure beyond these public statements; the
  preparation house is named, not described, and neither page gives masking ritual.
