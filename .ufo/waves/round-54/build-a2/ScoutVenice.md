### NEW-ScoutVenice-1

citation: Neveu, Marc J. 2010. "The Space of the Mask: From Stage to Ridotto." In
*Architectural Space in Eighteenth-Century Europe: Constructing Identities and
Interiors*, 149–163 in the repository PDF (the landing page gives 149–168).
California Polytechnic State University, Architecture faculty research repository,
item arch_fac/60.

url: https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/arch_fac/60 (landing page) and
https://digitalcommons.calpoly.edu/cgi/viewcontent.cgi?article=1061&context=arch_fac
(PDF).

route: landing page direct, HTTP 200. PDF endpoint direct, HTTP 403 (bot
interstitial: challenge met, not passed). PDF opened from the Wayback capture
`20230523044652if_` of that endpoint; citation metadata opened from the Wayback
capture `20240603102111id_` of the landing page. Access date 2026-10-22.

kind: paper (chapter in an edited scholarly volume).

tier: A — the author's own published chapter, repository copy, authoritative for the
chapter's documented statements; B for the 1732 law's archival warrant, since note 25
traces it to legislation lists (Reato; Barricelli) and not to a decree text.

scope: the chapter's own account of eighteenth-century Venetian masking — masks as a
condition of entry into ridotti and theaters, the bauta and moretta as social rather
than character masks, the Magistrato alle Pompe, the 1732 theatre-mask law, the
ridotto chronology to 1774. It is not a transcription of any decree and gives no
archival shelfmark for the 1732 law.

quotes:
- p. 149, chapter opening paragraph: "During the carnival season, masking was regulated and required for entry into interior public spaces such as ridotti (gaming halls) and theaters."
- p. 155, section "Masking the Theater of the City": "a law from 1732 required patrons to mask their faces and their clothing when attending the theater, specifically with the bauta e tabarro"
- p. 155, section "Masking the Theater of the City": "The white mask allowed the wearer to speak freely while concealing their facial features."
- p. 155, section "Masking the Theater of the City": "This had an interesting effect: the wearer was unable to speak."

teaches: a Renaissance-to-Enlightenment city can make the mask an entry requirement,
a compelled dress code, an alibi with no character behind it, and a pair of opposite
voice rules attached to two mask shapes.

draft sentences:
1. Make the mask a door policy rather than a costume: during carnival, masking was
   "regulated and required for entry into interior public spaces such as ridotti
   (gaming halls) and theaters" (NEW-ScoutVenice-1, p. 149).
2. Decide whether the mask confers a character: the bauta and the moretta belonged to
   no commedia stock character and gave the wearer no alternate identity to play — the
   mask marked an anonymous equal, not a named persona (NEW-ScoutVenice-1, p. 155).
3. Give each mask type its own voice rule: the bauta's white half-mask "allowed the
   wearer to speak freely while concealing their facial features," while the moretta,
   a smaller round mask secured by a button the wearer held between the teeth, left the
   wearer unable to speak (NEW-ScoutVenice-1, p. 155).
4. Make compulsion part of the law, not only prohibition: "a law from 1732 required
   patrons to mask their faces and their clothing when attending the theater,
   specifically with the bauta e tabarro" (NEW-ScoutVenice-1, p. 155).
5. Write an exemption list: in the ridotto only the staff who handled the house money
   went unmasked, and they were forbidden to wear one (NEW-ScoutVenice-1, p. 152).
6. Date the institution and let a decree end it: the first legally operated ridotto
   room opened in the San Moisè area in 1638, at least 118 operated by 1744, and in
   1774 a ban on games of chance closed them all (NEW-ScoutVenice-1, p. 151).

### NEW-ScoutVenice-2

citation: Fondazione Musei Civici di Venezia. "Forma per maschera Bauta." MUVE
collections record, Casa di Carlo Goldoni, inv. Cl XI n° 1421; object eighteenth
century, bronze, 14.5 × 16 cm; no author or publication date on the page.

url: https://www.visitmuve.it/capolavoro/forma-per-maschera-bauta/

route: direct plain request, HTTP 200, full record read. Access date 2026-10-22.

kind: record (holding museum's own collection record).

tier: A — the museum that holds the object publishes the record; authoritative for the
object's fields and for the museum's own published description, not for legal history.

scope: one bronze mask form (a mould, not a finished mask) and the museum's
description of the bauta's permitted occasions, face coverage, voice deformation and
handling. The record gives no decree and no date for any mask law.

quotes:
- record description, sentence 2: "Tra tutte le maschere di Venezia la bauta era quella che aveva il maggior permesso di vagare per calli e campi"
- record description, sentence 2, final clause: "quando le altre maschere erano bandite"
- record description, sentence 4: "il labbro superiore deformato ed allungato in modo da deformare anche la voce di chi la indossa"
- record description, sentence 7: "Questa conformazione diventava una cassa armonica che rendeva chiusa e contratta la voce deformandone il timbro."

teaches: what a bauta is as a made object — a form that covers three-quarters of the
face, keeps the chin visible, deforms the voice on purpose, and carries a wider
calendar of permitted occasions than other masks.

draft sentences:
1. Rank mask types by licence, not by shape: the museum says the bauta had the
   greatest permission of all Venetian masks to move through streets and squares,
   including on the days of Saint Mark and the Ascension and for the election of doges
   and procurators, when other masks were banned (NEW-ScoutVenice-2, record
   description, sentence 2).
2. Let the mask, not the person, be the addressee: the record says etiquette owed
   respect or greeting to every mask precisely because the wearer could not be known,
   whether grandee or commoner (NEW-ScoutVenice-2, record description, sentence 3).
3. Build the voice into the object: the bauta's upper lip is deformed and lengthened
   so that it also deforms the wearer's voice, so they are not recognised
   (NEW-ScoutVenice-2, record description, sentence 4).
4. Treat the mask as an instrument: the museum describes the form as a resonance
   chamber that made the voice closed and contracted, altering its timbre
   (NEW-ScoutVenice-2, record description, sentence 7).
5. Decide what stays visible: the form covered three-quarters of the face, left the
   chin slightly visible, had two elliptical eye holes, raised cheekbones and a sloping
   ridge that starts under the nose and opens like a beak (NEW-ScoutVenice-2, record
   description, sentence 6).
6. The mould itself can be property: the museum holds an eighteenth-century bronze
   mask form, 14.5 by 16 cm, inventory Cl XI n° 1421, in the Casa di Carlo Goldoni —
   a world can give the form a maker, an owner and a provenance, not only the finished
   mask (NEW-ScoutVenice-2, record fields).
