draft sentences:
- In Greek the same noun does both jobs: πρόσωπον is glossed first as "face, countenance", and at the third sense the lexicon equates it with προσωπεῖον, the mask (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-1, sense III).
- Under that same mask sense the lexicon files the Roman ancestor images and portrait busts, so a mask that stands for a named dead person is a listed meaning of the word and not a metaphor a worldbuilder is inventing (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-1, sense III, end).
- The mask's dramatic sub-sense is "dramatic part, character", which lets a culture use one noun for the object worn and the role it carries, and makes "who is playing whom" a question the language does not have to mark (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-1, sense III.2).
- The fourth sense is the person, carried by the phrase "in person, in bodily presence" and by the idiom of receiving someone's face, which the lexicon glosses both as admitting a person to one's presence and as showing partiality (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-1, sense IV).
- A worldbuilder deciding whether the mask is a portrait, a presence or a type can point to a real lexicon in which one word does four jobs — face, worn mask, ancestor image, person — and must then decide whether the culture's own law treats those four as one thing (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-1, senses I–IV).

draft sentences:
- The Latin dictionary does not assert the sounding-through derivation: its etymology bracket reads "acc. to Gabius Bassus ap. Gell. 5, 7, 1 sq.", so the story enters Latin lexicography as a named ancient claim rather than as the dictionary's own finding (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-2, entry etymology bracket).
- The Latin mask covers the whole head and is varied according to the character, which makes a mask set a character list and puts the type on the object rather than on the wearer's face (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-2, sense I).
- The role is assumed and then owed: the entry illustrates the word with Seneca's "hanc personam induisti: agenda est", a mask that must be acted once it is put on, which is the sentence to build a "what the mask licenses and what it obliges" rule from (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-2, sense II.B, citing Sen. - Legal personality is one of the word's senses and the entry states its boundary in the same clause — a being having legal rights and obligations, not including slaves — so the vocabulary that supplies the mask also supplies a rule about who counts as a person; whether mask-wearing and personhood follow the same rule is a design decision, not something the dictionary settles (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-2, sense II.B.2 (γ)).

## Calibration and coupling (what the L1 can put on the registry/entry)

**Calibration row.** Status: *ancient, attributed, and not established*. L&S reports the derivation with "acc. to Gabius Bassus" (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-2); Gellius presents it as Bassus's argument and admires its cleverness (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-3); the modern study classifies it as ancient etymology (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-4). Therefore the entry may say "Gavius Bassus derived persōna from personāre, 'to sound through', and Gellius admired the argument" and must not say "persōna comes from personāre". The Etruscan *phersu* alternative is widely repeated but **was not opened this session** — if the entry mentions it, mark it `(to-verify)` or leave it out.

**Linguistic coupling.** Greek πρόσωπον runs face → worn mask → role/character → person; Latin persōna runs worn mask → role/character → person, and is not the Latin word for the face (NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-1, senses I–IV; NEW-ScoutPersonaProsopon-2, senses I–II.B). The two ranges should be presented as parallel, not as a loan: LSJ gives no etymology for πρόσωπον, and no opened source demonstrates a Greek-to-Latin path. For the semantic-drift mechanics the entry should point at `corpus/knowledge/linguistics/semantic-fields-lexicon-building.md` and `corpus/knowledge/linguistics/historical-linguistics.md` rather than restate them.
