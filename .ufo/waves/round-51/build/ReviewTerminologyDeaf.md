## 5. Framing errors to reject in the C entry

1. **Deficit framing.** Deaf characters staged as "afflicted", "struck dumb", "confined to silence", or deafness as something to be fixed before the plot can move. Reject: the community standard names identities, and "impaired" language establishes hearing as the standard and everything else as damaged (NEW-1). Write from the identity/linguistic-diversity framing (NEW-2, NEW-3). Province note: keep this as terminology guidance for writers — do not import clinical or rights-ruling content (implants, schooling mandates) into the entry.
2. **"Universal sign language."** One sign language understood across a world or continent without history. Reject: sign languages are country- and community-specific (NEW-2); village varieties even differ structurally from national Deaf-community languages (NEW-5, p.2). If a world needs a contact variety, model it as an International-Sign-like auxiliary contact system — `(to-verify)` unless the entry opens a source for it.
3. **"Sign = pantomime."** Signing staged as wordless picture-drawing that any onlooker can "just see". Reject: the pictorial-gesture view is the named stage-1 position the field abandoned (NEW-4, Abstract and Intro ¶2); sign languages have the same grammatical properties as spoken languages (NEW-3, Background ¶4). Concretely: a hearing character gets no free comprehension of an unrelated sign language, and iconicity does not equal transparency.
4. **"Deaf village idyll."** A shared signing community written as equality achieved — everyone signs, so everyone is included, power is flat, and the community is timeless. Reject: Kusters documents the romanticization and shows uneven signing proficiency, exclusion from top governance, unequal marriage rates, deaf-specific roles and subcommunities, and endangerment pressure from national-language schooling (NEW-5). Use "shared signing community" and name the tensions; the idyllic reading is literally the trope the paper dissects ("a utopia in the eyes of many Deaf people", Abstract).
5. **"Sign is quick and easy to pick up."** A hearing character acquires a sign language in days because "it's just gestures". Reject: "There is no evidence to back up the claim that sign languages are easier to learn than spoken languages" (NEW-3). Bonus value: this line doubles as the calibration flag for any fictional "instant fluency" trope.
