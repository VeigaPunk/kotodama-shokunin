## Verdicts

VERDICT P01 SUPPORTED — "One thing that makes this paper object unique within the Posen Library are the visible fold marks. The page is creased. These creases indicate that the person or people who used the amulet folded it up so that it was small enough to carry around or hold onto." The JWA blog page body ("What we found") carries the quote verbatim ("This elaborately detailed amulet from Kerala, Kochi was used to guard against both infertility and infant death."), carries the teaches, and carries every entry claim: the amulet guarded against both infertility and infant death, and the crease/fold-marks sentence directly supports the "Carried and folded" claim.

VERDICT P02 SUPPORTED — "Inscriptions identify the subject of this amulet as the Woman with the Issue of Blood (Mark 5:25–34; Luke 8:43–48)." The Wayback capture of the Met collection page carries the quote verbatim ("Hematite was believed to stop the flow of blood and was a favorite medium for amulets related to female reproductive health and menstrual problems."), carries the teaches, and carries the entry's single claim.

VERDICT P03 SUPPORTED — "These elements consist of its being an earthenware bowl that was inscribed with an incantation, adorned with an image, and buried upside-down." The Edinburgh JnJ page (Discussion of the source, "The image") carries the quote verbatim ("The amuletic object is a combination of elements, all of which are significant factors in making it potent."), carries the teaches, and carries every element of the entry (earthenware vessel, written incantation, image of a bound demoness, burial upside down).

VERDICT P04 NOT-SUPPORTED — (1) fails: the quoted passage is not verbatim on the page; both the live page and the Wayback capture read "inscribed with prayers and invocations for safe delivery", not "inscribed with firstly and invocations" ("firstly" occurs nowhere in the page). The teaches and the entry claims otherwise match the page text.

VERDICT P05 SUPPORTED — "The specific arrangement of texts and elements on the amulet or talisman is achieved by carving, modeling, or assembling the materials into symbolic shapes." The PMC article carries the quote verbatim, carries the teaches, and carries both entry claims including "while others can only be activated by reading the text written on them".

VERDICT P06 SUPPORTED — "The position of this panther mask on an exterior wall of the city near an important city gate is indeed precisely where one would expect to find such an apotropaic image." The Archaeopress sample PDF carries the quote verbatim ("A frontal face was also used as a protective amulet to ward off the plague."), carries the teaches, and carries both entry claims (frontal face as protective amulet against plague; "Eyes also appear as a common apotropaic device on ships").

VERDICT P07 NOT-SUPPORTED — (3) fails: the entry adds "the form was carried from Ming-dynasty Nanjing by returning envoys", and the thesis PDF carries no such claim — there is no occurrence of Ming, Nanjing, envoys or dynasty in the extracted text (the belief is instead traced to the Near East / Mesopotamia / Mediterranean basin). The quote and the teaches are present verbatim.

VERDICT P08 SUPPORTED — "Shinsatsu may be made of wood, paper, or metal and bear a written or printed inscription." The Kokugakuin Digital Museum page carries the quote verbatim, carries the teaches, and carries all three entry claims: distribution at shrines as a symbolic token of a spiritual being or its supranormal power; enshrined in kamidana or affixed above gates, on doors and pillars, or on ceilings; and distribution at shrines.
