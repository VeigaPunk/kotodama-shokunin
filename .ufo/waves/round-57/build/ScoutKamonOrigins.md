draft sentences:
- Japanese mons began, by the tradition McClatchie records, as patterns embroidered or woven into the state garments of the old court nobles at Kyōto, so a worldbuilder's marks can start as non-hereditary textile decoration before any family law fixes them (NEW-ScoutKamonOrigins-1, page 4).
- The embroidered patterns were generally circular, which is why nearly all Japanese heraldic devices are circular in shape — a convention with a remembered cause, not a random design preference (NEW-ScoutKamonOrigins-1, page 4).
- Japan had no shield and thus no coat of arms proper; the only heraldic insignia were badges and crests borne on the helmet or breastplate, so a world without shields still has room for crests, just not for a blazoned "arms" system (NEW-ScoutKamonOrigins-1, page 2).
- Badges were at first assumed at will by anyone choosing a distinguishing mark for himself and his family, and only later do we find badges conferred by a chieftain on retainers who had distinguished themselves in battle — grant by a lord is an innovation, not the origin (NEW-ScoutKamonOrigins-1, page 5).
- Members of the same family could wear different badges, and differencing was done by modifying or adding to a device to show two families sprung from one stock, with colour changes on flags (Tokugawa hollyhock in gold on blue as against Mito, Owari and Kii variants) while the badge itself stayed identical — kinship is shown by similarity, not by a fixed one-house-one-mark rule (NEW-ScoutKamonOrigins-1, page 15).
