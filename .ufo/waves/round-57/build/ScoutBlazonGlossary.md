teaches: Parker treats blazon as a reproducible technical specification, naming the field, ordinary, and charges in sequence while distinguishing family arms from badges.
draft sentences:
- For this British vocabulary, Parker says most recognized heraldic terms derive from old Norman French of the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries, while their spellings and senses changed over time (NEW-ScoutBlazonGlossary-1, Introduction, terminology paragraph)
- Define blazon as a constrained description meant to let a reader reconstruct a coat accurately, and specify the tinctures, ordinaries, charges, and shield-points the register needs (NEW-ScoutBlazonGlossary-1, s.v. “Blazon,” pp. 64–67)
- In Parker’s scheme, name the field first, the principal ordinary next, then charges placed in relation to it; use this as a British blazon grammar, not a universal rule for all emblem systems (NEW-ScoutBlazonGlossary-1, s.v. “Blazon,” pp. 64–67)
- If family arms are inherited in the setting, state the descent rule and how branches differ: Parker defines family arms as hereditary and borne with proper differences by descendants of the first bearer (NEW-ScoutBlazonGlossary-1, s.v. “Arms,” p. 18)
- Keep a badge or cognizance distinct from a crest: Parker describes it as a supplemental distinction and places it on banners, horse trappings, and servants’ or followers’ clothing (NEW-ScoutBlazonGlossary-1, s.v. “Badge,” pp. 30–31)
- Warn against treating a later popular genealogy as proof of ancient arms: Parker records writers assigning coats to Adam, Eve, and legendary figures, which he says obscured the distinction between genuine and fictitious arms (NEW-ScoutBlazonGlossary-1, s.v. “Heraldry,” pp. 324–26)
