draft sentences:
- In English practice as Fox-Davies states it, the second son's mark is a crescent, the third son's a mullet, the fourth's a martlet, the fifth's an annulet. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF1)
- The eldest son bears a label of three points only during his father's lifetime, and sheds it when he succeeds to the undifferenced shield. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF2)
- Strictly the label marks the heir-apparent rather than "the eldest son" as such, and the same page rules that heraldry knows no disinheritance — heirship is inalienable blood descent. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF3)
- Wearing the difference marks is optional: no official or unofficial attempt enforces them in England, and only courtesy restrains a cadet from the head's arms. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF4)
- Beyond six brethren a father may not invent differences himself — Bossewell's rule, quoted on the same page, gives assignment to the kings of arms at their visitations. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF5)
- Before the Stuart period the number of points on a label carried no fixed meaning, so old labels cannot be read as birth-rank charts. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF6)
- The landless-fourth-son explanation of the martlet is a rationalisation Fox-Davies himself hedges ("whether this be true or false"), a popular account a designer should not canonise. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-1, LF7)

- Woodward dates the systematisation of the fixed marks to the reigns of the Tudor sovereigns — a sixteenth-century rule, not a medieval one. (NEW-ScoutCadencyMarks-2, LW1)
