teaches: Norwegian municipal coats of arms are designed to be two-colour (one metal plus one colour) single-motif, stylized emblems blazoned verbally for recognition at a distance.

draft sentences:
- A municipal coat of arms in the modern Norwegian tradition is called a "kommunevåpen"; its parts are the skjold (shield), the farger (tinctures, normally one metal gold or silver with one colour), and one motif that may be repeated rather than crowded with detail (NEW-ScoutCivicHeraldry-1, LOCATED https://heraldikk.no/PDF/GODE%20R%C3%85D%20OM%20NYE%20KOMMUNEV%C3%85PEN.pdf).
- The stated modern design rules are blunt: the arms should preferably use only two tinctures and a single charge, with colours chosen from the metals gold or silver combined with red, blue, black, green or purple, and figures rendered stylized, flattened, and free of unnecessary detail (NEW-ScoutCivicHeraldry-1, LOCATED https://heraldikk.no/PDF/GODE%20R%C3%85D%20OM%20NYE%20KOMMUNEV%C3%85PEN.pdf).
- A design rule is that the arms must be describable in the heraldic technical language, blasonering, so the identity is fixed by the verbal description while illustrations may vary (NEW-ScoutCivicHeraldry-1, LOCATED https://heraldikk.no/PDF/GODE%20R%C3%85D%20OM%20NYE%20KOMMUNEV%C3%85PEN.pdf).
- The simplicity rule traces to medieval knights needing instantly recognizable marks on shields in battle, so the modern guideline positions municipal arms as a recognition device, not a logo loaded with symbolism; an outsider's reading of municipal arms as a general logo over-reads them, since their purpose and rules are the armorial ones (NEW-ScoutCivicHeraldry-1, LOCATED https://heraldikk.no/PDF/GODE%20R%C3%85D%20OM%20NYE%20KOMMUNEV%C3%85PEN.pdf).
