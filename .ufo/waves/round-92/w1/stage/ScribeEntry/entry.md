## 1. Scope and ownership

This is a design-and-method reference on the depictive word class, not a canon statement. It fixes four things a worldbuilder needs and cannot borrow from the ordinary lexicon of a real language: what the class is (a lexeme class defined by what it depicts, not by what it copies), where it sits grammatically (a marked, usually quotation-framed construction rather than a plain predicate), what it does to a language's texture (it carries sensory detail no ordinary verb carries), and how to build one deliberately.

The class has three names in the literature and they are not interchangeable. The Africanist term, popularized by Doke (1935), is *ideophone*; the Japonic term is *mimetics*; South Asian linguistics prefers *expressives* (S957). All three labels are used in this entry where the source distinguishes them, and the surrounding prose uses *ideophone* as the head noun because it is the widest-attested comparative term.

The adjacent owner of the sound-symbolic token material is `sound-symbolism.md`, and the boundary is drawn in §12. Nothing here re-derives bouba/kiki effects, cross-linguistic shape-sound biases, the frequency code or phonesthemes; those are read there.

## 2. What an ideophone is: the typological definition

The operational definition this entry works from is the comparative-concept one adopted in the typological literature: "[a] member of an open lexical class of marked words that depict sensory imagery" (S957, quoting Dingemanse 2019: 16). Two features of that definition carry the whole design consequence.

First, the class is defined by *depiction*, not by imitation of a sound. The onomatopoeic case — a word that copies a noise — is one corner of the class, not its definition. A word for the way light glints off wet ground depicts without imitating anything audible, and it belongs to the class for exactly the same reason a word for a barking dog does.

Second, the class is *open* and *marked*. Open means a language can keep minting members from other word classes: the captured survey reports intensifying ideophones derived from verbs in one language, and two very productive templates deriving intensive and attenuative ideophones from triliteral verbal roots in another (S957).
Marked means a member is recognizable to speakers as depicting rather than describing, and the captured general tendencies say that recognition rides on phonological features absent from other word classes, on morphological invariance, and on a preference for quotative constructions (S959).

Whether a language actually has a word class of ideophones is a separate, language-internal question, and the sources treat it as genuinely open rather than settled (S957). In almost all the languages surveyed in the special issue, ideophones do form a distinct lexical category on morphological and/or syntactic criteria, which lets a description work bottom-up and then map the result onto the comparative concept (S957). The exception is instructive: a top-down study of sound roots in Umóⁿhoⁿ found that the roots have ideophone-like features but no basis for identifying a distinct morphosyntactic class (S957). A class can therefore be a comparative category that some languages realize and others do not.

The practical reading for worldbuilding: do not decide "does my language have ideophones" by asking whether a few words sound imitative. Ask what a depicting word would have to do differently from an ordinary verb, then decide whether that difference is morphological, prosodic, syntactic, or all three.

## 3. Names and traditions: ideophone, mimetics, expressive

The label a field uses carries an assumption about the class, and reading the label is cheap. The term *ideophone* is the one the Africanist tradition established and the one the typological literature prefers, while the Japonic tradition tends to use *mimetics* and the South Asian tradition tends to use *expressives* (S957). The typological literature treats *onomatopoeia* as a related but non-identical class, with the relationship contested in the following way.

## 4. Where onomatopoeia, expressives and interjections end

The relation between the classes is unsettled and the sources say so plainly, so a design should choose a side and record it (S957). On onomatopoeia, some authors treat it as a subtype of the ideophone class, others hold that sound-denoting ideophones are onomatopoeia while onomatopoeia in general are not ideophones, and at least one language study treats the two as distinct but overlapping categories in which a few lexemes belong to both (S957). One source also reports that its sound-imitative roots are wholly distinct from its onomatopoeia (S957).

Interjections are reported by every contributor who mentions them to be a distinct word class from the ideophone class (S957). The two nonetheless meet in one place: the quotative construction. Where a light verb 'say' or 'do' carries an ideophone, some interjections undergo a semantic shift and become indistinguishable from ideophones, which makes the quotative construction a pivot where the same lexeme serves as interjection or ideophone with different results (S957). That pivot is the single most useful thing in this section for a designer: it means a language can let its interjections and its depictive words be one inventory with two readings.

## 5. Typology table: how the depictive class integrates into grammar

The special issue surveyed the integration patterns across its contributor languages, and the survey is the most directly reusable artefact in this entry. The table below is the lane's reading of that survey: it is a design menu, not a census.

| language or area | family or branch | how the class enters the clause | class status as reported | the design lever this hands you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kambaata | Highland East Cushitic, Ethiopia | an invariant form plus a support verb, always | an open word class of morphologically invariant lexemes | support verbs as the integration device |
| Amharic | Ethiosemitic, Ethiopia | an invariant coverb plus an inflecting light verb | a separate word class in the language | one coverb slot, two light verbs, a noncausal/causal contrast |
| Teko | Tupi, French Guiana | predicative, argument-taking, or holophrastic | a clear-cut, easily identifiable category | the most thoroughly described case, with quantitative data |
| Northern Amis | East Formosan, Taiwan | predicate position, with voice markers | a continuum between onomatopoeia and a fully integrated class | a continuum as a deliberate design choice |
| Upper Guinea Creoles | Afro-Portuguese creoles, West Africa | adverbial, modifying a verb phrase or adjective | a field with a continental and an insular branch | inventory size as a contact variable |
| Kiranti | Trans-Himalayan Sino-Tibetan, Nepal | adverbial, modifying a verb phrase or adjective | identified from morphophonological templates in descriptions | templates as the discovery procedure |
| Sena | Bantu, Mozambique | predicative or adverbial, and holophrastic | a distinctive word class | two integration modes at once |
| Archi | East Caucasian, Dagestan | light verb construction with 'say' | defined by its occurrence in that construction | one verb as the class marker |
| Umóⁿhoⁿ | Siouan, North America | verb-modifying function in verb series | ideophone-like features without a distinct morphosyntactic class | the negative case: a class without a class |

## 6. Cushitic: Kambaata ideophones

The Kambaata description is the most usable grammar in the set for a designer, because it is a language-internal definition stated cleanly. Ideophones are an open word class of morphologically invariant lexemes that require the support verbs *y-* 'say' in intransitive clauses and *a'-* 'do' in transitive clauses before they can be inflected and integrated into an utterance (S958). The class is thereby distinguished from the equally invariant interjections: an ideophone does not constitute an utterance on its own, and independent use is rare and confined to elliptic artistic speech (S958).

Support verbs are enclitic to the ideophone, they alternate with *ih-* 'become' and *ass-* 'do', and they keep their full lexical meaning when used alone as ordinary verbs (S958). The construction is areal rather than accidental: it is described as a feature of the Ethiopian linguistic area and Northeast Africa generally, and the terminology around it varies between compound verb, composite verb and descriptive compound (S958). A designer should read that as a warning — the phenomenon is a regional trait, and a fictional culture in contact with such a region would borrow it, not invent it.

Phonologically the class is mostly inconspicuous; only a handful of members use phonemes that are otherwise marginal, and the onomatopoeic members are the only known lexemes with a palatal nasal, a word-final glottal fricative and a geminate glottal fricative (S958). Phonotactically the typical member is monosyllabic, less commonly disyllabic, and ends in a geminate consonant or consonant cluster (S958). Morphosyntactically, once it has its support verb, the class can be used in any syntactic context where verbs are used — as a converb, as a non-declarative benedictive main clause verb, as a final constituent of a relative clause, and inside a case-inflected verbal noun complement — while it cannot be pronominalized or passivized and is not accusative-marked (S958).

The morphology is productive in both directions. A resultative adjective is derived with the suffix *-eem*, and this derivation is exclusive to ideophones with no non-ideophonic inputs attested: *xóqq=y-* 'rise' yields *xoqq-eem-á(ta)* 'raised, elevated' (S958). Semantically the class ranges from the semantically broad to the very specific, and it evokes complex scenes such as an animal's whole posture (S958). Where a verbal concept is expressed by an ideophone, the choice between the two is arbitrary, which is the sharpest possible warning against reading motivation out of a lexicon (S958).

Frequency is where a designer gets numbers. The class is not confined to one text type: it turns up in narrative, procedural text, conversation, proverbs and schoolbooks, and even in a translated Gospel, which carries 13 ideophone types in 73 tokens across 11,851 words, roughly one ideophone per 162 words, against roughly one per 31 words in ordinary conversation (S958). Translated text dilutes the class; spontaneous speech does not.

## 7. Ethiosemitic: Amharic ideophones and the say-verb

Amharic gives the same class a different shape, and the difference is instructive rather than contradictory. Amharic verbal predicates divide into verbs derived from a consonantal root and complex predicates consisting of an invariant coverb followed by an inflecting light verb, most commonly the verb *alä* 'say' (S959). The claim made for the language is that only an ideophonic coverb can form noncausal/causal complex predicate pairs with the light verbs *alä* 'say' and *adärrägä* 'do', and other coverbs — interjections, adverbials, reduplicated nouns and verbs, quotations — do not express that contrast (S959).

That contrast is a gift to a designer. It means the depictive class is not decoration bolted onto a verb; it is the only thing in the language that can say "this happened" and "this was caused" with the same coverb, and the two light verbs carry the difference. Ideophones rarely occur on their own in Amharic and are typically morphosyntactically integrated by light verbs (S959).

The class is also derivable. Amharic productively interdigitates a lexical root with one of two templates for triliteral roots, one giving an intensive reading and one an attenuative reading, against the event expressed by a regularly inflected verb from the same root; the derived forms behave exactly like lexical members, appearing as the coverb of the light verbs and reduplicable (S959). Most verbs therefore occur in a tripartite system of neutral, intensive and attenuative forms (S959). This derivation is typologically rare: it occurs in some Bantu languages and in Bulgarian as well (S959).

Two further conclusions in the same source bear on design. The complex predicates with 'say' and 'do' are so common in Amharic that they must be treated as a separate inflectional verb class, with reduced morphological complexity, and they are a productive strategy for incorporating loanwords that do not fit the regular morphology (S959). And the class is productive enough to feed its own nominalization, so a depictive stem can be turned into a noun (S959). A language whose mimetic stratum is productive will leak it into morphology, and that leakage is a consequence, not a decoration.

## 8. Sensory semantics and the depictive field hierarchy

The semantic side is the part a worldbuilder is most tempted to improvise, and the sources give a handle. The typological definition proposes a five-part definition as the basis for a comparative concept and an implicational hierarchy for the sensory domains that ideophones depict, and the hierarchy has served as a heuristic that individual studies then confirm or revise, sometimes by adding to or slightly revising it, and sometimes by being recast as a semantic map (S957). The practical consequence is that depictive vocabulary is expected to cluster into sensory domains with a predictable order of availability, not to spread evenly.

The domain inventory in one surveyed language includes sound and speech, non-auditory sensations, ingestion, movement and effortful activities, and places, and the same source places that language inside a wider context of language contact (S957). Read across the special issue, one language's depictive words run along a continuum whose ends are onomatopoeia on one side — fully depictive lexemes — and fully grammatically integrated ideophones on the other, with the range between them covering depictive through descriptive function (S957).

For design, the discipline is to choose domains and to know that choosing them has consequences. A culture that gives its depictive words to sound and to movement but not to taste is making a claim about what its speakers notice, and that claim will be checked against its ritual, its food and its silence. Nothing in this entry decides what any fictional culture does with that.

## 9. Acquisition and processing of sound-symbolic effects

The processing question is whether a depictive register helps a child learn the language, and the answer in the one study captured here is a qualified no for the youngest learners. The claim under test is that sound-symbolic effects facilitate word learning because of the motivated relationship between form and meaning, and that these effects may follow from human-specific perceptive and cognitive biases and thus be universal (S960). The test used children acquiring Spanish, a language lacking this word class, and asked how sensitive they were to ideophones, which are treated as a type of sound-symbolic word (S960).

The design of the study: six groups of Spanish students, 64 children in total, 29 boys and 35 girls, from three grades with two groups per grade, second grade born 2016, fourth grade born 2014 and sixth grade born 2012, each group of ten or eleven children, at one school in Cádiz in southern Spain (S960). Two two-alternative forced-choice tests were used, both built from the same set of 30 ideophones drawn from five natural languages in five families — Siwu and Ewe from Niger-Congo in Ghana, Korean and Japanese as the isolated languages of Korea and Japan, and Semai from Austroasiatic in Malaysia — six ideophones per language, two each for colour, shape and motion (S960).

The results are the part that should temper a designer's optimism. Younger children were mostly insensitive to sound-symbolic effects as found in ideophones, and that sensitivity emerged and improved with age (S960). Only the oldest children answered better than chance in both tasks, with significance in the word task at p = 0.0002 and in the picture task at p = 0.017, and a significant age effect appeared only for the word task, at p = 0.0053 (S960). Children performed better on verbal than on visual responses, while no effect of sex and no effect of the type of ideophone was observed (S960). One methodological wrinkle is recorded: the youngest group could not understand the written-word task and was given the picture version instead (S960).

The reading for a fictional culture: a depictive register is not a pedagogical shortcut for beginners, and it should not be designed as one. A mimetic stratum that children cannot read is a register with a social rather than a developmental function — it is marked, and markedness is a cost. The finding also undercuts any claim that a depictive word teaches its own meaning by motivation, which is a caution for the sound-symbolic claims that live in the adjacent entry.

## 10. Design procedure for a fictional depictive register

The procedure below is derived from the located material; each step names the evidence it rests on. It is a procedure, not a decision: the outputs are choices for a world to make, and the worldbuilder owns them.

1. **Fix the label and the pole.** Choose whether the register is sound-mimicking, state-mimicking, or both, and record which neighbour class it bleeds into — onomatopoeia, interjections, or neither (S957). A register that copies noises and a register that depicts the way a thing looks are different inventions.
2. **Choose the integration mode from one row of the typology table.** Predicate, adverbial, or both. The reported languages each pick differently, and the choice determines the syntax the rest of the design has to satisfy (S957).
3. **Give the class its marker.** Decide what makes a member recognizable: a support verb, an enclitic light verb, a fixed prosodic contour, a morphological template, or a combination. The captured Cushitic case uses a verb, and in it the verb does the work (S958).
   The captured Ethiosemitic case also uses a verb, and again the verb does the work (S959).
4. **Decide whether the class is open.** If members can be minted from verbs and adjectives, the derivation must be stated as a rule, not a coincidence — the intensive and attenuative templates of one language are the model (S959).
5. **Set the phonotactics from the class, not from the language.** If the typical member is monosyllabic and ends in a geminate or cluster, say so and make the phonotactics of the class a rule of its own (S958).
6. **Pick the sensory domains and check them against the culture.** The domains a language can depict are ordered and selective, so a chosen set of domains is a claim about attention (S957).
7. **Budget the frequency.** Translated text runs at roughly one member per 162 words and spontaneous speech at roughly one per 31 in the captured case, so a design that wants dense mimetic speech must put it in dialogue and keep it out of scripture (S958).
8. **Do not sell the register as a learning aid.** The captured acquisition study found young children insensitive to sound-symbolic effects in ideophones, with sensitivity arriving only later (S960).
9. **Plan the leakage.** If the class is productive it will feed noun derivation and word formation, as it does in the captured Cushitic case (S958).
   The captured Ethiosemitic case shows the same leakage into morphology (S959).
10. **Name the adjacent owner.** Sound-symbolic token effects stay with the sound-symbolism entry; this register owns depiction, not motivation. The distinction is the corpus's own, and it is why this entry cites the surveyed typology rather than the sound-symbolic evidence base.

## 11. Decision tables for worldbuilding

Two compact tables, meant to be used at the drafting desk.

Table one fixes the integration choice. Each row is a coherent package: the class marker, the syntax it needs, and the failure mode that follows if it is used alone.

| integration mode | class marker | what the syntax must provide | what breaks first if the culture leans on it |
|---|---|---|---|
| predicated class | support verb or light verb | a slot the marked form can occupy and a way to mark noncausal and causal readings | a class with no verb loses the contrast between happening and being caused |
| adverbial class | a prosodic or segmental profile | a host verb phrase or adjective for the member to modify | intensity without content; every modifier ends up meaning roughly "strongly" |
| both modes | a marker that is visible in either slot | rules for which member goes in which slot | drift toward the adverbial end, which is the cheaper and the less memorable |
| class with no word-class status | sound-imitating roots only | nothing; the roots stay verbal | a culture believes it has a mimetic register and has only sound-imitating verbs |
| pivot construction | a quotative light verb | a way for one lexeme to switch reading with the verb | an inventory that cannot tell an interjection from a depiction |

Table two fixes the naming decision, because the label a designer picks commits them to a claim about the class.

| label chosen | the claim it commits you to | the claim it refuses you | typological precedent reported |
|---|---|---|---|
| ideophone | a comparative word class of depicting marks | a purely imitative register | the widest-attested comparative term |
| mimetic | a large conventionalized system with productive patterns | a small borrowed set of sound words | the Japonic usage |
| expressive | a class identified by its morphology | a class identified by its semantics | the South Asian usage |
| onomatopoeia | copying of sound only | the non-auditory depicting end of the range | reported both as a subtype of the class and as distinct from it |
| interjection | a syntactically free, meaning-carrying particle | a class with its own internal structure | reported as a distinct class that pivots with the quotative construction |

## 12. Boundaries, calibration, and the handoff

**Calibration.** The typological definition of the ideophone as an open lexical class of marked words depicting sensory imagery is mainstream in the literature cited here, and the use of that definition as a comparative concept is reported as nearly ubiquitous in the field (S957). The word-class status of ideophones in any particular language is genuinely contested, and the sources present it as an open research question rather than a settled result (S957).

The claim that sound-symbolic effects facilitate word learning, and that they may be universal because they rest on human-specific perceptual biases, is contested by the captured acquisition study, which found younger children largely insensitive to those effects (S960). Nothing in this entry is discredited material; the contested items are flagged as contested.

**Boundary with the adjacent owner.** `sound-symbolism.md` stays authoritative for sound-symbolic token effects: the cross-linguistic shape-sound biases, the bouba/kiki complex, the frequency code, and phonestheme banks. This entry does not restate, correct or extend any of that, and it makes no claim about sound-symbolic effects on individual words. The one place the two meet is the acquisition question in §9, and there the reading is deliberately narrow: a study of how children responded to ideophones as a type of sound-symbolic word (S960). If a designer needs a motivated sound pairing, that entry is the source; if a designer needs a depicting word class with its own grammar, this one is.

**Scope limits.** This entry holds no claim about any real language's inventory beyond the four sources cited, no claim about how ideophones change over time except what the captured derivation material shows, and no claim about learners of any language other than the Spanish-speaking children in the captured study (S960).

**Handoff.** The deliverable stops here. A fictional culture's depictive register is a canon decision for lore to make, not a fact this corpus records; what crosses the boundary is the typology table, the two decision tables, and the ten-step procedure, as method. Any doctrine that a depictive word *does* what it sounds like is an in-world belief and belongs to the myth and synthesis entries, which own the difference between a linguistic bias and a cultural claim (S957). The five design questions left open for the receiving world are the label, the integration mode, the class marker, the sensory domains, and the frequency budget.
