- topic: two things the naming entry could not say before. First, that a Chinese naming
  system is a *late cultural acquisition* — 名 precedes 姓, and their combination is a
  habit formed under contact, not a natural given. Second, that Chinese given names draw
  on a finite, listable semantic inventory, which the onomastics literature classifies into
  subcategories and counts.
- gap filled: rounds 2 and 3 added scholarship *about* Chinese names from psychology and
  from the wider onomastics field, and round 3's Chinese-language row was the abstract of a
  Western onomastics article. This amendment supplies a Chinese-language study of the
  system (S897) and the classification itself, stated by its own author in the field's own
  journal and open in full (S903).
- sources: the two rows above — one tier B, one tier A.

- scope: an amendment to the naming entry. It describes how a naming system is structured and how its inventory can be specified; it decides no culture's names.

### Proposed body (dated addition)

- **§N A system acquired, not given** — from S897: 姓 and 名 distinguished; 名 used before 姓; the 姓（氏）+名 pattern stable from the 春秋戰國 period onward; the pattern spreading with cultural contact, and the single/double given-name distinction tracking that spread.
- **§N+1 The inventory is finite** — from S903: given names classified by the lexemes they carry; 88 plant, flower and tree terms; the 17 most common lexemes and characters; the cultural reading attached to each class.
- **§N+2 What this licenses for an invented culture** — a name lexicon can be tabulated (lexeme class, frequency, cultural reading), so "does this name fit" becomes checkable against a list rather than argued.

### Proposed body (dated addition)

- **§N The professional reference layer** — from S888 and S889: the dictionary as a published work (東邦通信社, 1929-), and the entry format: occupation, birthplace, 本名義, dates, career. A world bible's name table needs the same slots.
- **§N+1 Terminology that does not travel** — from S886: 戸籍's abolition of 姓 and its dissolution into 苗字; the emergence of 苗字 among the warrior class; 姓名 as 氏 + 名; and the explicit warning that Japan's 苗字 is not the Chinese 姓.
- **§N+2 The onomastics field on Chinese names** — from S890: names formed individually and predominantly semantically transparent; the categories a person holds (名, 大名, 本名, 正名, 學名, 號); and the absence of grammatical gender rules in names.
- **§N+3 Distribution, not just form** — from S891: surname-plus-given-name as the standard pattern, compound surnames inherited from one parent, and a measurable matching preference index over a large civic dataset.
- **§N+4 Naming as a cultural form** — from S896: 姓名 as a sign distinguishing persons and as a social phenomenon, which is why naming practice is a per-period question.
- **§N+5 What is still missing** — no *Chinese-language* onomastics venue was reachable (《中国语文》, CNKI); these rows are the onomastics literature about Chinese names, not the Chinese-language discipline.
