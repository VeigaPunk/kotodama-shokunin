## 1. Load-bearing frames: how to use this entry

- **Rapoport (1969)** is the standing correction: house form is driven primarily by **sociocultural factors** (family structure, status display, defense, cosmology); climate, materials, and site are **modifying factors**. Same climate, radically different houses; the same house form across very different climates. Build fictional settlements from the social priority, then let climate trim it.
- **Oliver (1997)** is the exemplar mine: three volumes of vernacular typology by culture and material — when you need a real precedent for a fictional form, look there before inventing.
- **Kostof (1991)** gives settlement *morphology*: organic, grid, and "grand manner" plans are historical processes, not styles — cities are events with strata.
- **Lynch (1960)** is the prose toolkit: **paths, edges, districts, nodes, landmarks** — the five elements citizens use to map a city. A fictional city described in these five terms reads navigable in one paragraph.
- **Semantic method**: house parts form semantic fields like any other (couple: semantic-fields-lexicon-building.md). The relativity question — do settlement words shape thought? — is calibrated in §8; the answer is "weakly, demonstrably, never deterministically."
- Named heuristics used below: **the wall names the city**; **the hall shrinks**; **status is bread**; **name the building by what happens in it**; **planners only lexify what they can measure**.

## 2. House-part nomenclature: six traditions

| Term (tradition) | Gloss | What it encodes |
|---|---|---|
| fauces (Rome) | "throat" — entry passage | thresholds are bodily; deeper throat = grander house |
| atrium (Rome) | hall under the compluvium roof-opening | ancestral hall (wax *imagines* of ancestors displayed) turned status room |
| impluvium / compluvium | sunken basin / roof opening above it | rain harvested inside the wall line |
| tablinum (Rome) | record office between atrium and peristyle | a paper wall between public and private halves |
| triclinium (Rome) | "three-couch room" | dining *posture* named in the room word |

(Vitruvius VI; Lewis & Short s.vv.; OED s.v. atrium)

- **Japanese**: **moya** 母屋 "mother(-house)" = the structural core bay; **hisashi** 廂 = lean-to bays under extended eaves; **engawa** 縁側 = veranda strip; **doma** 土間 = packed-earth work bay where shoes stay and the hearth sits; **zashiki** 座敷 = matted parlor with **kamiza/shimoza** high/low seat ends; **tokonoma** = honor alcove; **kura** 蔵 = fireproof storehouse — wealth lives in a separate building (Itoh 1972; Nishi & Hozumi 1983). Two worldview fossils: the house has a *kinship structure* (mother-core plus attached children-bays), and **engawa** is homophonous with **en** 縁 'bond, fate' — the edge strip is where relationships sit (wordplay exploited in Japanese speech; the spatial term itself is just "edge side"). **Genkan** 玄関 (lit. 'dark/mysterious barrier') is the shoe line — inside/outside made visible; the term is standardly said to originate as a Zen temple's formal gate before demotion into house entry (standard account, now sourced — JAANUS s.v. *genkan*).
- **Chinese siheyuan 四合院**: **yingbi** 影壁 screen wall inside the gate (sightline block; the explanation that spirits travel in straight lines is reported folk belief, current in popular and heritage accounts — no scholarly ethnography located); **chuihuamen** 垂花门 "festooned-flower gate" dividing outer (male/guest) from inner (female/family) court; **zhengfang** 正房 principal rooms facing south — seniority = centrality = light; **xiangfang** 厢房 flanking rooms for juniors; **daozuo** 倒座 "reversed seat" front rooms facing the wrong way — the humblest slot named by direction. The plan is a status grammar (Steinhardt 1990 for the cosmological frame).
- **Arab dār**: **wasṭ al-dār** "the middle of the house" = the courtyard is the navel; **majlis** "place of sitting" = reception — and several modern parliaments are named Majlis (furniture → government, §7); **īwān/liwān** vaulted open-front hall (Persian origin, borrowed empire-wide); **mashrabiya** carved lattice screen — folk etymology 'drinking shelf' is traditional and partly debated — a competing *mashrafiya* ("overlook") proposal circulates (Fathy 1986; Mazzetto 2025); privacy is engineered at the street, not the plot line: houses turn **cul-de-sac inward** (Hillenbrand 1994; Lapidus 1967).
- **The English hall and its satellites**: the medieval **hall** was the one room; functions then budded off as named annexes — **buttery** < *bouteillerie* 'bottle room' (butler = bottle-man), **pantry** < *pain* 'bread', **larder** < *lard*, **solar** < *solārium* 'upper private room', **parlour** < *parler* 'to talk' (OED s.vv.). **The hall shrinks**: as annexes multiplied, *hall* narrowed from "the room" to "the corridor" — the parent word demoted by its children. *Butler/pantryman* survive as professions: storage vocabulary fossilizes into job titles.
- **Mongolian ger**: **khana** lattice walls, **uni** roof poles, **toono** crown ring (smoke hole = the sky opening directly above the hearth), **bagana** doorposts, door to the south; the dwelling is a portable cosmos (Oliver 1997).
- **Design levers**: (a) name rooms by **stored object** (buttery), **activity** (parlour), **position** (daozuo), or **kinship** (moya) — the dominant strategy is the culture's ontology; (b) every culture needs threshold furniture (fauces, genkan, yingbi, pomerium) because thresholds carry the entry rite (Van Gennep 1909; couple: ritual-liminality.md); (c) give status rooms the poetic names and storage rooms the blunt ones, or deliberately invert it for an ascetic culture.

## 3. Roof forms and climate

| Form | Emblematized by | Climate/tech logic | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| steep gable thatch (~60°) | Japanese **gasshō-zukuri** 合掌造り "joined-hands construction" (Shirakawa-gō, UNESCO 1995) | snow sheds; attic = silk-work floor | name = gesture of prayer — form named after hands |
| hipped | Japanese **yosemune**; SE Asian roofs | typhoon/wind resistance — no gable end to catch wind | gable (kirizuma 切妻) vs hip is the basic Japanese pair, plus **irimoya** hip-and-gable hybrids and **karahafu** cusped gables as status ornament |
| flat + parapet | SW Asian/N. African adobe | low rain, high diurnal swing; thermal mass; roofs used as rooms | "flat" roofs need real structure — flatness is a material statement |
| dome | Pantheon; mosque domes | masonry compression; then **symbolic**: a sky over the sanctuary | dome over tomb and prayer hall predates its "Islamic" reading |
| windcatcher | Persian **badgir** (Yazd) | passive stack ventilation of cellars (Bahadori 1978) | a *named air-device*: your hot culture should lexify its cooling machine |
| mansard / steep Parisian | 17th–19th c. France | extra storey under the roof line | "roof tax dodge" is the popular story — contested; Paris's 1783 cornice-height rule (the roof zone above the cornice free) is the better-documented driver (Conway 2019) |
| sawtooth / monitor | industrial era | north-light glazing, ventilation ridges | the roof form that dates a street as industrial, at a glance |

- **The body lexicon of roofs**: English **ridge** < OE *hrycg* 'the back'; **collar** beam, **bressummer** 'breast-beam', **knee** brace — carpentry names its members after ours. A fictional culture's roof anatomy will mirror its body lexicon; run the check.
- **Heating lexicon = sitting lexicon**: Roman **hypocaust** (underfloor, with tiled piers), Korean **ondol** 온돌 'warm stone', North Chinese **kang** 炕 heated platform-bed, Austrian **Kachelofen** tile stove; Catalan **glória** underfloor smoke channel (reported regional farm tradition; descriptive sources thin — treat as a design lead). Where the heat is in the *floor*, the culture *sits on the heat* — the kang platform and the matted zashiki are social architecture, not furniture.
- **Design levers**: (a) pitch follows precipitation, mass follows diurnal swing, but *which room is warm* follows status — decide who gets the heat; (b) name the roof after a gesture, animal, or body part and the skyline becomes readable in-world; (c) anti-determinism check (Rapoport 1969): if two of your cultures share a biome, their roofs should still differ — or have a historical reason not to.

## 4. Courtyard vs hall vs stilt: three arcs

| Arc | Emblem words | What it keeps out | Exemplars |
|---|---|---|---|
| courtyard | wasṭ al-dār, atrium/impluvium, aangan (haveli), siheyuan | street: heat, dust, prying eyes, unsegregated genders | Han-Chinese and Islamic-city houses; Roman domus; Indian haveli |
| hall | megaron, skáli, hall, shoin-zukuri zashiki | winter, strangers, internal disorder | Mycenaean megaron; Norse skáli; English medieval hall; Germanic *Pfalz*; Japanese shoin reception halls |
| stilt | pile dwelling, **rumah panggung** (stilt house), gasshō (raised floor) | water, animals, mud, spirits-under-floor | Austronesian/Tai stilt houses; Batak **ruma/sopo** house-granary mirror pair; Minangkabau **rumah gadang** whose **gonjong** horn-peak roof invokes the buffalo of the founding legend — in a matrilineal society the house lineage follows the mother; Alpine prehistoric pile dwellings (UNESCO 2011) |

(Oliver 1997 for all vernacular entries; Kostof 1991 for the urban-court arc)

- **Courtyard logic**: privacy is *engineered at the boundary*, life is *inside facing an open sky*; gender segregation gets built gates (chuihuamen). **Hall logic**: one big room before all others — hierarchy is performed *in view*; annexes then sprout (§2 English). **Stilt logic**: the ground floor is for animals, storage, or nothing — social life happens *above*; entering someone's house always means climbing, which is why thresholds and ladders carry the entry rite (Van Gennep 1909).
- **Rapoport's (1969) demonstration**: the same biome produces all three arcs elsewhere — so justify your culture's arc from its social facts (who guards whom, who eats with whom, what wealth is — grain in a kura? cattle under the floor?) before citing climate.
- **Naming calibration**: "Anasazi" (Navajo, roughly 'ancient enemies/ones') is disfavored in South-west archaeology; the standard term is **Ancestral Puebloan**. Naming rights over ruins are themselves worldview — usable in-world.
- **Design lever**: pick the arc by what the house must keep out, then *let the etiquette say it back*: courtyard cultures invent screen walls and inner gates; hall cultures invent the high seat and the salt-line at the door; stilt cultures make the ladder sacred.

## 5. Sacred architecture: temple / church / shrine / mosque are distinct concepts, and the words say so

| Word | From | Literal | Concept it fossilizes |
|---|---|---|---|
| temple | L **templum** | *tem-* 'cut': the augurs' surveyed, cut-off precinct in sky or ground | sacred space is *measured out* (Lewis & Short; OED) |
| temenos (Gk) | τέμενος | land 'cut off' for a god | same deep logic, Greek instance (LSJ; Burkert 1985) |
| naos (Gk) | ναός | 'dwelling' — the god's house | the temple as divine residence; acropolis = "high city" |
| church | OE cirice < Gk **kyriakon** (dōma) | "the Lord's (house)" | Germanic languages name the building after its owner |
| église / iglesia | Gk **ekklēsia** | "the assembly" | Romance languages name it after *the people* |
| mosque | Ar **masjid** | "place of prostration" (root s-j-d) | a place for an *act* — Islamic theology explicitly non-domestic: God has no house |
| synagogue | Gk synagōgē | "bringing together" | the assembly that reads — named for its practice |
| shrine | L **scrinium** | scroll-case → reliquary | sacred by *containing* something |
| chapel | L **cappella** | "little cloak" (St Martin's relic cloak) | sacred by *relic*, grown into a room word; chaplain rides on it |
| cathedral | Gk **kathédra** | "church of the (bishop's) seat" | authority is a chair |
| basilica | Gk basilikē (stoā) | "royal hall" | imperial architecture adopted by a church that met in it |
| nave / aisle | L **nāvis** / **āla** | "ship" / "wing" (folk-spelled after 'isle') | the congregation as hull; body-metaphor plan |
| garbhagriha (Skt) | "womb-house" | innermost sanctum of a Hindu temple | the temple generates; siting by **vastu-purusha-mandala** grids (81 squares), texts: *Manasara*, *Mayamata* (Kramrisch 1946; Dagens 1985) |
| jinja/sha 社, miya 宮 | shrine terms; **jingū** for Ise | via ruler-patronage vocabulary | shrine (kami) vs temple (寺 ji) vs church are *institutional* distinctions in Japanese, not stylistic ones (Coaldrake 1996) |
| haram / ḥarīm | Ar root ḥ-r-m | inviolate precinct / protected (women's) quarters → "harem" | one root spans sanctuary, prohibition, and the household's protected half (Khadduri 1955; OED s.v. harem) |

- **The killer pair**: *church* (Lord's house) vs *église* (assembly) — the same building is named after its **owner** in one language family and its **congregation** in the other. Etymology here is applied ecclesiology; a fictional schism can be readable from the two factions' different words for the same hall.
- **寺 ji 'temple'**: the character originally named a **Han government bureau**; the founding legend of China's first Buddhist establishment (White Horse Temple, Luoyang, traditionally 68 CE) has the monks lodged in such an office, and the bureau's name becomes the Buddhist temple word (Zürcher 1959). Vocabulary conquest, stepwise documented — a conversion of a language's building words is itself datable history.
- **Torii**: etymology contested (bird-perch *tori-i* reading, gateway-type, or calque theories) — present it as unresolved, not as fact.
- **Toolkit for mosque description** (Hillenbrand 1994): **qibla** prayer direction; **mihrab** niche on the qibla wall (the word's Qur'anic sense is 'fortified station' — the niche sense is a later semantic shift, discussed in the literature); **minbar** stepped seat; **sahn** courtyard; **minaret** < *manāra* 'light/beacon place'.
- **Design levers**: name the sacred building for the **act** (prostration, assembly, reading), the **office** (chair, priest), the **container** (cloak, relics, scrolls), or the **god's domesticity** (house, womb) — each implies a different metaphysics you can back-write; then let heresies and reforms *rename the buildings* (temple→meeting-house is a real Reformation move).

## 6. City-planning lexicon: planners only lexify what they can measure

- **Roman**: **castrum** grid → **cardo** (N–S axis) × **decumanus** (E–W) crossing at the **forum**; surveyed with the **groma**; founding rite: ploughing the **sulcus primigenius** around the **pomerium** — the city begins as a furrow and a law-line (Rykwert 1976; Lewis & Short s.v. pomerium). Exemplar: Timgad (100 CE). The grid is cadastral: a state that *measures land* builds this city.
- **Greek**: the **Hippodamian** grid — Miletus rebuilt after 479 BCE, Priene, Olynthus; Aristotle *Politics* II.8 credits Hippodamus with grid plus political theory (his Piraeus attribution is debated). **Agora** (assembly-ground) vs **temenos**: the Greek plan lexifies the meeting and the god, separately sited (Burkert 1985; Kostof 1991).
- **Chinese**: the *Kao Gong Ji* (Artificers' Record, in the *Rites of Zhou*) prescribes the ideal capital — nine li square, three gates a side, nine avenues each way, **ancestral temple left, soil-and-grain altar right, court in front, market behind** — an orientation-and-number cosmology actualized at Chang'an and Beijing (Steinhardt 1990). Tang Chang'an: 100+ walled **fang** 坊 wards with gated, surveilled access; Song Kaifeng's street-opening "collapse of the ward system" is the great planning-vocabulary shift — wall-words lose, street-words win. **Feng shui** 風水 ('wind–water'; the older technical term is **kanyu** 堪舆) has two schools — **form school** (landform reading, Jiangxi tradition) vs **compass school** (**luopan** instrument, Fujian tradition): not one monolithic "science" (Feuchtwang 1974).
- **Japanese**: **jōkamachi** castle town (Coaldrake 1996): castle core, samurai rings graded by rank-distance, **machiya** 町屋 merchant rows with narrow frontage — the Kyoto "eel's sleeping place" (**unagi no nedoko**) deep-plot shop-house (Nishi & Hozumi 1983) — and **teramachi** temple districts relocated to city edges (institutional control and firebreaks). **Machi/mura** (town/village) suffixes carry legal status, not size.
- **Islamic**: **madina** city; Baghdad founded 762 CE as the round **Madinat al-Salam** with **khittaṭ** allotted quarters that then grew organically into **ḥāra/mahalla** neighborhoods; the **jāmiʿ** (Friday mosque) anchors the center, the **bazaar/suq** is the spine, **khan/caravanserai** for trade, cul-de-sac residential privacy (Lapidus 1967; Hillenbrand 1994). An "organic-looking" plan that is actually rule-governed — customary law lexified.
- **Industrial/modern toolkit** (for eras with smoke and reform): **garden city** (Howard 1902), zoned "radiant" planning, and Lynch's (1960) five elements as your description grammar. Planning manifestos mint vocabulary (**green belt**, **zone**) — a reformist era in your world should mint planning words and lose arguments with them.
- **Design levers**: (a) give every planner culture an **instrument + founding rite + claim word** (groma/plough/pomerium; luopan/siting/kanyu; khittat allotment); (b) encode fear in plan vocabulary: ward gates, teramachi firebreaks, screen walls; (c) encode worship in the center: ancestral-temple-left-altar-right, mosque-first, church-at-crossing.

## 7. How settlement words fossilize worldview

- **Place-name strata — read the map like a dig** (Ekwall 1960; Gelling & Cole 2000):

| Suffix | Stratum | Base word |
|---|---|---|
| -chester / -caster / -cester | Roman | castrum 'fort' |
| -wich / -wick | Roman-road + trade | L vīcus → OE wīc 'trading/industrial site' — salt towns Nantwich, Droitwich; ≠ ON vík 'bay' (Gelling's warning: homographs, different strata) |
| -by, -thorpe | Danelaw (Norse) | bý 'farm', þorp 'hamlet' (Derby, Grimsby) |
| -ton, -ham | Anglo-Saxon | tūn 'enclosure, farmstead', hām 'homestead' — both later 'town' |
| -bury, -borough/-burgh | fortified | burg 'fort' |
| -leigh/-ley, -field | land-use | lēah 'clearing', feld 'open land' |
| -combe | Brittonic | cumb 'deep valley' |

- **The wall names the city**: 城 chéng 'city wall' → 'city' (the wall *is* the city); Slavic **-grad/-gorod** (Belgrade 'white city'); Sanskrit **-pura** (Jaipur; Singapore 'lion-city'); **metropolis** 'mother-city' — colonization as kinship; **Istanbul** standardly < Greek *eis tēn polin* 'to the city'; Latin **urbs** (physical) vs **civitas** (citizen body) → English doublets *urban/civil*; and English **town** < tūn 'fence, enclosure', cognate with German **Zaun** 'fence', Dutch **tuin** 'garden' — *the fence was the settlement* (OED s.v. town; Watkins 2011).
- **Status is bread**: **lord** < OE *hlāf-weard* 'loaf-warden'; **lady** < *hlǣf-dige* 'loaf-kneader' (second element debated); the two top titles of English are a household's bread economy. Same root deepens: PIE **\*dem-** 'house(hold)' → *domus, domestic, dominate, dame, tame*, **despot** < Gk *despótēs* < \**dems-potis* 'house-master', English **timber** (Watkins 2011). To dominate is, etymologically, to run the house.
- **Enclosure family**: PIE **\*gher-** 'enclose' → *garden, yard, orchard, garth, gird, court* (L *cohors*), *horticulture* — and **paradise** < Old Persian/Avestan **pairidaēza** 'walled enclosure' (Watkins; OED). Eden is a fence before it is plants: the garden concept is property, then botany.
- **Pejoration gradient — the center's contempt fossilized as insults**: **villein** (villa-worker, serf) → *villain*; **ceorl** → *churl/churlish*; **boor** (cf. German *Bauer* 'farmer') → *boorish*; **pagan** 'villager/civilian' → 'heathen' (4th-c. Christian usage); **heathen** 'heath-dweller'; Gk **idiōtēs** 'private person, one who keeps out of public life' → 'ignoramus' (LSJ; OED s.vv.). Design lever: mint your culture's slurs from the rival's settlement words — instantly believable contempt.
- **Government is furniture**: **thing** < OE þing 'assembly, matter' (Þingvellir, Althing c. 930 CE — the assembly predates the object sense); **hustings** < *hūs-þing* 'house-assembly'; **Majlis** 'sitting/reception' → national legislatures; **sanhedrin** < Gk *synedrion* 'sitting-together'; **senate** < *senex* 'old man'; **duma** 'thought/judgment' (Germanic \*dōmą, cognate *doom* — Vasmer); **parliament** < *parler*. Councils are named after sitting, talking, thinking, elders, and the room's furniture. Name your fictional council "the Bench," "the Hearth-ring," or "the Cushions" and its politics are pre-written.
- **Nested houses as civilization**: Arabic **dār** 'house' scales to **dār al-islām** vs **dār al-ḥarb** — world order as house boundaries (Khadduri 1955). One domestic word, geopoliticized.
- **Glyphs fossilize**: **家 jiā** 'house/family' = roof 宀 over pig 豕 (composition uncontroversial; the original sense — pig-pen, sacrificial victim, or ancestral temple — is debated); **安 ān** 'peace' = woman under roof; **宿 sù** 'lodge' = person under roof. Writing systems archive settlement cosmology (couple: writing-systems.md).
- **Japanese inside/outside**: **uchi** 内 'home/inside/in-group' vs **soto** is the load-bearing social opposition (Bachnik & Quinn 1994); **honne/tatemae** — **tatemae** 建前 is literally a *built front, facade*: the sincerity lexicon borrows architecture (Lebra 1976). Threshold law: **eavesdrop** < OE *yfesdrype*, one who stands within the drip-line to listen — the eaves even marked a legal strip around the building (OED). Doors deified: Latin **ianua** 'door' → **Janus**, gatekeeper of transitions (Lewis & Short); hearth as focus: Latin **focus** 'hearth' → the point where things converge (fr. *foyer*, sp. *hogar*).
- **Threshold rites everywhere**: genkan shoe-lines, yingbi spirit-screens, pomerium furrows, ladders (§4) — Van Gennep (1909) is the named framework; thresholds are the smallest liminality your world can stage in one sentence of stage business.

## 8. Calibration (contested or misremembered — state it plainly in any lore doc)

- **Strong linguistic relativity is dead; weak relativity is alive.** Whorfian determinism and the "N words for snow" party trick are discredited as evidence (Pullum 1991); color universals cut against deep relativity (Berlin & Kay 1969), while spatial-frame studies support real, bounded effects of language on cognition (Levinson 2003); the universalist baseline itself is contested (Evans & Levinson 2009). Canon rule: settlement words *index* worldview richly (§7 is all attested); they do not *compel* cognition.
- **Climate determinism is discredited.** Huntington (1915)-style environment→civilization claims are the cautionary case; Rapoport (1969) is the standing correction. Never derive a fictional culture's form from biome alone.
- **Feng shui and vastu popularizations** compress living technical traditions into lifestyle rules: cite the textual and ethnographic bases (Feuchtwang 1974 for geomancy; Kramrisch 1946 and Dagens 1985 for the vastu-purusha-mandala and *Mayamata*), not the pop market. Modern "vastu consultancy" is a market phenomenon distinct from the śilpa-śāstra tradition.
- **Mansard "roof tax"**: popular, but the documented driver is Parisian cornice/height regulation; treat the tax story as anecdote; the documented driver is the 1783 Paris rule measuring height to the cornice (Conway 2019).
- **Etymological flags**: *lady*'s second element debated; *mashrabiya* 'drinking-shelf' traditional and partly debated (Fathy 1986; Mazzetto 2025); *torii* etymology unresolved; *moya* as kinship metaphor is a gloss, not attested etymon semantics; *timber* under \**dem-* is the standard PIE account but is a reconstruction — cite dictionaries, not asterisked forms; **家**'s original sense genuinely debated; Hippodamus's role at Piraeus debated; the **genkan** < Zen gate account is standard and documented (JAANUS s.v. *genkan*).
- **UNESCO citations**: give site names and inscription years as above; ref numbers omitted deliberately here to avoid ID drift.

## 9. Applying to a fictional culture — craft checklist

1. Fix the **boundary word** first (where does inside stop?), then build its rite: genkan, fauces, pomerium, ladder, spirit-screen.
2. Choose a dominant room-naming strategy — stored object / activity / position / kinship — and let one borrowed stratum show contact history.
3. Decide **where the heat is** and who sits on it (kang/ondol/hearth-hall); warm place = plot place.
4. Pitch roofs from precipitation, but name them from gesture, animal, or body (gasshō, ridge); your carpentry lexicon mirrors your body lexicon.
5. Pick courtyard / hall / stilt by what the house keeps out; encode the answer in etiquette and in the plan's words (screen wall, high seat, sacred ladder).
6. Name the sacred building for act, office, container, or divine domesticity — then back-write the metaphysics, and let schisms rename buildings.
7. Give planners an instrument, a founding rite, and a claim word; walls, wards, and firebreak districts encode what the state fears.
8. Layer place-name suffixes by settlement stratum (fort → trade → farm → clearing); a two-hour walk through your city should read like a dig report.
9. Mint insults from rivals' settlement words (villain/churl/boor pattern) and parliaments from furniture (sitting/thinking/elders).
10. Run one nested-house geopoliticization (dār al-islām/dār al-ḥarb pattern) or one glyph fossil (家 pattern) — one is texture, two is a system.
11. Relativity discipline: no "they have twelve words for X, therefore…" in canon; index worldview with attested mechanisms (pejoration, strata, metonymy), not word-count arguments.
12. Handoff discipline: this file supplies method + comparative data only; which words exist in-world is lore-shokunin's canon call (lane rules).
