## 1. Periodization is a legitimacy technology, not a filing system

- Two design axes decide everything else: (a) **what a time-unit licenses** — does a new era void contracts, reset taxes, require new coinage, open a policy window?; (b) **who owns the count** — palace astrologers, temple astronomers, the mint, genealogists, the fisc, a standardization body.
- Distinguish **emic periodization** (how the culture wants its own past cut: eras, dynasties, world ages) from **etic periodization** (how outsiders cut it for museums and textbooks: Three-Age system, "Byzantine," "Middle Kingdom"). The friction between the two is itself characterization: the people labeled "Byzantine" called themselves Romans (Rhomaioi) and never used the label — the word is Hieronymus Wolf's, 1557 (Grumel 1958 for their actual dating habits).
- Heuristic: era *names* die with the regime that owns them; calendar *machinery* (month lengths, leap rules, weekdays) survives across regimes. A conquest that swaps the era name but keeps the calendar is soft occupation; resetting the count itself (France's Revolutionary calendar, 1793–1805) declares a new world, and usually fails to stick.

## 3. Regnal era names: the Sinosphere flagship

- **China.** Era names (nianhao 年號) begin under Emperor Wu of Han: Jianyuan 建元, "Establishing the Origin," 140 BCE — the earliest ones may have been formalized retrospectively (Wilkinson 2018). Pre-eranaming reigns were split as "first / middle / last year" (qian/zhong/hou yuan) under Wen and Jing. Trigger typology before the Ming: accession, auspicious omens (xiangrui — a cauldron dug from the river, a captured unicorn), disasters needing expiation, calendar reform. Emperor Wu changed eras roughly eleven times; Wu Zetian burned through a dozen-plus in fifteen years — each change re-issued calendars, coins, and archives, so era-name churn is a direct proxy for political instability.
- **The Ming–Qing settlement:** one reign, one era, from Hongwu 1368 (the great exception: Emperor Yingzong reigned twice — Zhengtong 1436–49, restored as Tianshun 1457–64). Accession-year rule: the era changes at the *next* New Year (yunian gaiyuan 逾年改元), so a dead emperor's era runs to December 31 of the accession year, and the first year of the new era is read yuannian 元年, "the Origin Year," never "year one."
- **Japan.** Taika 大化 (645 CE) is the first nengō, proclaimed with the Taika reform edicts; the sequence is continuous only from Taihō (701). Eras were changed for auspicious reasons (shōzui 祥瑞) until Meiji; the one-reign-one-era practice (issei ichigen 一世一元) starts with Meiji (1868) and is now law in a single article: the Gengō-hō (Law No. 43, 1979) says the era changes **only** on imperial succession. Reiwa is the 248th era.
- **Era-name craft rules** (the de facto criteria the government confirmed in 2019): two characters; easy to read and write; never used before; not ordinary words; auspicious meaning; sourced from a classic. Meiji 明治 comes from the Yijing ("the sage faces south and all toward light is well-governed"); Shōwa 昭和 from the Shujing's Yaodian (the people made bright, the ten thousand states in harmony); Heisei 平成 from the Shiji and Shujing ("inside at peace, outside achieved; earth leveled, heaven completed"). Reiwa 令和 (2019) broke thirteen centuries of precedent by coming from a *Japanese* classic — the plum-blossom preface, Man'yōshū 5.815 (Ōtomo no Tabito, 730 CE): "in early spring, in a fine month, the air clear, the wind calm." The characters are the whole name: there is no separate "meaning" that survives translation-guessing well.
- **Offshoots.** Goryeo/Joseon Korea dated by *Chinese* eras as an act of loyalty; Gojong's Korean Empire minted its own Gwangmu 光武 (1897), then Yunghui 隆熙 (1907–1910), after which Japanese nengō were imposed — occupation overwrites the temporal lexicon. Vietnam ran niên hiệu (Gia Long, Minh Mạng, Khải Định, Bảo Đại). The Republic of China replaced eras with the Minguo count (1912 = year 1, still official in Taiwan). North Korea's Juche calendar (adopted 1997) starts at 1912, the founder's birth year — a secular nianhao. South Korea used the Dangun calendar (epoch 2333 BCE) officially 1948–1961.

## 4. Transition rituals and the logistics of the seam

- **The two trigger classes.** Death transitions are backdated to the accession moment: Hirohito died 7 January 1989, and Heisei was announced the same day by Chief Cabinet Secretary Obuchi — so Shōwa 64 existed for exactly seven days (1–7 Jan 1989), and every printed calendar and datebook for "Shōwa 64" became scrap overnight. Abdication transitions are legislated in advance: in 2019, Heisei 31 ran 1 Jan–30 Apr, and Reiwa 1 began 1 May with Naruhito's accession — the only postwar mid-reign seam.
- **The Japanese performance sequence (2019):** scholars propose candidates (authors confidential by custom) → cabinet narrows and decides → the Chief Cabinet Secretary raises a wooden board with the two characters brushed on a tanzaku: the *physical reveal of the calligraphy* is the ritual climax, watched live by the whole country. Rites then unfurl in order: the abdication ceremony (Taiirei Seiden no gi), the private handover of the sword-and-jewel regalia and seals (Kenji tō Shōkei no gi) — the *objects*, not documents, effect the transfer — then the public enthronement (Sokui no rei) and the Daijōsai thanksgiving.
- **Chinese parallels:** gaiyuan 改元; era changes issued by edict, with the new name propagating at the speed of paper — couriers, provincial calendars, re-struck cash coins bearing the era name. A coin is a dated object minted inside its era's monopoly: fakes and rebels strike their own era coins, which is how archaeologists date usurpations.
- **What breaks at the seam (design this deliberately):** standing legal references ("era year 20" of a lapsed era), mintage, school records, insurance terms, software date tables. Japan industrialized the problem: JIS X 0301 defines a wareki date format, and every OS and database had to ship era-table patches for Reiwa — a modern transition ritual nobody scripted.
- **Vocabulary of the seam:** kaigen 改元 (Jp) / gaiyuan 改元 (Ch) "change the era"; jianyuan 建元 "establish an era"; issai ichigen 一世一元 "one reign, one era"; yuannian 元年 "Origin Year"; senso 即位 accession; zhengshuo 正朔 (§5).

## 5. Calendar as loyalty: zhengshuo, contested and colonized eras

- **Zhengshuo 正朔**, the "correct calendar and first day": issuing a new calendar (改正朔) was the founding act of a dynasty — Qin started its year in the tenth month; Emperor Wu's Taichu reform (104 BCE) reset the year to the first month. To *accept* another state's calendar (feng zhengshuo 奉正朔, "receive the correct first-day") is to acknowledge its legitimacy; running your own era in the same year as a rival's is open rebellion in the grammatical tense of time. Rival regimes routinely dated the *same year* with different era names, and official histories later "corrected" the losers' years to the winners'.
- Colonization and revanchism both play on the count: annexed Korea received Japanese nengō; the ROC kept Minguo years in Taiwan as a counter-calendar; restorations revive dead eras or found eras named "restoration" (Meiji 明治 itself can be read "enlightened rule" against the shogunate; Guangwu's Eastern Han restoration, 25 CE, and the Tongzhi Restoration 1862–74 are regime-saving periodizations).
- Craft application: give every faction in a war its own count, then let which calendar a neutral town uses be the loyalty tell. A merchant who writes both years on contracts is hedging; a temple that keeps the old count is a monument of resistance.

## 6. Dynastic cycles: mandate theory and the restoration vocabulary

- **The Mandate of Heaven (tianming 天命)** is Zhou-dynasty conquest ideology (c. 1046 BCE) systematized in Shujing chapters: Heaven grants rule to the virtuous, withdraws it from the corrupt, and signals the transfer through the people's welfare and military fortune. Mencius sharpens it into doctrine: the tyrant is no king — "I have heard of the execution of that one man Zhou, but not of the assassination of a ruler" (Mencius 1B.8). Regicide is thereby re-classified as personnel management.
- The folk **dynastic cycle** — vigorous founding, consolidation, high prosperity, fiscal-moral decay, rebellion, fall, restoration (zhongxing 中興, "restored prosperity," a term dynasties applied to themselves) — is an *emic* retrospective schema: powerful as the culture's self-understanding, unreliable as an analytic engine (real dynasties die of specific fiscal, climatic, and military causes; see §12). Modern echoes: "Ming restoration" politics in Taiwan and Hong Kong protest movements revived "restore the Ming" iconography — periodization as slogan.
- Design use: let your dynasty *believe* the cycle and act on it — pre-emptive era changes and ritual renewals to fake the "founding" phase; historians inside the world arguing over which phase they're in.

## 7. Translatio imperii: four monarchies, two cities, and the Ottoman mirror

- **Daniel's schema.** Nebuchadnezzar's statue and the four beasts (Daniel 2, 7) seed the four-monarchies doctrine; Jerome's commentary on Daniel (c. 407) fixes the sequence Assyria/Babylon → Medo-Persia → Macedon → Rome, with a fifth, eternal divine kingdom smashing the fourth. Medieval Latin Europe ran the **translatio imperii** — the *transfer of rule* — from Rome to the Franks (Charlemagne, 800) to the German emperors, systematized by Otto of Freising in his Chronicle of the Two Cities (c. 1146); Goez (1958) is the standard monograph. The twin concept **translatio studii** transfers *learning*: Greece → Rome → Paris, so Chrétien de Troyes can claim French chivalry as Rome's heir in the prologue to Cligès. Both are conquest-by-genealogy: a power argues it is not new but *transferred*.
- **The Byzantine continuatio.** Byzantium claimed to *be* Rome, not Rome's heir — no translation needed — and dated deeds by a triple formula: imperial regnal year, month, indiction (the 15-year tax cycle instituted under Diocletian), plus the Anno Mundi year (1 September, 5509 BCE epoch: Grumel 1958). Universal chronicles stacked all of it: AM + four monarchies + regnal years, so every date made a theological-political claim.
- **The Ottoman mirror.** Ottoman statecraft ran on the **circle of justice** (da'ire-i adalet: no ruler without soldiers, no soldiers without treasury, no treasury without the subjects' prosperity) in mirrors-for-princes like Kınalızâde's Ahlâk-ı Alâî — a cyclical health-of-state model instead of a linear decline. The *decline narrative* is a 16th–17th-c. genre (Mustafa Âlî's Counsel for Sultans, 1581; Kâtip Çelebi) and a modern historiographic habit that post-1980s scholarship has largely dismantled: Kafadar (1995) and Tezcan (2010) re-read the same centuries as transformation and constitutional change, not decay (§12).

## 8. The metal-age schema: archaeology's own periodization and its critiques

- **Origin.** C. J. Thomsen ordered the Copenhagen museum's collection Stone → Bronze → Iron in Ledetraad til Nordisk Oldkyndighed (1836), sorting *closed finds* by what artifacts occurred together; Worsaae (1843) confirmed the sequence stratigraphically (bogs, grave types) — a genuine scientific result for Danish prehistory. Lubbock (1865) split the stone phase into Palaeolithic and Neolithic; "Mesolithic" was added in the 1890s; a Copper Age (Chalcolithic) had to be inserted; Montelius (1885) refined bronze typology into relative phases.
- **The critiques, plainly.** (1) It was a European museum heuristic promoted to a *universal ladder* — teleological, technology-first, blind to social causation (Trigger 2006). (2) The ladder has no rung for cultures that skip stages: parts of sub-Saharan Africa have well-attested ironworking without a preceding local Bronze Age; the Americas developed metallurgy for ornament and never the schema's utilitarian Bronze Age. (3) "Ages" overlap and coexist: stone tools ran alongside bronze and iron for centuries; contact, not stage, explains distribution. (4) The names keep drifting (Chalcolithic, Eneolithic, "Paleolithic" as time vs. as techno-complex) because the underlying model was always relative typology wearing absolute-time clothes.
- Standing lesson for this lane: the Three-Age system is *etic* periodization (§1) that became a folk model in its own right — steampunk and fantasy "Iron Age culture" talk is 19th-century museum taxonomy, not how any ancient culture described itself. A fictional culture that *believes* it lives in a metal age is already doing something interesting (usually: naturalizing its own dominance, cf. Daniel's statue in §7).

## 9. World-ages doctrine: the cosmos keeps its own calendar

- **The West Asian/European line.** Hesiod's Works and Days 106–201 gives five races: Golden, Silver, Bronze, *Heroic* (inserted, breaking his own metal ladder — even the ur-text fudges its schema), Iron. Ovid (Met. 1.89–150) trims to four metals. Augustine's **six ages of the world** maps the six creation days onto six epochs, with Christ at the turn of the sixth and a sabbath millennium after the end; he rejected literal chiliasm (De civitate Dei XX.7) while Lactantius (Div. Inst. VII) kept the 7,000-year world-week popular. **Joachim of Fiore** (d. 1202) recut history into three *status* — of the Father (Law), the Son (Grace), the Holy Spirit (fuller love) — with the third age dawning c. 1260; the Joachimite wave (Gerardo's "Eternal Gospel" scandal of 1254, Spiritual Franciscans) is why Löwith (1949) traces modern progress-narratives to prophecy books.
- **Hindu yugas.** Four ages in 4:3:2:1 proportion — Krita/Satya, Treta, Dvapara, Kali — one mahayuga = 4,320,000 years; we live deep in the Kali Yuga, begun (traditional arithmetic) 3102 BCE, to be ended by the Kalki avatar (Vishnu Purana IV.24, VI). A *descending* moral saw-blade with scheduled renewal: degeneration is calendrical, not contingent.
- **Zoroastrian world-weeks.** The cosmos runs 12,000 years in four 3,000-year stretches (Bundahishn 1): creation, the mixed time, the victory time — with three world-renewing saviors (saoshyants) born in the last millennium of Zoroaster's preserved seed, and the final frashō.kereti (renovation) closing the count (Bundahishn 34).
- **Mesoamerican counts.** Aztec **Five Suns**: four prior worlds, each named by the day-sign of its destruction — 4-Jaguar (giants devoured), 4-Wind (hurricanes), 4-Rain-of-fire, 4-Water (flood) — with the present sun 4-Movement fated to end in earthquakes; the 52-year Calendar Round was ritually rebooted by the **New Fire** ceremony (fires extinguished nationwide, a new flame drilled at the temple on the victim's chest; Sahagún, Florentine Codex Bk VII). Maya courts ran the **Long Count** (creation 3114 BCE) and stacked k'atun endings (a k'atun = 20 tuns ≈ 19.7 years) as regime milestones; the Books of Chilam Balam attach prophecies to k'atuns — cyclical history as political almanac. The "Maya 2012 apocalypse" was a modern fabrication: Tortuguero Monument 6 commemorates the 13.0.0.0.0 date-ending with standard dedication language, no doom attached.
- Craft pattern: world-ages systems are *self-validating* — every disaster confirms "we are in the bad age," every renewal rite resets hope. They also give your culture a moral forecast and a date the priesthood dreads, which is plot.

## 10. Generation counts and scholarly scaffolds

- **Herodotus 2.142** states the folk rule outright: three generations = 100 years; he converts 345 generations of Egyptian priests into 11,340 years — genealogy as chronometric instrument, and a warning about its error bars.
- **Varro's system** (reported by Censorinus, De die natali 17, 21): the *saeculum* is the longest lived member of a generation (~100 years; Etruscan religion ran on divine saecula grants), and Varro cut all human history into **six epochs** bounded by: human origins → flood of Ogyges → flood of Deucalion → fall of Troy → first Olympiad (776 BCE) → founding of Rome (753 BCE) → his own day (consulship of Hirtius and Pansa, 43 BCE). Note the move: *epoch boundaries are famous catastrophes and famous contests*, i.e., periodization by memorable events, not by mechanism.
- **The Varronian AUC count** (ab urbe condita, "from the city's founding," 753 BCE) is a *modern-antique hybrid*: Romans in practice dated by consuls, not by AUC. Dating scaffolds that actually ran daily: Athenian archon years, Roman consular fasti, Assyrian limmu eponym lists (the canon anchored to the solar eclipse in Bur-Sagale's year, June 763 BCE — the pivot that lets us convert eponym years to absolute dates).
- **Era-as-epoch for long tables:** the Seleucid era (AG, 312/311 BCE) served the whole Near East into the medieval period; Ptolemy's Canon of kings back to Nabonassar (747 BCE) is the cross-strut that still props up ancient chronology; the Christian count starts with Dionysius Exiguus's Easter tables (525 CE) and became standard through Bede (De temporum ratione, 725), who also gave sustained use to "before Christ"; Byzantine AM 5509 BCE and rabbinic AM 3761 BCE (Seder Olam, 2nd c. CE) are competing creation epochs; the Hijri (622 CE) is a *founding-event* epoch, instituted under Umar by tradition.
- Craft application: genealogy-counting is your pre-numerate culture's archaeology — it works, accumulates legendary inflation (descent from gods), and is exactly how someone in-world would "date" a ruin.

## 12. Calibration: what is contested or retired

- **The dynastic cycle is emic, not analytic.** Use it as what your culture believes; do not let the narrator endorse it. Same for "the decline of Rome" habit-transfers into any fictional empire — decline narratives are retrospective moral bookkeeping (cf. the Ottoman case).
- **The Ottoman decline paradigm is disfavored** in post-1980s scholarship: 16th–17th-c. advice literature diagnosed decline, and modern historians (Kafadar 1995; Tezcan 2010) re-read the evidence as transformation. A worldbuilding trap: an in-world adviser citing "decline" may be lobbying, not observing — that ambiguity is realistic.
- **The Three-Age system is a 19th-c. European museum heuristic**, useful as relative chronology, misleading as universal ladder (§8). Its very success is a cautionary tale about etic labels becoming folk truth.
- **Millenarian computations always slip:** Joachimites' 1260, Lactantius's world-week, the Millerites' 1844 "Great Disappointment," the 2012 Long-Count fabrication. In-world prediction failure is a feature to exploit, not a bug to fix.
- The Xia–Shang–Zhou Chronology Project's dates (1996–2000) remain contested — even well-funded era-building is disputable, which is realistic texture for any in-world chronology commission.
- The Dangun-calendar dates above (1948–1961) are confirmed against the Korean era-name laws of 1948 and 1961 (see sources); all citations are standard references, primary texts, or statutes as listed.

## 13. Applying this to a fictional culture — a build order

1. **Pick the administrative layer:** era names (Sinosphere-style) if the state is strong and centralized; eponym years (consuls/limmu) if the elite is a rotating magistracy; regnal years only if reigns are short and document-filing is weak. Layer an indiction-style fiscal cycle if taxes matter.
2. **Pick the cosmic layer:** descending yuga-style ages (fatalist tone), rebooted bundles with renewal rites (Aztec tone), open-ended long counts (bureaucratic-astronomical tone), or a transfer-of-rule genealogy (imperial tone). The cosmic layer should disagree with the administrative layer at least once per generation.
3. **Write the era-change law before the first era name:** trigger (accession only? omens? disasters?), announcer, backdating rule (same-day vs next New Year), and what voids (coins? contracts? nothing?). Wu Zetian's dozen-plus eras and the Ming one-reign-one-era settlement are your two calibration poles; stability of era names is a *regime strength statistic*.
4. **Name like the house style of §3:** two morphemes, classics-quoting, never reused, screened against the ruler's personal-name taboo — then let scholars in-world fight over the source text. The *battle over the gloss* is free plot.
5. **Script the seam (§4):** a reveal object (board, coin, regalia), a phrase for the herald, and at least one broken artifact (a "year 64" calendar) that survives into the next era as a period detail.
6. **Arm the calendar as politics (§5):** vassals receive the suzerain's count; rebels mint their own era; a neutral town writes double dates; a conquest deletes the old count only if it intends to stay forever.
7. **Test the system:** does a historian inside your world use it to date a fire, a fraud, and a forgery — and can a rival use the same system to argue the dynasty is in its "silver age"? If yes, the periodization is load-bearing.
