## 1. The two founding laws

- **Kula's law** (Kula 1986): a measure is a *negotiated claim*, not a fixed quantity. The same town used different measures for buying and selling; "custom" — not metrology — settled quantity, and every standardization from above decayed back into local usage within a generation or two. The popular picture ("chaos until the enlightened state") is wrong in both directions: pre-modern measures were a workable *social order*, and modern standardization is a repeated, contested political project, not a one-time rescue.
- **The weighing drift** (named here): measure-words → money-words → pure numbers. *Pound, lira, talent, shekel, tael, mark* all began as physical weighings, became coin denominations, and survived as pure units of account after the metal left. Money-names routinely outlive their referents — the single most exploitable fact for fiction (see §4, §10).
- **Calibration**: units of account (the written livre/lira) were decoupled from actual coins early and everywhere (Spufford 1988; Kula 1986). Write ledgers that use a *different* money than the pouch: it is historically normal, not exotic.

## 2. Weights and measures: body parts, grain, casks, strings

- Four natural sources, in rough order of frequency: **the body**, **the substance measured** (grain, seed, casks), **the action** (a day's plowing), and **the container/counting technology** (strings, tallies).

| Unit | Source-word / source object | Note |
|---|---|---|
| cubit | Lat. *cubitum* 'elbow' | Egyptian royal cubit (~52.4 cm); cubit rods with divine/regnal inscriptions survive (e.g., the 18th-Dynasty Turin rod) |
| ell | OE *eln* 'forearm' (< PIE \**el-* 'elbow') | the cloth-measure; English ell = 45 in |
| fathom | OE *fæðm* 'embrace' | armspread; still the nautical depth word |
| span, hand, foot, pace | body parts | hand (4 in) survives in horse height |
| mile | Lat. *mille passus* 'a thousand double paces' | counting the soldier's walk |
| furlong | 'furrow-long' | length of the plow furrow before the ox turns |
| acre | what a yoke of oxen plows in a day | a *work*-unit pretending to be an area (Zupko 1977) |
| inch, grain | 3 barleycorns = 1 inch; the grain = a barley grain | medieval *Composition of Yards and Perches* (13th–14th c.); the troy grain survives (64.8 mg) |
| obol, drachma | Gk *obelos* 'roasting-spit'; *draxmē* 'handful' | ancient tradition: six iron spits = one handful (Kraay 1976; spit-hoards found in Greek sanctuaries) |
| carat | Gk *keration* 'little horn' → carob pod | jewel weight (200 mg, standardized 1907); *karat* = 1/24 purity in gold |
| talent, mina, shekel | Gk *talanton* 'balance'; Akk *manû* 'count'; Semitic *šql* 'weigh' | the sexagesimal ladder: 1 talent = 60 minas = 3,600 shekels |
| tun, pipe, butt, hogshead | cask names | the wine trade's volume ladder; *tonnage* < *tun* |
| tael, monme, mace | Chinese *liǎng* 'ounce'; *qián* (coin = weight) | East Asian money-weights; the *guàn*/kan = a strung 1,000 cash |
| grivna, ruble | Slavic neck-ring ingot; *rubit'* 'to chop' | ingots cut into change (Vasmer; standard Slavic etymologies) |
| mark | Gmc *marca*, a weight/coin of account | survives in Finnish *markka* |

- **The ladder is theology**: money- and measure-ladders encode sacred numerals — Babylon 60 (talent/mina/shekel), Carolingian Europe 12×20 (12 pence = 1 shilling, 20 shillings = 1 pound, from Charlemagne's reform; Council of Frankfurt, 794; in Britain until 1971), Rome 12 (*uncia* → both *inch* and *ounce*). Cf. corpus/knowledge/myth-symbol/number-symbolism.md.
- **Calibration**: "Troy weight" is linked to the Champagne fair-town Troyes by the OED only as a *probable* proper-name etymon (s.v. *troy* n.², earliest evidence c. 1390–1, in the Earl of Derby's accounts; entry not yet fully revised) — received opinion, not proof: the system's own origin is unknown, and a dialect *troi*/*tron* 'balance, weigh-house' derivation is on record (Wright, *English Dialect Dictionary* s.v. *troy*; Watson 1910: 82–83) without displacing the Troyes reading. Tower pound served English coinage until 1526, when Troy took over; *avoirdupois* < OF *aveir de peis* 'goods of weight' — the split of precious-metal weight from bulk-goods weight is itself a social fact worth copying.

## 3. Sacred standardization: the vow, the temple office, the inspector

- **The Bible's weight-commandments**: Lev 19:35–36 and Deut 25:13–16 make false measures an abomination; Amos 8:5 curses those who "make the ephah small and the shekel great"; Prov 11:1 — "a false balance is an abomination to the LORD." The scale is cosmological before it is commercial.
- **The Qurʾanic Balance**: Q 55:7–9 creates the cosmos with the *mīzān* and forbids transgressing it; Q 17:35 and 26:181–183 command full measure; sura 83 (*al-Muṭaffifīn*, "the defrauders") damns short-weighers. Institution: the **ḥisba** — the market inspectorate whose officer, the **muḥtasib**, tested weights, prices, and morals (Māwardī, trans. Wahba 1996; al-Shayzarī's 12th-c. inspector's manual, trans. Buckley 1999).
- **Mesopotamia**: inscribed royal stone/duck weights as god-kept standards; Hammurabi-style codes fix wages, fees, and hire rates and punish cheating (Roth 1995). Clean-slate edicts below (§7).
- **Rome's temple mint**: the temple of Juno **Moneta** (vowed 345 BC; Livy 6.20) became Rome's mint (from c. 269 BC) → Lat. *moneta* → *money*, *mint*, and (via Russian) even Azerbaijani *manat*. Named heuristic: **the Moneta move** — name money after the institution that guarantees it.
- **China**: the Qin unification of 221 BC standardizing weights, script, axle-gauges, and the round coin with square hole (Sima Qian, *Shiji* 6; Watson 1993). Tang law regulated market hours and fair dealing (Johnson 1979–97).
- **India**: Kauṭilya's *Arthashastra* (Bk 2) posts a *pautavādhyakṣa*, Superintendent of Weights and Measures, who stamps the royal seal on standard weights (Olivelle 2013).
- **Japan**: ritsuryō Japan copied Tang metrology; Hideyoshi standard-ized the *kyō-masu* measure box, re-standardized by the bakufu in 1669. Japanese *masu* later became the sake cup — measure-ware turning ritual (cf. costume/ceramic reuse).
- **Europe**: Magna Carta cl. 35 (1215) fixes wine, ale, corn, and cloth measures; the assizes of bread and ale (statutory 1266) enforce bread-by-price; imperial system 1824. **France, 1790s**: the metre as civic religion — Delambre and Méchain's meridian survey (Alder 2002), the slogan "for all people, for all time" (attributed to Condorcet) — standardization reframed from god's balance to the nation-as-humanity.
- **Design lever**: every culture needs *an office and a myth* for its standard — a temple, a god's balance, an inspector's title, a founding edict. The inspector (muḥtasib, assizer, fair warden) is an instant NPC generator.

**A. Money-words that are weighings** (the *pend-* family is the master set: Lat. *pendere* 'hang, weigh' → *spend, expend, pension, stipend* (stips 'offering'), *compensate*; *price, praise, precious* < *pretium* — Watkins 2011; OED):

| lira, livre | Lat. *lībra* 'scales, pound' | same drift in Italian, French, Turkish |
| peso | Lat. *pēnsum* 'weighed' | the Romance "weight-coin" (Spanish dollar = piece of eight) |
| penny, pfennig | Germanic *panning-* | origin obscure even to the dictionaries (OED) |
| talent, mina, shekel | 'balance, count, weigh' (§2) | Biblical "talents" are weights of silver, not skills |

| dinar, dirham, fils | Lat. *dēnārius*, Gk *drachmē* (via Aramaic), *follis* | early Islamic coinage calques Greco-Roman names — a whole monetary system in borrowed words |

| capital, cattle, chattel | Lat. *caput* 'head' | wealth counted by heads |

- **Design levers**: (a) apply the Moneta move — one culture's money carries a god's or dynasty's name, its rival's carries a marketplace inn's; (b) let coin-names *immigrate* (dinar/dirham show a whole system imported by calque); (c) keep the name, lose the metal — a "silver pound" paid in paper is free irony and period texture.

## 5. Marketplace registers: how bargaining talks

- **Geertz's suq** (Geertz, Geertz & Rosen 1979, on Sefrou): no posted prices and scarce information mean **haggling is price discovery**, not theater; traders escape the cost by **clientelization** — loyal buyer–seller pairs trading at "soft" prices. A market's etiquette (must the first offer be refused? may a broker state price aloud?) is its information economy made manners.
- **Polanyi's spectrum** (Polanyi et al. 1957): administered prices under palace/temple inspection at one end, port-of-trade neutral ground under truce at the other, competitive bazaar in between. Decide *where* on this spectrum each of your markets sits before writing a scene.
- **Registers without words**: silent trade — deposit goods, withdraw, no speech (Herodotus 4.196, Carthaginians; West African gold-for-salt, Bovill 1958); silent hand-grip bargaining under a cloth sleeve is attested across North African and Levantine markets — the price passes palm to palm, never aloud.
- **Time belongs to the church, then to the merchant** (Le Goff 1980): market bells vs church bells; Champagne's six fairs hang on the liturgical calendar, and the counts' fair wardens (*garde des foires*) enforce contracts under a fair-truce (Spufford 1988; Braudel 1982). A fair is a temporal exception — a licensed carnival of contract.
- **Role-words** (instant NPC lexicon): muḥtasib (inspector), dallāl (crier-auctioneer-broker), simsār (speculator-broker; Geniza trade, Goitein 1967), ṣarrāf → *shroff* (coin-tester/money-changer), mahājan (moneylender "great man"), Jagat Seth ('world banker', Bengal title), *bania* (trader caste < Skt *vāṇijya* 'commerce'), *esnaf* (Ottoman craft-body < Ar. *aṣnāf* 'categories'); English: *grocer* < 'engrosser' (buys en gros), *monger* < Lat. *mango* 'dealer (in slaves)' (OED), *chandler*, hawker, peddler.
- **Trade tongues and cants**: Swahili as caravan lingua franca (Nurse & Spear 1985); port pidgins; beggar-and-peddler cant codified by Harman (1566); Cockney rhyming slang out of 19th-c. East End market porters. Every heavy-trade contact zone breeds a register; *Hobson-Jobson* (1886) is the model in-world glossary.
- **Design lever**: pick each culture's *bargain grammar* — refuse-the-first-offer ritual, grip-under-cloth, auction chant ("going once…"), fixed temple price with an inspector — and let class decide who may use which register on whom.

## 6. Guilds and trade associations

- **Etymology first**: *guild* < OE *gild* 'payment' (cf. *geld* in Danegeld) — guilds began as feasting, cult, and mutual-insurance sodalities that later captured trade (Epstein 1991). Rome's *collegia* and *scholae*; German *Zunft*, *Innung*; *hanse* 'company, troop' (the Hanseatic League); London **livery companies** — *livery* = distributed robes, guild identity worn (cf. costume-semiotics).
- **The three ranks**: *apprentice* (OF *aprendre*, to learn) → *journeyman* (Fr. *journée*, paid by the day) → *master*, proven by the **masterpiece** (Ger. *Meisterstück*). The masterpiece is a ritual artifact: the object that makes a person.
- **Japan**: *za* 座 'seat' — temple- and shrine-sheltered guilds (e.g., brewers under Enryakuji; Adolphson 2000); Edo-period chartered **kabunakama** holding tradeable membership shares (*kabu* = share/stake — the same word as the stock exchange) (Hall, ed. 1991, vol. 4).
- **China**: *háng* 行 'row' → trade, firm, profession; the Canton *hong* merchants and the Cohong monopoly (Van Dyke 2005); *huíguǎn* native-place lodges as merchant infrastructure.
- **India**: *śreṇi* guilds as juristic persons with their own courts and dynastic endowments (Thapar 2002).
- **Islamic lands**: whether "guilds" in the European sense existed is a classic historiographical question — Geniza documents show craft groups and *muḥtasib*-regulated trades without corporate charters (Goitein 1967; Constable 1994). Ottoman *esnaf* are the closest functional match. Don't transplant European guild structures unexamined.
- **Quality as heraldry**: English hallmarking (statute of 1300; London date-letters from 1478) — assay office, maker, date punched in metal (Jackson 1905). A hallmark is a guild's signature worn by the object.
- **Calibration — the live debate**: Ogilvie (2019) reads European guilds as rent-seeking monopolies that damaged growth; Epstein (2000) defended guild training and quality coordination as growth-positive. Both are serious positions; pick your culture's guilds to argue one side.
- **Design lever**: give each craft body (i) a shelter (temple, shrine, crown, commune), (ii) a revenue logic (fees, chartered monopoly, tax farm), (iii) a worn identity (livery, badge, hallmark), and (iv) a rite of passage with an artifact (masterpiece, masterpiece-plus-feast).

## 7. Contract and debt vocabulary

- **Roman sale is a weighing**: *mancipatio* "per aes et libram" — by bronze and scales, five witnesses and a *lībrīpēns* scale-holder (Gaius, *Inst.* 1.119); the *stipulatio* is a formal question-and-answer oath ("Do you promise?" "I do"); *nexum* debt-bondage, abolished 326 BC (Lex Poetelia Papiria, Livy 8.28); all of commerce rests on *fides*, good faith. The scale-holder is a character type your culture should have.
- **Split-object authentication** (named heuristic: the teeth of the contract): Greek *symbolon* (two halves of a token, matched to recognize); English chirograph and *indenture* — the deed cut through a wavy "tooth" line, each party keeping a counterpart; Exchequer tally sticks split into stock and foil halves (Clanchy 1993; Jenkinson 1924). The cut object *is* the contract.
- **China**: contracts red (officially taxed and sealed) vs white (private); Dunhuang archives even hold *purchase deeds for the afterlife* — land bought for the dead buried in the tomb (Hansen 1995) — a perfect myth-symbol couple.
- **Japan**: *kishōmon* oath-scrolls sworn before deities, invoking divine punishment on breach — contract enforcement outsourced to the gods (attested medieval practice).
- **Clean slates**: Akkadian *andurārum/mišarum* — royal edicts canceling debts and freeing debt-servants; the Ammiṣaduqa tablet (c. 1646 BC) is the classic record (Hudson & Van De Mieroop 2002). Hebrew *dĕrôr* 'release' (Lev 25:10 — "liberty throughout the land," later cast on the Liberty Bell) institutionalizes the cycle as Sabbatical and Jubilee. Named heuristic: **the debtor's calendar**.
- **The pledge family**: *pledge, wage, wed, wedlock* all < Gmc \**wadją* 'pledge' (Watkins) — marriage vocabulary is security vocabulary; Germanic *mundr* bride-price in Grágás (trans. Dennis, Foote & Perkins 1980); Lat. *dōs* → *dowry, dot*. *Mortgage* < OF *mort gage* 'dead pledge' (vs Bracton's *vif gage* 'living pledge' that pays rent from itself) — a name that explains the instrument.
- **Usury registers**: Deut 23:19–20 licenses interest from the foreigner, not the brother — the ethnic geometry of credit; Aquinas, *ST* II-II q.78, damns usury, q.77 frames the just price (de Roover 1958 shows the scholastic just price was cost-of-production, not bargaining); Dante seats usurers in hell holding purses painted with their family arms (*Inferno* XVII) — hereditary money-bags, ready-made costume.
- **Design levers**: (a) decide who *witnesses* the oath — god, ancestor, king, tally — and your breach-penalty follows; (b) install a release festival and a regnal "year of release"; (c) name the pledge after what dies (dead pledge) or lives (living pledge).

## 8. Instruments: trade loans that move with goods

- **Risk grammar first** (named heuristic): every trade instrument is an answer to *who eats the loss if the ship sinks, the caravan robs, or the buyer defaults* — then the name is cut from the object that bears the risk.

| Instrument | Place/era | Risk allocation & naming logic |
|---|---|---|
| sea loan (*faenus nauticum*) | Greco-Roman | lender eats sea risk, charges explicit steep interest; Rhodian jettison law shares cargo loss pro rata (*Digest* 14.2, "de lege Rhodia de iactu" → modern "general average") |
| bottomry, respondentia | maritime, continuous to 19th c. | loan secured on the *bottom* (keel) or cargo — void if the voyage fails; the ship's body is the collateral |
| commenda / colleganza | medieval Italy | sedentary investor funds, traveling agent executes; profit split (2:1, 3:1); investor bears capital loss (Lopez & Raymond 1955) |
| qirāḍ / muḍāraba | Islamic trade | the same geometry, profit-share instead of interest; commenda–qirāḍ influence is debated (Udovitch 1970) |
| bill of exchange | 13th-c. Italy–Fairs–Bruges | four parties, two currencies, repayment *usance* fixed by route; interest hidden in the exchange rate — the usury-proof instrument (de Roover 1948) |
| fair-netting (*virements*) | Champagne fairs | multilateral clearing of claims at the fair court before packing up (Spufford 1988) |
| hundi | Mughal–colonial India | *darshani* (sight) vs *muddati* (term) drafts, endorsed and discounted along banker-caste networks (Chaudhuri 1978; Markovits 2000) |
| scrip & trade tokens | 17th-c. England, company towns | private small change and store credit when coin runs short — an economy's itch made visible |
| coffeehouse finance | London c. 1690s | Lloyd's (marine insurance), Jonathan's (stock jobbers → Stock Exchange 1773) — finance named after cafés (Dickson 1967) |

- **Design lever**: name the instrument after the thing that carries the risk (keel-loan, caravan-share, wintering-penny); give each route its own *usance* word, and let the exchange-rate spread be your culture's polite word for interest.

## 9. Calibration: folklore traps and contested terrain

- **"Roman soldiers were paid in salt"** — false. Only the *name* is ancient: *salārium*, linked to salt-money by Pliny (*NH* 31.41); no source shows payment in salt.
- **"Carob seeds are perfectly uniform"** — false; seed masses vary. The carat's stability came from institutional adoption, not botany.
- **"Money arose from barter"** — the standard origin story has no ethnographic support: barter appears *between* money economies, not before them (Humphrey 1985; Polanyi et al. 1957; Graeber 2011 ch. 2). Graeber's synthesis is influential but contested in detail by ancient historians — useful, cite carefully.
- **Guild teleology cuts both ways**: "guilds were proto-unions/progressive" and "guilds were pure rent-seeking brakes" are both live scholarly positions (Epstein 2000 vs Ogilvie 2019).
- **Silent trade is not a universal "primitive stage"** — it is a specific protocol for frontier exchange between non-speaking parties (Herodotus 4.196; Bovill 1958).
- **Etymological folklore**: "pay through the nose" (Viking nose-tax) — unsupported; pawnbrokers' three golden balls < Medici palle — a legend; German *Messe* 'fair' < *Missa* 'mass' — often said, unproven; "baker's dozen" from short-weight penalties — plausible, attestation gap; *sterling*'s star-penny vs Easterling story — open (OED); "pound of flesh" — no known real-world bond clause; it is literature (Shakespeare), which is exactly why it circulates.
- **Idioms are trade-technology fossils**: "worth one's salt," "on the nail" (exchange pillars; origin debated), "cash on the barrelhead" (tap-room cask), "on the cuff," "not worth a Continental" (1779 inflation). Collect these; they are free period texture, but check each before canonizing its story.

## 10. Design levers (the drills)

1. **The Moneta move**: name the currency after the guarantor — temple, dynasty, guildhall, or inn; the name is a claim about who stands behind the weight.
2. **Write the drift**: ingot-name → coin-name → paper-name, keeping the word (pound, lira, tael). Readers feel centuries of debasement in one vocabulary.
3. **Give the measure a vow**: the scripture-verse or founding edict that curses false weights (§3), plus the inspector's title and his street-level testing scene.
4. **Build a ladder with theology**: ratios 12×20, 60, or 8 — and let reformers fight over the ladder's sacred numbers (cf. number-symbolism).
5. **The debtor's calendar**: a fixed amnesty festival, a "year of release" in regnal dating, and the social memory of the last clean slate (write the ledger that survived it).
6. **Set the bargain grammar**: refuse-the-first-offer, grip-under-cloth, auction chant, or fixed inspected price — one per culture, class-marked.
7. **Risk grammar for instruments**: name credit-tools after the body that bears the loss (keel, bill, share-stick); write the route-usance table for your trade network.
8. **The teeth of the contract**: split tokens, tooth-cut deeds, tally halves — one authentication technology per administrative sphere, shared with marriage and oath.
