## 2. The four container jobs — a typology to fill per culture

Every vessel inventory in the world answers four questions, and most cultures specialise the *forms* per answer. Fill the table per culture; the empty cells are the design work.

| Job | What it must do | Real exemplar forms | Named institutional trace |
|---|---|---|---|
| COOKING | take fire and thermal shock; often round-bottomed for hearths, flat for stoves | Greek *chytra* (stew pot), *lopas* (pan); Japanese *nabe* 鍋, *kama* 釜; Chinese *ding* 鼎 (tripod cauldron — bronze, ritualised), *guo* 鍋 | the hearth/stove as architecture (see architecture-settlement-lexicon.md: doma, ondol/kang) |
| STORAGE | hold bulk, keep out pests and damp; large, immobile, often buried to the shoulder | Greek *pithos* (human-sized at Knossos; Christakis 2005 on Cretan storage systems); Roman *dolium*; Japanese *kame* 甕, *tsubo* 壺; Chinese *gang* 缸 (vat); granaries and silos | granary as tax and famine institution; the storeroom as power (Egyptian state granaries) |
| TRANSPORT | survive the road; shaped for stacking, tying, carrying; often standardised so it can be counted | Greek/Roman *amphora* (two-handled, pointed base, ~20–30 L classes; Peacock & Williams 1986); skin bag, waterskin; *masu* 枡 box; barrel/cask, firkin, puncheon | the customs seal, the shipper's stamp, the caravanserai warehouse |
| SERVING | present, pour, divide, mark status at the table | Greek *krater* (mixing bowl), *oinochoe* (wine jug), *kylix* (drinking cup), *phiale* (libation dish); Japanese *chawan* 茶碗, *donburi* 丼; Chinese *wan* 碗, *pan* 盤, *hu* 壺; English cup, bowl, jug, platter | table rank (who gets which cup); feast grammar (cuisine-food-lexicon.md) |

Extensions that are their own design problems, with attested anchors: **ritual** (Greek *phiale* and Roman *patera* for libation; Chinese bronze ritual sets — *ding*, *gui* 簋, *dou* 豆, *zun* 尊 — cast with dedicatory inscriptions, Bagley 1987; Rawson 1990), **funerary** (the pithos as coffin; the lekythos as grave gift; the cinerary urn), **cosmetic/perfume** (Greek *alabastron*, *aryballos*; the kohl pot — see adornment-body-codes.md), **documentary** (the sealed jar as a legal object; the amphora as a shipping container with a painted label — §8).

- Design lever: **a culture's identity can sit in one job.** A maritime trading culture's most elaborate vocabulary will be in TRANSPORT classes; a palace culture's in SERVING and RITUAL; a mountain farming culture's in STORAGE. Name the privileged job first, then let the other three borrow its morphology and its loanwords — that borrowing pattern is legible to in-world philologists and to readers.

## 3. Vessel-naming grammar — six strategies, with real name families

Vessel names are not arbitrary; they cluster into six derivational strategies. A culture's dominant strategies are readable evidence of what it thinks a vessel *is*.

| Strategy | Mechanism | Attested names | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| FUNCTION / verb-derived | the name says what the pot does | Greek *krater* "mixer" (from *kerannymi*, to mix wine and water); *oinochoe* "wine-pourer"; *amphora* "carried on both sides" (*amphi-* + *pherein*); *hydria* "water-er"; Japanese *nabe* 鍋 (pan/pot); Chinese *ding* 鼎 (cooking tripod) | the densest strategy in Greek and Japanese; yields transparent compounds |
| CAPACITY / measure-derived | the name says how much it holds; the vessel and the unit become one word | English *bushel*, *peck*, *pottle*; Japanese *masu* 枡 (the box that is also the unit); Chinese *dou* 斗, *dan/shi* 石; Roman *modius*; "a barrel of", "a sack of" | this is the bridge into trade-economy-lexicon.md's measure ladder — cross-read, don't duplicate |
| MATERIAL-derived | the name says what it is made of; often becomes the material's name | *porcelain* (the ware word becomes the material word); English *china* (place → material); *terra sigillata* "sealed earth"; Japanese *yakimono* 焼き物 "fired thing" (generic for ceramics); Chinese *ci* 瓷 (high-fired ware) vs *tao* 陶 (earthenware) | material names age differently from shape names — they survive kiln change |
| SHAPE-derived | the name describes the body | English *bowl*, *cup*, *basin*, *tub*; Greek *skyphos* (deep cup, "hollow"); Japanese *donburi* 丼 (deep bowl); Chinese *ping* 瓶 (bottle) | shape names are the most stable stratum cross-linguistically |
| EPONYM / toponym | named for a place, person, or dynasty | *delftware* (Delft), *majolica* (from Majorca, for the tin-glaze route), *faience* (Faenza — but see §9), *Jingdezhen* ware, *Wedgwood*, *kangxi* porcelain; "Nikosthenic amphora" (after the potter Nikosthenes) | the eponym stratum dates contact and fashion, and is exactly where modern scholarly labels masquerade as ancient names |
| LOAN / contact word | the artifact travels with its word | *jar* (via French *jarre* from Arabic *jarra*), *cask*, *amphora* itself (Greek into Latin into modern languages), *celadon* (a European coinage for a Chinese ware — §9), *teapot*'s whole family along the tea route | loans outlive the objects; the stratum is a contact date (language-contact.md) |

- **The emic/etic border is load-bearing.** Greek vase scholarship is the standard demonstration: some names in Richter & Milne (1935) are ancient words (*krater*, *amphora*, *hydria*, *lekythos*), others are modern conveniences for shapes ancient users may have named differently or not at all. Roman amphorae are named by modern *Dressel types* (Dressel 1, Dressel 20) — those type numbers are **etic**, the creation of 19th-century scholarship (Heinrich Dressel's *CIL* XV typology), not Roman usage. Rule: in a world bible, mark which vessel names are in-world words and which are the antiquarians' labels — the two carry different epistemic weight (to-verify per-name antiquity before printing any specific claim).
- **Naming politics**: what a culture calls a borrowed pot is a political act. The *qipao/cheongsam* pattern in garment-lexicon-crosslinguistic.md §2 recurs in ceramics: a ware can carry an ethnic tag, a place tag, and a shape tag simultaneously, and which one wins in each speech community is free plot.

## 4. Part-naming — the body metaphor is near-universal, so use it and let it fossilise

Potters' registers describe vessels as bodies. This is one of the most reliable cross-linguistic patterns in material vocabulary, and it couples directly to the body-metaphor grammar already documented for tools in craft-artisan-lexicon.md.

| Slot | English (potters') | Chinese | Japanese | Body sense |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| opening | mouth, lip, rim | 口 *kou* "mouth" | 口 *kuchi* "mouth" | mouth |
| above the body | neck, collar | 頸 *jing* "neck" | 頸/首 *kubi* "neck" | neck |
| widest part | shoulder, belly, body | 腹 *fu* "belly"; 肩 *jian* "shoulder" | 胴 *dō* "torso" | shoulder/belly |
| base | foot, footring, base | 足 *zu* "foot"; 底 *di* "bottom" | 高台 *kōdai* "high base"; 底 *soko* | foot |
| handle/lug | handle, lug, ear | 耳 *er* "ear" (lugs on bronzes) | 耳 *mimi* "ear" (lugs) | ear |
| spout | spout, beak | 流 *liu* "flow" | 注ぎ口 *sosogiguchi* "pour-mouth" | mouth/flow |

- The **ear** word for handles is the neatest fossil: it is attested in Chinese bronze terminology (鼎 with 耳) and in Japanese (*mimi*), and English potters use "lug" and "ear" side by side. Two unrelated languages landing on the same body part is a gift for worldbuilding — a conlang's vessel vocabulary can carry it, and an in-world etymologist can (wrongly or rightly) build a folk theory on it.
- **Design lever**: pick the metaphor and freeze it. Renaming a part after a technology change (e.g., wheel-throwing replacing coiling) is a real historical possibility but reads as noise unless it is a plot point. Attested pattern: the part name survives the technique that motivated it (the "foot" stays after the pot stops being a body).
- Parts also generate **defect vocabulary** (waster, kiln-crack, spall, crazing, pinhole) — a workshop register (craft-artisan-lexicon.md) that leaks into trade grading and then into slang.

## 5. The container becomes the unit — and then the money-word

- The chain is a special case of the metonymy already tracked in historical-linguistics.md: **container → capacity → price unit → pure number**. Real legs: the Roman *amphora* as a capacity class in shipping and taxation; the wine trade's *tun* → English *ton* (OED s.vv. tun, ton — not re-consulted this round; the precise 13th-century wine-trade chain is **to-verify**); the *bushel* as both basket-word and dry measure (Zupko 1977); the Japanese *masu* 枡 box, standardised as kyō-masu and re-standardised by the bakufu in 1669, later surviving as the sake cup — the measure-ware turning ritual object (corpus/knowledge/culture-material/trade-economy-lexicon.md, cited by path).
- **The container is the enforcement point.** A state that wants honest measure does not audit arithmetic; it audits *containers* — it issues the standard jar, stamps it, and inspects it. That is why the sealed standard vessel is an institution with a title attached (the inspector, the assize), and why a fictional culture's metrology story should be told through one object (see trade-economy-lexicon.md §3 for the inspector institutions themselves).
- Design lever: decide whether your culture's units are **filled** (volume), **weighed**, or **counted by container** — then decide who owns the standard container, and what happens to a merchant caught with a false one. The legal formula is cheap to lift; the scene writes itself.

## 6. Ceramic material classes — the vocabulary each rung of the ladder generates

The material ladder is a *vocabulary generator*: each rung adds words that lower rungs cannot have. Firing-temperature figures below are approximate and composition-dependent — treat them as ranges to design with, not constants to print (**to-verify** before use as hard numbers; ranges per Rice 1987 and Shepard 1956).

| Class | What it is | Firing (approx.) | Words it generates | Real anchors |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Earthenware | porous, low-fired clay body; often glazed or slipped | ~900–1100 °C | slip, engobe, burnishing, terracotta, pottery (generic) | Japanese *doki* 土器 / Jōmon ware (Habu 2004); Neolithic wares worldwide |
| Stoneware | vitrified, dense, non-translucent | ~1200–1300 °C | salt-glaze, ash-glaze, high-fire | Chinese *ci* 瓷 lineage; German/Rhenish stoneware; Japanese *tōki* 陶器 |
| Porcelain | vitrified, white, translucent; kaolin + a fluxing stone (Chinese *cishi*/petuntse) | ~1300 °C+ | kaolin, petuntse, blue-and-white, underglaze, overglaze, reign mark | Chinese porcelain from the Tang–Song onward; Jingdezhen; Kerr & Wood 2004; Vainker 1991 |
| Faience (Egyptian) | glazed crushed-quartz paste, NOT clay | low | faience, Egyptian blue | Egyptian amulets and vessels — a different material with a colliding name (§9) |
| Faience/majolica (European) | tin-glazed earthenware | ~950–1050 °C | tin glaze, majolica, delftware | Faenza, Delft, Hispano-Moresque lusterware |
| Fritware / stonepaste | ground quartz + glass frit + clay; made for a white body | ~1100–1200 °C | fritware, stonepaste | Islamic ceramics (Kashan, Iznik) |
| Celadon | a ware *name* (and a colour word), not a body class: iron-bearing glaze fired in reduction | high | celadon, greenware, crackle | Longquan (Chinese), Goryeo (Korean), Japanese and Southeast Asian greenwares; Krannert Art Museum (fetched 2026-10-17) notes the term is imprecise and covers various green-glazed wares |

- **Forming vocabulary** (chaîne opératoire per material-culture-inference-method.md): hand-building — pinching, coiling, slab-building, paddle-and-anvil; moulding; the **slow wheel** (*tournette*, a turntable for finishing) versus the **fast wheel** (true throwing; kick wheel, treadle wheel); Japanese *rokuro* 轆轤; industrial jiggering/jolleying. Gosselain (2000) is the calibration case: **technique is a choice, not a ladder** — potters in the same region may reject the wheel while their neighbours adopt it, and the choice signals identity.
- **Surface vocabulary**: slip, engobe, burnishing, glaze (lead, ash, feldspar, salt, tin), *terra sigillata* (Roman red-slip ware; the same term names a modern slip technique — do not conflate, §9), luster, underglaze vs overglaze, enamel.
- **Firing vocabulary**: open/pit firing, updraft kiln, downdraft kiln, cross-draft; the hill-climbing **dragon kiln** 龍窯 (*longyao*, Chinese) and its Japanese relatives *anagama*/*noborigama*; **oxidation vs reduction** atmosphere (the same iron oxide gives celadon green in reduction and amber in oxidation); kiln furniture (saggar), waster, kiln god / kiln prayer (craft-artisan-lexicon.md has the Pseudo-Homeric "Kiln Prayer" — do not repeat).
- Design lever: **the ladder is a budget, not a rank.** A culture with no porcelain has more, not fewer, vessel words in the lower rungs — and its status wares will be non-ceramic (metal, lacquer, glass, gourd, basketry). Choose the status material deliberately; do not let ceramics silently carry "civilisation".

## 7. Non-ceramic containers — the half of the inventory archaeology usually loses

Perishable containers dominate real inventories and vanish from the record (formation processes; the missing perishable majority — material-culture-inference-method.md cites Hurcombe 2014). A worldbuilder who wants an *archaeologically legible* world must decide what survives; a worldbuilder who wants a *living* world must decide what is actually used.

| Family | Forms | Vocabulary anchors |
|---|---|---|
| Basketry | coiled, twined, plaited, wicker; the basket as measure and as transport unit | Adovasio (1977) is the identification/analysis standard; "a bushel basket" fuses container and measure |
| Cooperage (wood) | barrel, cask, keg, firkin, puncheon, hogshead, butt, pipe; staves, hoops, coopers' marks | Zupko (1977) on the English wine/cask measure ladder; cooper as a guild trade |
| Metal | bronze ritual vessels (Chinese *ding*/*gui*/*dou*), brass and copper pots, tinned wares | Bagley (1987), Rawson (1990) for the ritual-bronze corpus and its inscriptions |
| Glass | cast and mould-blown Roman vessels, Islamic lustre glass, Venetian *cristallo* | glass as the fragile prestige container; couples to trade (trade-economy-lexicon.md) |
| Skin and textile | waterskin, leather bottle, sacks, bales, net bags | couples to textile-technology.md (fiber, dye, trade strata) |
| Gourd, shell, horn, bamboo | calabash, coconut shell, bamboo tube (also a document container) | the "found" container that costs no craft; its vocabulary is often inherited, not coined |

- Design lever: **the invisible-majority ratio.** State the ratio of ceramic to non-ceramic containers in daily use, then state which ones an in-world excavator would find. If the two lists are identical, the world is archaeologically naive; if they are wildly different, you have a built-in mystery for the world's own antiquarians (§8).

## 8. Containers as documents and as chronometers

Two facts make containers the most quotable artifacts in the world: they are **dated by their own inscriptions**, and they are **the classic index fossil of seriation**.

- **Documents written on containers**: Roman amphorae carried painted *tituli picti* (contents, weight, quality, shipper) and potters' stamps; terra sigillata carries *figlina* stamps that are standard dating tools (Peacock & Williams 1986; Johns 1971). Chinese porcelain carries **reign marks** from the early 15th century onward, with apocryphal earlier reigns as the standard connoisseur trap; the potter's stamp sits inside the signature typology at craft-artisan-lexicon.md §4 — **cross-read, not repeated here**. The worldbuilding point: a container label is a *small document with a legal weight*, and a culture's sealing/permitting apparatus (customs, excise, shippers' marks) will show up first on jars.
- **Chronometers**: Flinders Petrie's sequence dating of Naqada graves (1899) is the founding case of seriation — pottery types ordered into a relative sequence before any absolute date existed; Trigger (1989) places it in the historiography, Orton & Hughes (2013) give the working method. Named consequence for worldbuilding: **a period can be named after a pot.** *Jōmon* means "cord-marked", named for the decoration on its pottery (Habu 2004) — compare the metal-based Three-Age system in era-periodization-models.md; the two naming logics (pot-name vs material-name) produce different in-world origin stories for the same past.
- Design lever: if your world has antiquarians, decide **what they use as the index fossil** (pots, coins, inscriptions, bones) and let their period names follow that choice. Their disputes (typology wars, "our sequence vs theirs") are free, well-grounded texture.

## 9. Calibration — contested, discredited, and apocryphal

- **"Pots = people" is discredited as a default.** Pottery style and ethnic group are only loosely coupled; the culture-historical equation is not usable as evidence (Shennan 1989; the full treatment is in material-culture-inference-method.md §5 — do not restate, apply).
- **Pottery is not a farming or sedentism marker.** The oldest known pottery (Xianrendong Cave, ~20,000 BP; Wu et al. 2012) predates agriculture by millennia, and Jōmon hunter-gatherers were among the world's most prolific potters (Habu 2004). Any world-bible line of the form "the pot proves the village" is a defect.
- **The two wheels are unrelated.** The potter's fast wheel and the vehicle wheel are separate inventions with separate histories; wheel-throwing is not a rung on a cultural ladder, and its rejection can be a positive identity choice (Gosselain 2000). Cross-check against transport-mobility-lexicon.md before writing any "invention of the wheel" scene.
- **Celadon's etymology is genuinely unsettled.** At least three proposals circulate — from the shepherd Céladon in Honoré d'Urfé's *L'Astrée* (1610s), from a garbling of "Saladin", and a Sanskrit-based proposal (*sila* "stone" + *dhara* "green") — the Krannert Art Museum page (fetched 2026-10-17) lists the plurality and calls the origin unknown; the Sanskrit leg reads as a folk etymology. Print it as disputed or not at all. (OED s.v. celadon gives the d'Urfé line; OED not re-consulted this round — **to-verify**.)
- **"Porcelain" is a European word for a Chinese ware** (via French/Italian *porcelaine*, commonly derived from *porcellana*, a cowrie-shell name — OED s.v. porcelain, not re-consulted; **to-verify**). The Chinese terms *tao* 陶 and *ci* 瓷 do not map one-to-one onto the European earthenware/porcelain split; do not translate them as if they did.
- **Two unrelated "faience"**: Egyptian faience is a glazed quartz paste; European faience is tin-glazed earthenware named after Faenza. A false friend that has misled real scholarship — flag it in any glossary.
- **Terra sigillata is two things**: the Roman red-slip ware (Arretine/Samian; Johns 1971) and a modern slip-recipe technique named after it. Do not use one to explain the other.
- **Kintsugi's origin story is legend-grade.** The Muromachi-era Ashikaga Yoshimasa tea-bowl anecdote circulates widely but is a legend; the *practice* of gold-lacquer repair is documented on objects (the Krannert record includes a Goryeo celadon bowl, 918–1392, "ceramic with gold repair", object 1997-17-2). Use the object, mark the anecdote (**to-verify**).
- **Capacity figures**: "the" Roman amphora ≈ 26 L is a modern reconstruction (one Roman cubic foot); real classes vary by type and commodity (Peacock & Williams 1986). Pithos capacities likewise vary by site and phase (Christakis 2005). Never print a single number as *the* standard.
- **Amphorae are not "wine jars"**. They carried wine, olive oil, fish sauce (*garum*), olives, fruit and other commodities; the type, not the era, tells you the content (Peacock & Williams 1986).
- **Dressel numbers and other type names are etic** — modern typology, not ancient vocabulary (§3). The same caution applies to Chinese bronze "types" and Japanese kiln-site ware names.

## 11. Applying it to a fictional culture — build order

1. **Inventory the jobs** (§2 table): fill cooking, storage, transport, serving; then add ritual, funerary, cosmetic, documentary rows only where the culture's institutions need them.
2. **Pick the privileged job** (DR-1) and say why in one line — the reason should be ecological, economic, or political, never "it's traditional".
3. **Name the standard transport container** and give it a capacity class, a closure, and an owner. If the culture trades at distance, this object is now a character.
4. **Choose the naming strategy set** (DR-2): one dominant, one secondary; date the secondary stratum in the culture's own chronology.
5. **Build the part vocabulary** (DR-3): six slots (mouth, neck, shoulder/belly, base/foot, handle/lug, spout) plus three defect words; keep the body sense.
6. **Set the ceramic ceiling and the status material** (DR-5). Name the kiln (or the absence of one) and the firing atmosphere; if there is no kiln, name what the fire is (open hearth, pit).
7. **Write the perishable ratio and the survival ratio** (DR-6) — two different lists.
8. **Pick the document system** (DR-7) and the dating habit (DR-8): what does the culture write on a jar, and what would its own antiquarians sequence first?
9. **Test the coupling**: run the invented vessel list through the sibling decision tables — measure words against trade-economy-lexicon.md, classifiers against semantics-pragmatics.md, colour/glaze words against color-terminology.md, loans against language-contact.md.
10. **Calibrate before handoff**: strike any claim that a pot proves a people, a subsistence mode, or a level of civilisation (§9); mark remaining doubts `(to-verify)`.

**Worked micro-example (NON-CANON — a demonstration, not a world decision).** A river-delta culture (working label only) whose privileged job is STORAGE: its vessel vocabulary is dense in granary words (*kame*-style buried jars, above-ground vats), its transport container is a borrowed sealed jar with a foreign name (the loan stratum dates its river trade), its serving ware is unglamorous and mostly wood, and its status material is lacquered wood, not ceramic. Its pots are named by capacity (three standard sizes, state-issued, inspected at the landing) — so its measure system is a *container* system, its inspectors are a named office, and its antiquarians would date their own past by jar sizes, not by metals. Every one of those consequences was derived from DR-1 plus DR-4; none of it is a canon claim.
