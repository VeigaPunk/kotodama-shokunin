## 1. Core framework — what a flora register does that a fauna register cannot

- **The gap statement.** This partition covers fauna in depth (`animal-symbolism.md`: totemism, guardian animals, heraldic beasts, zodiac day-signs, serpent dualism) and color, number, and celestial registers each have their own files, but no file covers plants. That matters because flora is the register most cultures load with the *axis*, the *calendar*, the *boundary*, and the *identity* functions at once — and because a worldbuilder who has designed animals but not plants has left the single most visible seasonal object in the landscape undesigned.
- **Four properties of plants generate the symbolism, and they are not interchangeable:**
  1. **Stationary** — a plant cannot flee, so it can be owned, inherited, fenced, taxed, and used as a boundary marker in a way an animal cannot. This is the property that makes groves legal objects (§4) and trees witness marks.
  2. **Perennial and regrowing** — the same individual returns after dormancy or cutting, which is why vegetation carries death-and-return, resurrection, and dynastic-continuity meanings more readily than animals do (a felled coppice stool resprouts; a dead sheep does not).
  3. **Seasonal and legible in advance** — flowering and fruiting are *schedulable*: they can be watched for, announced, and waited on, which is why the flora register is where a culture stores its calendar hooks (§7).
  4. **Multi-purpose and therefore morally ambiguous** — one plant can be food, medicine, poison, fiber, dye, and drug at once (the same *Papaver*, *Ricinus*, or *Taxus* is a pharmacy and a weapon). The plant is therefore the natural carrier of the *ambivalence* that `symbolism-synthesis-method.md` needs a symbol to hold: it can be a blessing and a hazard under one name.
- **Craft maxim: the flora register is chosen by subsistence, not by taste.** A culture's symbolic plants are overwhelmingly its *staple* plants and its *dangerous* plants — the two sets its labor calendar and its mortality are organized around. Design sequence: fix the staples, then the hazards, then decorate. Inverting that order produces the commonest failure in fantasy flora — symbolic trees that no one farms.
- **Two registers, one machinery.** Everything `animal-symbolism.md` says about the anomaly rule (the category-straddler carries the pollution load) transfers to plants with a different trigger: a plant that breaks the local taxonomy (flowers without fruit, fruits without seed, a "tree" that is a grass, a food that is also a poison) is where the taboo, the omen, and the ritual use will cluster. Do not re-derive the machinery; couple to that file and change the trigger.

## 2. The flora register's four jobs (working typology)

| Job | What the culture asks of the plant | Attested exemplar | Design question to answer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Axis | "Where is the center of the world?" | Yggdrasil (ash); Iroquoian Tree of Peace (white pine); Maya ceiba (yaxcheʼ); inverted aśvattha (*Bhagavad Gita* 15.1) | Tree, mountain, or pillar — and can both exist without contradiction? (§3) |
| Boundary | "Where does the human order stop?" | sacred groves (§4); tree-felling taboos; boundary/witness trees | Who may enter, who may cut, and who enforces? |
| Clock | "What time is it, and what do we do now?" | Pleiades rise → harvest, set → plough (Hesiod, *WD* 383–385); blossom fronts (§7); first-fruits | Which plant announces which season, and who is licensed to read it? |
| Identity | "Who are we?" | clan and lineage plants; national plants; the Four Gentlemen (§6); lover's-flower codes | Which plant is on the banner, the grave, and the wedding, and are they the same plant? |

- **The four jobs conflict, and the conflict is content.** A staple crop cannot easily also be the untouchable sacred tree: the culture that eats its axis plant has to invent a rule to keep the two apart (a separate sacred variety, a taboo on the first-fruits only, or a myth in which the god permits harvesting). Write the rule, not the contradiction.
- **Do not assign all four jobs to one plant** unless you want a monomaniac culture; the richest real systems distribute them (a grain for the clock, a tree for the axis, a flower for identity, a grove for the boundary).

## 3. The axis solution family — world tree, cosmic mountain, pillar

- **The tree is one solution among three**, and `cosmology-cosmography.md` §2 already fixes the general rule: a center must be verifiable in architecture and ritual, not myth alone (its calibration cites J. Z. Smith 1987 against Eliade's universal *axis mundi*). The flora-specific version of that rule: a world tree in the myth needs a *cultivated* counterpart in the world — a grove, a planted courtyard tree, a procession route around a trunk — or it is decoration.
- **Attested axis trees, with the exact claim each can carry:**

| Tree | Culture / source | What it is actually attested as | Craft use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yggdrasil, the ash | Norse; *Völuspá* 19 (ash, "sprinkled with white loam", ever green over Urðarbrunnr); *Grímnismál* 31 (three roots: Hel, frost-giants, humans); Snorri, *Gylfaginning* 15–16 (three roots: Æsir, frost-giants, Niflheim; wells Urðarbrunnr, Mímisbrunnr, Hvergelmir) | A tree that is the gods' assembly place and the cosmos's load-bearing member; the *number and destination of the roots differ between the poem and Snorri* | **Do not harmonize the variants.** A culture's own exegetes disagreeing about the world tree's anatomy is free, realistic texture — and the disagreement is a faction generator |
| Tree of Peace | Haudenosaunee (Iroquois) tradition; Parker (1923); NMAI *Haudenosaunee Guide* | A white pine planted over buried weapons, with four white roots to the four directions and an eagle on top; the Great Law's emblem | The axis as *treaty*: the tree that makes peace enforceable — the best model in this file for a political cosmology |
| Ceiba / yaxcheʼ | Maya; Landa (c. 1566); Freidel, Schele & Parker (1993), as cited in `cosmology-cosmography.md` and `number-symbolism.md` §4 | The "first/green tree" at the center of a four-quartered world upheld by the Bacabs, with color-quarters (east red, west black, north white, south yellow, center green) | The axis that is also a *map*: couples directly to the direction register, and to a real tree species a culture can plant |
| Inverted aśvattha | Vedic/Epic India; *Bhagavad Gita* 15.1–2; *Katha Upanishad* 2.3.1 | A fig tree with roots above and branches below — the world pictured *upside down*, rooted in the transcendent | The inversion lever: a culture can draw the world with its roots in heaven, and its iconography, burial orientation, and temple plans follow |
| Bodhi tree | Buddhist; *Ficus religiosa* at Bodh Gaya; Mahabodhi Temple complex (UNESCO list 1056, inscribed 2002); Ashoka's 3rd-c. BCE marking of the site; the standing tree described by UNESCO as a "supposed direct descendant of the original Bodhi Tree" | The tree under which the Buddha awakened — a *historical*, locatable tree, whose successors are vegetatively propagated (cuttings carried to Sri Lanka and to temple gardens worldwide) | The axis that is a *place with a lineage*: a pilgrimage tree whose clones are relics — an institutional machine for a fictional religion, and a lesson in stating descent claims as claims |
| Tree(s) of the garden | Hebrew Bible; Genesis 2:9 (tree of life and tree of knowledge); 3:22–24 (guarded way); Revelation 22:2 (twelve fruits, leaves for the healing of nations); Ezekiel 47:12 | Two named trees whose *prohibition* organizes the narrative, and a restored tree at the end of the story | The axis as *prohibition*: the tree you may not eat is a more portable worldbuilding device than the tree you may climb |
| The cross as tree | Christian; Deuteronomy 21:22–23 → Galatians 3:13; Acts 5:30; *The Dream of the Rood* (Vercelli Book, 10th c.) | The instrument of execution described with tree vocabulary, and in the Old English poem the *rood itself speaks as a tree* | The axis as *reused material*: a culture can convert its execution device into its world tree — an era-drift mechanism (§9) |
| Peaches of immortality | Daoist China; *Xiwangmu*'s orchard in later Han and medieval accounts (locators `to-verify`) | Fruit, not tree, as the immortality object; the orchard is guarded and ripens on a long cycle | The axis as *orchard*: an axis you can eat, with a bureaucratic guard — couples to `calendar-festival-systems.md` |

- **Selection rules (use as a checklist):**
  1. Pick **one** axis solution as load-bearing; the others may exist as *metaphors* or *rivals* (a mountain culture that calls its king "the tree" is telling you the tree is subordinate).
  2. Give the axis a **species** and a **location** — an unnamed world tree is a mood, not an institution.
  3. Give it a **failing** — Yggdrasil is attested as suffering (a hart bites it, it decays on the sides, Níðhöggr gnaws beneath; *Grímnismál* 35, *to-verify* for the exact stanza) — a world tree that cannot be damaged gives the culture no eschatology and no maintenance ritual.
  4. Decide **who owns it** (priests, a dynasty, a confederacy council) and **who may approach it**.

## 4. Sacred groves and tree institutions — the boundary job

- **The grove is a legal technology, not scenery.** Across unrelated cultures the same structure recurs: an uncut patch of vegetation, a rule against cutting, a specialist who may enter, and a sanction (divine or royal) that makes the rule stick.
- **Vocabulary and its traps:** Latin *lucus* ("grove", with the famous false etymology *lucus a non lucendo*); Greek *alsos*; Gaulish *nemeton* (attested in place names and the Celticist literature; exact epigraphic range `to-verify`) → Modern Irish/Welsh *nemed*/[*nyfed*] cognates. **Never translate all of these as "temple"** — a grove is an *unbuilt* sacred place, and its rhetorical force comes precisely from the absence of architecture.
- **Attested grove institutions and what each demonstrates:**

| Grove / institution | Culture | Mechanism worth stealing |
|---|---|---|
| Dodona (oak oracle) | Greek (Epirus); Herodotus 2.55–57 on the sanctuary's origin story; the oracle's oak and its doves | The grove as *oracle*: prophecy attached to a specific tree, staffed by priestesses, consulted by states |
| Osun-Osogbo Sacred Grove | Yoruba (Osogbo, Nigeria); UNESCO list 1118, inscribed 2005; ~75 ha along the Osun River; 40 shrines and sculptures, restored from the 1950s by Susanne Wenger's circle; ~400 plant species, 200+ with recorded medicinal use | The grove as *living parish whose police force is taboo*: traditional laws forbid fishing, hunting, poaching, tree-felling and farming inside it, which is why the forest survived |
| *Chinju no mori* (shrine forests) | Japan | The grove as *residue of un-development*: the shrine's forest is often the only old-growth patch left, so the sacred rule accidentally conserves ecology |
| Indian sacred groves (*devara kadu*, *sarna*, etc.) | South Asia | The grove as *community property with a deity as the title-holder* — the deity holds the land, which is why it survives partition and sale |
| Roman *lucus* / Greek *alsos* | Mediterranean | The grove as *precinct boundary*: the point at which civic law changes and ritual law begins |

- **Grove politics is a modern plot line with real precedents.** Two documented confrontations show the same structure — a community body-shields trees against a state or commercial claim:
  - **Khejarli, 1730** (Rajasthan): Bishnoi villagers, led by Amrita Devi, died protecting *khejri* (*Prosopis cineraria*) trees from the Maharaja of Jodhpur's felling party; sources give **363** martyrs (some accounts 366–367 — treat the exact number as contested and cite the range).
  - **Chipko, 1973** (Uttarakhand, then Uttar Pradesh): villagers, prominently women at Reni under Gaura Devi, embraced trees to stop commercial felling; associated with Chandi Prasad Bhatt and Sunderlal Bahuguna; the name means "to hug/embrace". The later movement's claimed descent from the Bishnoi precedent is a *modern historiography* claim — useful as an in-world charter narrative, not as an unbroken lineage.
- **Grove design checklist:** (1) who may cut, and under what exception (ritual need, storm-fall, deadwood)? (2) who may *enter*, and does the rule differ by sex, rank, or season? (3) what happens to a violator (this is where the culture's real theology shows)? (4) what does the grove produce that the economy wants (timber, honey, game, medicine) — a grove nobody wants anything from has no tension.

## 5. The vegetation-deity complex — and the calibration that must ride with it

- **The classical picture.** Mannhardt (1875–77) read European harvest custom as the ritual killing of a "corn spirit" embodied in the last sheaf; Frazer (1890; 3rd ed. 1911–15) generalized it into the *dying and rising god* and the sacred king who must be killed when his strength fails. This is the most seductive framework in the file and the one most likely to contaminate a world bible.
- **The calibration (state it on first use):** J. Z. Smith (1987; 1990) demolished the category as a comparative construct — "the category of dying and rising deities is exceedingly dubious", built on Christian-interest-driven reading; Mettinger (2001) *The Riddle of Resurrection* re-examined the Near Eastern evidence and defended a *stricter, criteria-bearing* version of the category rather than Frazer's. **Status: the Frazerian universal is discredited; a narrow, evidence-bound category is contested-but-defensible; the corn-spirit reading of any given harvest custom is a hypothesis, not a finding.**
- **What survives as craft, stated without the universal:**
  - **The seasonal god slot.** A culture with grain agriculture commonly has *some* figure or ritual that handles the seed/burial/return sequence (Osiris as the dismembered and reassembled body — `civilizations/egypt-ancient.md`; Dumuzi/Tammuz's descent and the theory history — `myth-symbol/myth-ritual-theories.md`; Persephone's alternating residence — `civilizations/greece-classical.md`). Design the slot by function, then decide whether the figure "dies and rises" at all — many do not, and a culture whose god merely *withdraws* is perfectly coherent.
  - **The king-as-crop lever.** If rule and harvest are coupled (the king's virtue = the land's yield), then a bad harvest is a political crisis and a drought is a legitimacy test — an excellent plot engine that needs no universalism.
  - **First-fruits and prohibition.** The rule that the first sheaf is not eaten by its grower (offered, or reserved for a shrine) recurs widely and is *checkable per culture* — treat it as a design option with real analogues, not a law of religion.
- **Anti-pattern to ban in the handoff:** "the people worshipped a vegetation god because they were farmers." Subsistence does not dictate theology; it constrains the calendar the theology has to fit.

## 6. Flower codes — the identity job, and its modern forgeries

- **The Ottoman *selam* → Victorian floriography chain is documented, and its romanticization is too.** Lady Mary Wortley Montagu's letter of 1718 (published 1763) described an Ottoman coded communication in which objects and flowers carried verses; European writers then built a *systematic* flower code: French and English "language of flowers" manuals in the 1810s–1830s, culminating in illustrated gift books such as Greenaway's *Language of Flowers* (1884). **Calibration:** the claim that floriography is *ancient* (Egyptian, Greek, or "secret" harem lore) is a 19th-c. marketing frame; the *harem* framing itself is Montagu's and her readers' exoticism. Use the mechanism (a code that is deniable because it is only a bouquet), not the pedigree.
- **A flower code needs four parts to be usable in fiction:** (1) a *lexicon* (which flower = which meaning, with the ambiguity tolerated); (2) a *grammar* (arrangement, color variant, handedness, whether it is presented or sent); (3) an *enforcement* (is the code licit, tolerated, or a subversion of a stricter regime — this is where it becomes a plot device); (4) an *era stamp* (a code that requires cheap imported flowers is post-trade; a code built on a single local bloom is village-scale).
- **Attested flower codes and systems to model on:**

| System | Culture | Structure | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Four Gentlemen (*mei lan zhu ju* 梅蘭竹菊) | Chinese literati painting | Plum = winter/late winter (perseverance), orchid = spring (seclusion/virtue), bamboo = summer (integrity, flexibility), chrysanthemum = autumn (retirement/reclusion); standard brush subjects since the Song | The *virtue* attributions are a literati discourse, and the season mapping is stated consistently in the reference literature; do not treat the set as a folk calendar |
| Hanakotoba | Japan | Flower-meaning lexicon | The codified "hanakotoba" list is largely a modern (20th-c.) product; do not backdate it as an Edo-era system (`to-verify` for the specific claim you intend) |
| Victorian floriography | Britain/France/US | Gift-book code with variant lexicons that *disagree between editions* | Editions disagree — a real, useful ambiguity; do not present one list as "the" Victorian code |
| Lotus | Egypt / India / Buddhist Asia | Purity rising unstained from mud; the Buddha's seat; Egyptian art's blue water lily | **Botanical trap:** the Asian sacred lotus is *Nelumbo nucifera*; the Egyptian "lotus" of art is usually a *Nymphaea* water lily. A world that uses "the lotus" for both a river symbol and a throne must decide whether they are the same plant — and Homer's *lotus-eaters* (*Odyssey* 9.82–104) are a *third*, unidentified plant |
| Olive / laurel / palm | Mediterranean | Olive = Athena's gift, peace, the oil economy; laurel = Apollo/Daphne, the triumphator's crown, hence "laureate"; palm = triumph and martyrdom | These are *institutional* plants: their meaning rides on offices, not feelings — steal the institution, not the sentiment |
| National plants | Modern states | Shamrock (Ireland), thistle (Scotland), leek/daffodil (Wales), maple (Canada), sakura (Japan), protea (South Africa), edelweiss (Austria) | National plants are 19th–20th-c. *inventions with medieval raw material* (the thistle's Norse-attack legend is legend, not record); the invention date is the interesting fact |
| Fleur-de-lis | France and beyond | Royal emblem from the 12th c. | Botanical identity is **contested** (iris vs lily vs stylized spearhead); do not assert "it is an iris" in a bible |

- **Plant parts carry different grammars than whole plants** — a craft distinction the partition previously had no place to store: the *branch* is an offering and a claim (olive branch, laurel branch); the *wreath* is an office; the *seed* is a promise and a debt; the *fruit* is a consequence ("by their fruits"); the *root* is ancestry and cause. Fix which part your culture's ritual actually uses; "flowers" as a generic category is the least interesting choice.

## 7. Botanical clocks, omens, and invented traditions

- **Plants are the cheapest calendar a culture has, and the least accurate.** Hesiod's agricultural year runs on the Pleiades (rise → harvest, set → plough; *Works and Days* 383–385, as cited in `celestial-symbolism-calendars.md`), i.e. on a *star* read against a *crop* — the coupling of sky and flora is the normal pre-modern arrangement, and the flora half is what people actually watch. Design rule: give the culture one *announcing* plant per agricultural act (sowing, weeding, harvesting, storing), and let the priests own the announcement.
- **Blossom culture as engineered spectacle — the best documented case:**
  - Japanese *hanami* has Heian-period court roots (the shift from plum to cherry as the preferred blossom), became mass practice in the Edo period as the shogunate planted cherries in public sites (Tokugawa Yoshimune's plantings along the Sumida and at Asukayama, 1717–1737, per Izutsu 2023), and is now a *national* ritual.
  - The dominant cultivar, **Somei-yoshino**, originated in the mid-19th century in Somei village (Edo) and is propagated by grafting — so the trees are **clones that bloom simultaneously and identically**, which is precisely why a nationwide "blossom front" forecast is possible and why the spectacle is uniform.
  - The modern blossom front is a *meteorological product* (a forecast service, a tourism economy, a news ritual) — a fully documented example of a plant becoming an infrastructure.
- **Invented traditions — calibrate before use:**

| Claim | Verdict | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| "The Christmas tree is a pagan survival" | **Modern construct.** The custom is attested as a *Christian German* practice, with the earliest clear record in 1605 (Tille, quoted in *Elsevier's Dictionary of Plant Lore*); earlier pagan-tree explanations are retrospective | Hutton's critique of pagan-survival narratives; Tille chronology |
| "Prince Albert introduced the Christmas tree to Britain" | **Myth.** Queen Charlotte (German wife of George III) set up the first known English tree c. 1800; Albert's and Victoria's 1840s trees *popularized* an existing German custom | The 1848 illustrated press coverage is the popularization moment, not the origin |
| "The maypole is an ancient pagan fertility survival" | **Contested-to-constructed.** Maypoles are attested in medieval England; the "ancient pagan" reading is a 19th–20th-c. folklorist construction, and Hutton's *Stations of the Sun* dismantles most such survivals | Use a maypole in-world only with a stated medieval-style attestation date |
| "The Celtic tree calendar / ogham birth-trees are ancient" | **Modern invention (1948).** Robert Graves's *The White Goddess* constructed the thirteen-month tree calendar; ogham is a real 4th–6th-c. Irish script with medieval letter-name traditions, but the calendar is Graves's | Celticist critique; do not import it as ancient |
| "Jack-o'-lanterns are ancient" | **Composite.** The carved-vegetable practice is Irish (turnips/beets) and the pumpkin substitution is North American; the assembly is 19th-c. and later (`to-verify` for the specific chronology you cite) | Treat as a modern-diaspora construction |
| "The druids worshipped trees" | **Over-read of one Roman source.** Pliny, *NH* 16.95 is the locus for oak and mistletoe, including the white-robed priest, golden sickle, and two white bulls; the oak's primacy and the druids' "tree alphabet" are later accretions | One source, cited precisely, beats a folklore composite |

- **Omens.** Plant omens are cheap to design and easy to overdo. The attested patterns worth copying: a *failure* omen (blossom out of season, fruit with no seed, a tree flowering twice — the anomaly rule again), a *count* omen (petal or fruit counts, which couples to `number-symbolism.md`), and a *direction* omen (which quarter a tree falls toward). Do not build a plant-omen system without a licensed reader; an omen nobody is paid to interpret is just weather.

## 8. Plant-part metaphor grammar and the tree as a knowledge diagram

- **The root/branch/fruit cluster is a ready-made metaphor engine** and it is *language-specific*: English "root" covers ancestry, causation, and etymology; "branch" covers descent, office, and bureaucracy (a branch office, a branch of government); "fruit" covers consequence and offspring. A conlang that wants botanical metaphor must decide which of these slots its own plant vocabulary fills — and it need not fill all of them.
- **The tree is also a *diagram*, and this is the most transferable item in the file for a literate culture:**
  - Porphyry's tree — the tree as a classification diagram (genus → species by successive division) in the Neoplatonic tradition;
  - the Tree of Jesse (Isaiah 11:1; medieval iconography) — genealogy as a tree, the standard Western descent diagram;
  - Darwin's only diagram in the *Origin* (1859) — descent with modification drawn as a branching tree;
  - library and legal "trees" (a table of descents, an inheritance chart).
- **Craft use:** decide *which* knowledge your culture draws as a tree (kinship, rank, law, the gods' genealogy, the categories of plants) — because a tree diagram is a claim that the categories share one trunk, i.e. an ideology. A culture that draws its pantheon as a tree has already decided the gods are one family; one that draws it as a council has not.
- **Language coupling:** botanical classifier systems, plant-name etymologies (many plant names are transparent compounds — "ink-berry", "sword-fern"), and the taboos around naming or harvesting (couple to `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md`). Plant names are also the commonest source of *personal* names and toponyms (`naming-systems.md`): a culture with plant-name lineages has built its flora register into its onomastics.

## 9. Cross-register coupling and era drift

- **Couple the flora register to the other registers deliberately, not by coincidence:**
  - **Color** — dye plants set the *makeable* palette (madder, indigo/woad, kermes vs cochineal; `textile-technology.md`), so a color code's enforceability depends on a plant list. A "royal blue" that requires an imported plant is a trade-policy object.
  - **Number** — petal and fruit counts (5-petal Rosaceae, the 4-petal crucifer cross), flowering-day counts, harvest-day counts (couples to `number-symbolism.md`, whose §5 wuxing table already binds wood to east).
  - **Direction** — the quarter assignment of plants follows the culture's direction code; the wuxing table in `number-symbolism.md` §5 is the worked example (wood = east), and the Maya ceiba is the worked example of an axis that *is* the map.
  - **Animal** — habitat, feed, and pollinator: the animal register's fauna need a flora to live in, and mythic animals usually eat, hide in, or are born from a named plant (the silkworm/mulberry pairing is in `textile-technology.md`).
  - **Celestial** — the Pleiades/crop pairing (§7) and the blossom-front-as-calendar mechanism.
  - **Material** — timber, fiber, fodder, and fuel: a culture's plant list *is* its material list (`architecture-settlement-lexicon.md`, `cuisine-food-lexicon.md`, `trade-economy-lexicon.md`).
- **Era drift mechanisms specific to flora (six, each dateable in-world):**
  1. **Cultivar change** — a new variety replaces an old one (Somei-yoshino is the worked example: a mid-19th-c. cultivar that became the national tree).
  2. **Introduction by trade** — a plant arrives with its name (loanword strata: `historical-linguistics.md`, `language-contact.md`).
  3. **Botanical institution** — physic gardens, herbaria, and imperial plant-hunting turn flora into state science (couple to era files).
  4. **Nationalization** — a plant is legislated or mass-planted as an emblem (§6).
  5. **Disease or blight** — a single-clone or single-crop system fails all at once (the clone's uniformity is a vulnerability, not just a spectacle).
  6. **Invention of tradition** — a modern reading retrofits an ancient pedigree (§7's table).

## 10. Build protocol — the flora register in eight steps

1. **Fix subsistence** — staples, hazards, and the labor calendar; list five to ten plants the culture actually grows or fears.
2. **Choose the axis solution** — tree, mountain, or pillar; give the axis a species and a location, and give it a failure mode (§3).
3. **Install the boundary** — one grove or sacred precinct with cut/entry rules, a violator sanction, and an economic temptation (§4).
4. **Assign the clock** — one announcing plant per agricultural act; decide who reads it and whether the reading is contested (§7).
5. **Decide the seasonal-god slot** — by function, not by Frazerian template; state whether the figure dies, withdraws, or merely sleeps (§5).
6. **Build the identity code** — a flower or plant-part lexicon with grammar, enforcement, and era stamp; decide the national/lineage plant and *when* it was adopted (§6).
7. **Couple the registers** — write the one-page map from this register to color, number, direction, animal, celestial, and material; resolve conflicts by assigning one primary domain per plant (§9).
8. **Audit** — run the `symbolism-synthesis-method.md` audit on the result (one symbol, one primary domain; cross-register alignment; licensed transformations), then run the calibration table below against every "ancient" claim in the draft.

## 13. Canon boundary and handoff

- **This entry is reference and method only.** It supplies comparanda, mechanisms, decision tables, and a calibration bank. It does not decide what is true in any world: naming, era assignment, and canon recording belong to lore-shokunin (`synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md`).
