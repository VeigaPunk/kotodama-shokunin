## Evidence frame and usable distinctions
- **Attested:** an identifiable text, inscription, image, or archaeological context supports the statement; an official account is not therefore an impartial account.
- **Synthesis:** a scholarly reconstruction connects uneven evidence; differences among regions, periods, and genres remain significant.
- **Design transfer:** the proposed fictional mechanism is an analogy, not a claim that every Hittite institution worked that way.
- `(to-verify)` marks an unresolved source, reading, identification, or extrapolation; do not promote it into a factual design constraint.
- Keep four categories separate: **political Hatti / Hittite language / Hattic language / Anatolian language family**. None is a synonym for a homogeneous ethnicity.
- The principal coupling is not “an Indo-European pantheon”: inherited Anatolian speech, Hattic cult, Hurrian traditions, Mesopotamian scribal practices, and regional institutions intersect. [Melchert 2003; Taracha 2009]
- Royal archives and monumental art are unusually informative about institutions and unusually selective about ordinary lives; “the court represented X” is safer than “everyone believed X.”

## Era model: stage institutions, not an eternal “Hittite look”
| Stage | Approximate horizon, BCE | Language/writing and institutions | Design constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old Assyrian trading networks and Anatolian city-states | Twentieth–eighteenth centuries | Akkadian merchant documentation at Kaneš/Neša; Anatolian names and words occur within it | A merchant archive's language does not identify the whole population's language |
| Old Hittite kingdom | Approximately seventeenth–sixteenth centuries | Hattusa-centered kingship; cuneiform historical, legal, and ritual traditions | Separate an event's date from the date of the surviving copy |
| Conventionally “Middle Hittite” transition | Broadly fifteenth century, boundaries variable | Uneven documentation; changing southern connections and royal networks | Leave succession and institutional transitions open where evidence is thin |
| Empire/New Kingdom | Fourteenth–early twelfth centuries | International diplomacy, multilingual cult, cuneiform archives, expanding hieroglyphic display | Couple imperial claims to particular courts and regions, not a single national essence |
| Post-imperial and Iron Age regional kingdoms | After approximately 1200; many major inscriptions ninth–eighth centuries | Luwian monumental traditions alongside changing regional languages and powers | Carchemish, Malatya, Tabal, and other centers are not one unchanged successor state |
- These are working horizons, not exact synchronized cutoffs across Anatolia. Absolute dates and early royal sequences remain debated. [Bryce 2005; Hawkins 2000]
- **Chronicle scarcity:** the fifteenth-century transition and the immediate post-collapse interval are less evenly illuminated than the late imperial archive; do not fill gaps with a seamless king list.
- **Manuscript chronology:** “Old Hittite composition,” “Old Script manuscript,” and “later copy of an older composition” are different claims. Archaism can be inherited, copied, or renewed. [Hoffner and Melchert 2008]
- **Terminology trap:** New/Neo-Hittite as a linguistic stage of Hittite is not the same label as the later “Neo-Hittite” kingdoms, whose inscriptions are often Luwian.
- The collapse around 1200 was not the disappearance of every Anatolian population or tradition; a single universal destroyer and exact causal chain are not established by the surviving evidence.

## Language family and contact ecology
| System | Secure distinction | Useful coupling | Bad inference to reject |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hittite, conventionally also Nesite; *nešili* “in the language of Neša” | Indo-European, Anatolian branch | Court archive, legal and historical genres, ritual instructions | “Hittite” means an unchanged spoken language of every imperial subject |
| Luwian/Luvian | Anatolian relative of Hittite, not a Hittite dialect | Ritual utterances, contact vocabulary, names, seals, and monuments | Every Luwian speaker belonged to one centralized “Luwian nation” |
| Palaic | Another Anatolian language, sparsely preserved largely through ritual transmission | A restricted corpus can preserve an older liturgical language | Thin attestation measures the historical population size |
| Hattic | Non-Indo-European; not Hittite under another spelling | Inherited cult names and liturgical passages within Hittite textual settings | A demonstrated genealogical relation to a modern Caucasian language |
| Hurrian | Non-Indo-European; important southern and court-cultural connections | Divine names, myths, ritual specialists, multilingual elite networks | Every Hurrian name proves a change of dynasty or ethnicity |
| Akkadian and Sumerian scribal resources | Akkadian is Semitic; Sumerian is unrelated to Anatolian | Diplomatic language, learned writing, logograms, and inherited conventions | A Sumerogram in a Hittite clause means that clause was spoken in Sumerian |
- Comparative baseline: Hittite has common/neuter grammatical gender, rich case inflection, predominantly verb-final syntax, and ordered enclitic chains; it is not Sanskrit with different nouns. [Hoffner and Melchert 2008]
- Grammatical gender is not a direct social-gender map; a common/neuter system does not demonstrate gender equality or an absence of gendered institutions.
- Treat Hittite/Luwian contact as historical layering: loanwords, personal names, and scribal choices have different evidential weights. The language distribution of the population cannot be read directly from archive proportions. [Melchert 2003]
- “Cuneiform Luwian” and “Hieroglyphic Luwian” name corpora through their scripts; script, dialect, region, and date do not line up automatically. Avoid either “identical in every respect” or “unrelated languages.”
- Design transfer: specify **household speech / archive language / treaty language / ritual quotation / monumental register** separately; allow individuals to cross those divisions.

## Writing: tablet expertise and hieroglyphic elite display
| Medium and script | Historical affordance | Coupled authority | Reconstruction limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cuneiform on clay | Hittite and other languages; syllabic writing with logograms and determinatives | Archive retrieval, legal continuity, training, diplomatic correspondence | Wedge-shaped signs do not identify the language without reading them |
| Cuneiform Luwian passages | Particularly prominent in ritual transmission | A Hittite framing instruction can preserve another language's utterance | Do not assume all Luwian textual production was religious |
| Anatolian hieroglyphs | Seals, names/titles, rock and monumental inscriptions; extended texts attest Luwian | Office, royal identity, patronage, territorial claims | The older label “Hieroglyphic Hittite” is misleading about language |
| Digraphic royal seal | Cuneiform and hieroglyphic elements coexist on one authorization device | Different graphic traditions reinforce one office | Two scripts are not necessarily a translation for two segregated ethnic audiences |
| Iron Age Luwian monument | Longer regional statements of genealogy, building, benefaction, and divine backing | Local dynastic continuity and competition | Its formulas and dress cannot simply be projected into the seventeenth century |
- Anatolian hieroglyphs are not Egyptian hieroglyphs; visual resemblance does not make the sign systems interchangeable. [Melchert 2003; Hawkins 2000]
- Hieroglyphic writing combines logographic and phonetic resources: it is not merely a collection of sacred pictures. Modern capitalized Latin sign labels are editorial conventions, not ancient Latin text.
- Seals bearing only short logographic names or titles may not securely reveal the language of their users; early signs are weaker evidence for running Luwian discourse than extended phonetic inscriptions.
- “Elite display” is a strong use-case, not an exclusivity claim. Sealings also participate in practical administration; the language could have everyday speakers beyond monument patrons.
- Distinguish **legibility** from **recognizability**: a person can recognize a royal emblem without reading a clause; universal literacy cannot be inferred from a conspicuous relief.
- Hittite texts refer to writing boards, and a wooden diptych set was recovered from the Late Bronze Age Uluburun shipwreck; whether Anatolian boards carried hieroglyphs, cuneiform, or both is debated [Symington 1991; Payton 1991; Waal 2011]. A lost wooden-document tradition must not become a fully reconstructed secret archive.
- Design transfer: distribute graphic labor by task—archival copyists, seal owners, monument carvers—and decide how names move among speech, tablet spelling, and emblematic display.

## Pantheon core: storm power without a single uniform theology
| Cult focus | Historical relationship | Mythic/social coupling | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storm-god of Hatti; local storm-gods such as those of Nerik and Zippalanda | Related roles with place-specific cult identities | Rain, prosperity, political protection, shrine geography | Do not collapse every “Storm-god of X” into an interchangeable alias |
| Hittite Tarḫunna-, Luwian Tarḫunt-, Hurrian Teššub, Hattic Taru | Names belong to distinct linguistic and cult traditions that can be aligned in context | Translation of divine authority across multilingual institutions | Functional correspondence is not proof of one original, unchanged god |
| Sun-goddess of Arinna | Major royal cult focus, not a male sun-god by default | Royal protection, legitimacy, petition, place identity | Distinguish her from other solar deities and from every winged-sun image |
| Ḫebat and Teššub in Hurrian-influenced court settings | Prominent paired divine figures, especially in late imperial visual arrangements | Court patronage, divine households, regional religious exchange | Ḫebat should not simply be relabeled “the sun-goddess” in every context |
| Telipinu | Disappearance-and-return tradition with ritual implications | Fertility failure, anger, search, pacification, restoration | Not automatically a dying-and-resurrected vegetation god |
| Šauška, Šarruma, and other incorporated deities | Distinct divine agents within changing local and court arrangements | Gendered iconography, divine kinship, royal protection | An “imported god” need not have one fixed role everywhere |
- “Land of a thousand gods” is a useful description of multiplicity, not an audited count or a permanently closed divine membership list. [Taracha 2009; Beckman 1999]
- Keep localization, translation/equation, juxtaposition, and replacement distinct: these processes leave different names, rituals, and political consequences.
- **Contested framework:** Taracha's state/dynastic/local categories are productive questions, not three proven departments. Miller (2014: 286–288) challenges the supporting royal genealogy and the picture of an exclusively Hurrian dynastic pantheon.
- A multilingual religious corpus can preserve difference while enabling imperial coordination; it does not prove modern religious tolerance or voluntary consent.

## Treaty theology: divine witnesses turn geography into obligation
- Read each treaty as a historically situated instrument: royal relationship, prior events, obligations, witnesses, oath sanctions, and sometimes provisions for preservation or reading. Not every document has an identical sequence. [Beckman 1999]
- Distinguish parity diplomacy from subordinate agreements. “Brotherhood” between major rulers is a diplomatic relation, not evidence that every treaty partner was equal.
- Divine witness lists can gather gods of both parties and landscape powers: mountains, rivers, springs, heaven, and earth. The geography of obligation is more than a capital-city pantheon.
- “Thousand gods” can function as comprehensive collective invocation; do not turn the phrase into exactly one thousand individualized entries.
- Oath-breaking threatens a ruler, household, succession, or land through specified divine sanctions; consult the particular curse rather than assembling generic fantasy punishments.
- The historical prologue is interested persuasion: what the superior claims to have done for a partner frames the debt being demanded, not a neutral chronicle.
- Gods' inclusion in an imperial document neither erases local cults nor proves those cults retained unchanged autonomy.
- Design transfer: represent a treaty as **jurisdiction + local divine names + translated obligations + public/archival authentication + sanction + renewal procedure**; derive each component explicitly instead of borrowing a decorative oath.
- Cross-axis test: a newly incorporated river shrine should affect the witness list, scribal spelling, ritual itinerary, and office display only where the proposed institutional mechanism explains those links.

## Mythic grammar: preserve genre, variants, and transmission
| Textual complex | Recoverable mechanism | Usable craft move | False shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Illuyanka narratives, CTH 321 | Storm-god/serpent conflict; differing narratives transmitted in a ritual setting | One institution may preserve non-identical accounts with different causal details | A single definitive “Hittite dragon myth” assembled from all variants |
| Telipinu disappearance, CTH 324 | Withdrawal disrupts life; search and ritual management address divine anger | Couple emotion, ecological failure, specialist speech, and restored relations | A universal seasonal death/resurrection template |
| Kumarbi and associated Hurrian-derived songs | Divine succession and rivalry transmitted through Hittite textual culture | Separate story language, cultural transmission, and a later manuscript | Direct identity with Greek Kronos or proof of a universal inherited plot |
- These CTH numbers identify text groups, not single complete autograph scrolls; distinguish witnesses, restorations, translations, and modern composite presentations. [Hoffner 1998]
- A copied myth does not automatically describe a performed rite; a rite's textual instructions do not automatically recover every participant's interpretation.
- Compare motifs only after checking transmission and context. “All storm gods are the same archetype” destroys the local cult distinctions that treaties preserve.
- Design transfer: retain a disagreement register—two authorized versions may coexist because they serve distinct ritual, regional, or political situations; that explanation itself remains a fictional proposal.

## Symbolic system: composition and setting before a motif dictionary
| Element | Strong contextual reading | Systems to couple | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bull and storm-god associations | Storm power can be expressed through animal companions and imagery | Divine name, local shrine, offering, royal patronage | Every excavated bull object is not automatically the same deity |
| Horned divine headdress | Horns mark divine status in characteristic representations | Costume, rank within a divine procession, figure labels | Not a generic helmet for all human soldiers |
| Winged solar emblem in royal display | Royal/divine association in particular visual programs | Seal layout, title, patron, viewer recognition | Not proof of solar monotheism or an exclusively native motif |
| Mountain gods supporting a deity | Landscape is personified and placed within divine hierarchy | Cult topography, divine mobility, treaty witnesses | A mountain-shaped figure is not a literal report of a local costume |
| Protective divine embrace at Yazılıkaya | Šarruma protects Tudḫaliya IV in an explicitly royal image | King's name label, patron deity, commemorative setting | Not a private portrait of an everyday interpersonal gesture |
- Yazılıkaya's relief processions and hieroglyphic labels are a late imperial arrangement, not a census of every god ever worshipped in Hatti. [Taracha 2009; Miller 2014]
- Its precise cult function and representativeness are debated. A calendar/cosmos reading has been proposed [Zangger and Gautschy 2019; Zangger, Krupp, Demirel and Gautschy 2021], but it is not a consensus baseline: reporting in 2021 recorded named specialists doubting details and noting the argument's reliance on non-Hittite comparanda [New Scientist 2021], and each proposed model must still be evaluated against the reliefs.
- For every borrowed motif, record **object/site + date + associated label + posture + neighboring figures + proposed function + alternative reading**. [Melchert 2003; specialist visual follow-up: Seeher 2011]
- Ancient double-headed-eagle imagery is not evidence of an unchanged modern national, ethnic, or imperial meaning; later reuse needs its own history.

## Costume and material signature: build a role matrix, not a national uniform
| Visual/material register | Reference signature | What can be transferred | Evidence brake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Male divine figures in imperial relief | Short belted garments, pointed horned headgear, characteristic footwear | Silhouette and status distinctions within a documented composition | Exact textile, fastening, and construction remain `(to-verify)` without supporting evidence |
| Female divine figures | Long garments and tall headdresses in characteristic imperial arrangements | Contrast between divine roles, ranks, and processional positions | One relief program is not the wardrobe of all women |
| Royal ceremonial representation | Long robe and rounded cap in some images; curved staff associated with royal display | Role-specific dress and a recognizable authorization ensemble | Compare specific images before treating a pose or garment as invariant |
| Other royal/armed representation | Shorter garment and weapon-bearing representations also occur | A ruler can change represented role without changing ethnic identity | Do not splice details from different centuries into one “authentic” outfit |
| Textile economy | Wool and linen belong to the regional textile world; institutions organize material resources | Fiber supply, skilled labor, distribution, status, and garment terminology | A carved hem cannot identify fiber or dye; regional quantities need a dated text |
| Seals, sealings, and inscribed stone | Portable authorization and durable monumental display | Office identity may remain stable while spoken names and clothing shift | A seal legend is not proof that a hieroglyph was embroidered on the owner's robe |
- Baseline context: Bryce (2002) and Melchert (2003); image-specific identification and tailoring details require plates/context (Seeher 2011's plates were not inspected for this entry).
- Distinguish **depicted garment / textual garment term / excavated textile / reconstructed cut** in the evidence record; do not silently merge them.
- Colors, pleating construction, footwear materials, and region-specific ordinary dress are thinly supported here; leave precise palettes and patterns `(to-verify)` rather than supplying attractive certainties.
- Do not make Late Bronze Age Hatti a uniform iron-armored civilization or use the popular “Hittite iron monopoly” story as a technology chronology. Check a dated assemblage before choosing an equipment package.

## Naming practices: multilingual identity, not a suffix generator
- Record a name with **language analysis, attested spelling, script, person/site, period, title, and source**; a romanized form alone loses important evidence.
- Ḫattušili, Muršili, Muwatalli, and Tudḫaliya are useful royal-name comparanda, not an exhaustive phonotactic inventory or modern family-surname list. [Bryce 2005]
- Urḫi-Teššub's use of the regnal name Muršili III illustrates that different names can belong to one ruler; it does not establish compulsory double naming for all elites.
- Divine-name elements and patron deities connect onomastics to cult, but a theophoric name does not establish the named person's entire belief system or ethnic descent.
- A court can use inherited royal names, Hurrian names, and Luwian formations in overlapping networks; classify the formation before assigning an ethnicity. [Melchert 2003]
- **Title versus name:** cuneiform `LUGAL.GAL` conventionally renders “Great King”; it is not an ordinary personal-name syllable. Modern hieroglyphic sign labels such as `MAGNUS.REX` likewise are not spoken Latin titles.
- “My Sun”/“His Majesty” belongs to royal titulary and address, not an automatic birth name; preserve the difference between a translated title and a phonetic reconstruction.
- Do not claim an exact translation of a royal name from resemblance to a modern word. Name meanings, specific productive elements, and proposed sound changes remain `(to-verify)` until checked in an onomastic/linguistic treatment.
- Design transfer: define which institutions assign childhood, regnal, cultic, or translated names; invent the rule first, then generate new forms instead of pasting historical names into canon.

## What a worldbuilder can take
| Mechanism to adapt, not copy | Coupled output | Consistency test before lore-shokunin handoff |
|---|---|---|
| Multilingual institutional ecology | Different speech, ritual, archive, and display registers | Can the same person's name be traced across those registers without equating scripts with ethnicities? |
| Accumulating local gods under imperial coordination | Local epithets, treaty witnesses, itineraries, and patronage | Does incorporation explain which distinctions survive and who bears the cost? |
| Storm power anchored to landscape | Cult sites, environmental anxieties, divine sanctions, emblem choices | Are rain, mountain, river, and sovereignty linked by a mechanism rather than a mood board? |
| Graphic authority alongside variable literacy | Seals, emblem recognition, monumental claims, trained readers | Can a non-reader recognize office while a scribe verifies the text? |
| Role-indexed costume | Ceremonial, divine, armed, and administrative ensembles | Is each detail dated and role-specific rather than labeled simply “Hittite”? |
| Uneven survival and competing versions | Manuscript layers, local variants, contested genealogies | Are gaps and interpretations visible instead of resolved by invented historical facts? |
- Handoff only a **design option + mechanism + evidence pointers + uncertainty + affected systems + requested canon decision**. Lore-shokunin accepts, revises, or rejects; this entry establishes no fictional people, gods, dynasty, or wardrobe as canon.
- Minimal application workflow: choose an era and region; map registers; select a local cult relationship; specify one authorization medium; derive naming and dress consequences; run the table's checks.
- Negative test: changing only names while leaving myth, inscription, political obligation, and garment roles untouched has not used the Anatolian coupling model.

## Provenance cautions and known thin spots
- **Ancient imperial-source bias:** treaties, annals, prayers, and royal images privilege elite institutions. Subjects, deportees, rural communities, and women outside the court are not equally represented.
- **Modern framing bias:** older scholarship's “civilized center,” invasion narratives, racial language, and equation of language with people should not be imported into a design taxonomy. Genealogical linguistics does not supply a hierarchy of cultures.
- **Discredited universal ladder:** “primitive magic → mature religion” is not a defensible neutral chronology; Miller (2014: 284–285) explicitly contests that framing in Taracha. Separate the source's evidence from its inherited theory.
- **Contested, not resolved:** early royal succession, boundaries between period labels, the institutional reading of the pantheon, and the precise purpose of Yazılıkaya. Miller's 2014 review is a dated critical intervention, not a guarantee that no later evidence has changed a debate.
- **Thin here:** household ritual outside institutional texts; ordinary regional clothing; textile color and cut; exact personal-name etymologies; precise literacy rates; loss of perishable records; the immediate post-collapse sequence.
- **Verification debt:** obtain Seeher's plates before a precise visual reconstruction; verify an individual treaty/text witness before quoting it; check revised grammars before committing to exact phonetic values. No verbatim ancient quotations or conjectural name translations are supplied here.
- A museum overview or catalogue verifies a reference or broad claim, not every sentence in a scholarly book; inaccessible full text is not silently treated as inspected. Keep `(to-verify)` flags attached when reusing this entry.
