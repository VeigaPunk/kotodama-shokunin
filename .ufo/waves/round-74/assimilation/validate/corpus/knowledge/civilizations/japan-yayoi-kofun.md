## Scope and evidence ladder

- “Japan” is a geographical convenience here, not an assertion that a timeless nation occupied the archipelago throughout prehistory.
- Keep three clocks separate: archaeological object/context date; earliest surviving linguistic attestation; date when a later institution narrated its origins.
- **Attested:** a catalogued object, inscription, or statement in a named text. A statement’s existence does not establish that its narrated event happened.
- **Reconstruction:** a model connecting distributions, sequences, and independent evidence; retain competing explanations.
- **Contested:** language affiliations, substrate identifications, exact symbolic meanings, and some political/chronological identifications.
- **Design inference:** an explicitly fictional transformation of a relationship below; never present it as an ancient Japanese practice.
- Coverage is strongest for broad transition models, a named armored haniwa, and court-text motifs; weaker for everyday speech, ordinary dress, and region-specific ritual practice.

## Era model — stage locally, not as one national costume change

| Stage | Working date / place qualification | Coupled changes to track | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late/Final Jōmon background | Regionally variable; precedes and overlaps initial farming transitions | Established pottery, fishing/foraging and exchange practices; communities with their own histories | “Primitive nomads” awaiting civilization; one Jōmon language |
| Yayoi | In revised chronologies begins around 900 BCE in northern Kyūshū; conventionally ends around 250 CE | Wet-rice expansion, migration, local continuity, metal goods and changing settlement relations | Older 300/400 BCE starts treated as universal or automatically current |
| Late Yayoi textual horizon | First–third centuries CE; Chinese observers describe particular Wa polities | Diplomacy, gifts, named rulers, ritual authority; Himiko in third-century records | Yamatai securely equated with Yamato, or Himiko with Amaterasu |
| Early Kofun | Approximately 250–400 CE | Mounded/keyhole tomb networks, prestige-goods circulation and regional alliances | A fully uniform bureaucratic empire already present |
| Middle Kofun | Approximately 400–500 CE | Monumental tomb display; increasingly elaborate material representations of elite roles | Every tomb or costume assigned to a named chronicle ruler |
| Late/terminal Kofun | Sixth–seventh centuries; overlaps Asuka political/religious staging | Anthropomorphic haniwa, regional manufacture, continuing continental links; later court reorganization | One nationwide end date; seventh-century title/color rules backdated to Yayoi |

- Dates are working bins, not synchronized boundaries: see Hudson et al. (2020), archaeology section, and Shirai, chronological overview.
- Northern and southern trajectories do not fit the central-island rice→tomb sequence neatly; “outside the core” does not mean culturally static.
- Hudson’s 1999 framing and older museum labels may use older dates; record the adopted chronology with each reference image.
- Fictional application: let farming vocabulary spread before a court title system, while ornaments outlast both transitions; systems need not change in lockstep.

## Language family, writing, and the Jōmon→Yayoi transition

- **Secure family-level starting point:** Japonic includes Japanese and the Ryukyuan languages; Ryukyuan is not merely “Japanese with an ancient accent.”
- **Attestation limit:** the substantial Old Japanese record is much later than the beginning of Yayoi; reconstructing proto-Japonic is not reading a surviving Yayoi transcript (Frellesvig 2010).
- **Working model, not direct attestation:** proto-Japonic spread with some farming/migrant networks from the Korean peninsula, followed by language shift among local populations (Hudson 1999; Hudson et al. 2020).
- **External affiliation contested:** Korean–Japonic common descent, extended “Altaic/Transeurasian” proposals, and contact explanations remain disputed; shared word order or agriculture does not settle genealogy.
- Hudson et al. (2020) favor stronger genealogical links than Vovin’s contact account; cite that disagreement rather than presenting either as unanimous consensus.
- Use suffixing morphology, predominantly verb-final syntax and postpositions as Japanese/Japonic design references; these typological features cannot identify an unknown Jōmon language.
- Written Chinese and Chinese characters enter elite material/administrative contexts before the broad surviving Japanese-language corpus; an imported inscribed object does not prove its owner could read it.
- Kofun sword inscriptions are evidence of names, genealogy and authority in Chinese-character writing; phonetic readings and date assignments require specialist treatment (Cooke et al. 2021, discussion and reference 57).
- Do not supply Yayoi people with a standardized kana alphabet; distinguish learned written language, character-based name transcription, and local spoken varieties.
- A Chinese transcription such as “Wa” or “Himiko” passes through an observer’s phonology and categories; it is not transparent proof of an indigenous spelling or self-designation.

| Explanation / evidence | Calibration | Fictional analogue that preserves the distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Farming/language dispersal | Plausible, productive model; correlations require independent chronological checks | Settler households transmit a language along irrigation and marriage networks |
| Substrate effects after language shift | Possible; individual proposed words/features need regular correspondences and contact geography | A surviving hydronym stratum and regionally restricted pronunciations |
| Borrowing without shift | A different process from substrate influence | Jewel cutters retain their language while patrons borrow workshop terms |
| Genetic admixture | Demonstrates biological histories, not what language an individual spoke | Ancestry, mother tongue and court affiliation are separate character fields |
| Automatic “Jōmon + Yayoi = creole” | Not demonstrated; mixed ancestry does not establish creolization | Specify sustained contact conditions before choosing a contact-language structure |

## Ainu-substrate debate — calibrated, not an origin shortcut

- Ainu varieties are not demonstrated members of Japonic; resemblance between words is not sufficient evidence of common descent.
- **Do not equate:** Jōmon archaeological assemblage = one biological population = one language = historically documented Ainu culture.
- Jōmon communities extended over a huge time/space range; the number and affiliations of their languages remain poorly recoverable.
- Ainu-related toponymy and historical contact can illuminate parts of the archipelago, especially northern regions; extrapolating every such identification across all Jōmon Japan is unwarranted.
- Hudson et al. (2020), historical-linguistics section, discuss Ainu place-name proposals, later reconstructable proto-Ainu, and substantial Japonic contact; their synthesis is a hypothesis framework, not an exhaustive substrate dictionary.
- A shallow reconstructed proto-Ainu does not prove there were no earlier ancestors: reconstruction depth depends on surviving varieties and lost branches.
- Nor does Ainu continuity with some Jōmon ancestry make later Ainu religion or costume a fossilized Yayoi/Jōmon wardrobe (Hudson 1999; Hudson et al. 2020).
- Cooke et al. (2021) refine a dual-origin demographic story with a tripartite model; their limited Yayoi/Kofun sampling cannot identify an “Ainu-speaking genome.”
- Specific popular Ainu etymologies for mountain names, divine names or Japanese basic vocabulary require a named linguistic study and its objections: Frellesvig (2010), ch. 4 §4.1 “Ainu”; Vovin (2022), who argues for Ainu loans in early Japonic against the field’s default of none; Nakagawa (2003), who judges the genetic relation improbable and the later contact limited in effect.
- Do not convert the presence of beads, bear imagery, or “animism” into evidence of an Ainu substrate; independent histories can produce similar motifs.
- Fictional application: create multiple displaced/local languages with different survival niches; keep substrate labels provisional and allow later chroniclers to collapse them incorrectly.

## Mythic core — later court narratives, not a recovered Yayoi creed

- The *Kojiki* (712) and *Nihon shoki/Nihongi* (720) are later compilations; use them to study narrated antiquity and court genealogy, not as contemporary Yayoi reportage.
- The following motifs are directly locatable in Aston (1896), books I–II; names use familiar modern romanization rather than pretending to reproduce prehistoric pronunciation.

| Narrative node | Coupling visible in the text | Design use / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Izanagi and Izanami | Island generation, kinship, death, a blocked underworld route, purification | Make boundaries and purification consequential; do not claim this was one universal Jōmon cosmology |
| Amaterasu | Solar authority linked to weaving, cultivated fields and descent | Connect sovereignty to productive and ritual work, not simply to a “sun-element” power |
| Susanoo | Disruption of fields/weaving; expulsion; serpent episode and sword acquisition | A deity’s jurisdiction can change across narratives; “storm god” alone loses the social relations |
| Ōnamuchi/Ōkuninushi and land transfer | Local land-ordering and negotiated/subordinated authority under a heavenly lineage | Let incorporated traditions retain ritual jurisdictions rather than vanish |
| Ame-no-Uzume, Ame-no-Koyane, Futodama and craft ancestors | Performance, liturgy, offerings, mirrors and jewels become hereditary origin claims | Ritual specialists authorize speech and production through ancestry |

- Read variants as evidence: *Nihongi* repeatedly preserves “one writing says”; do not silently combine incompatible versions into an original single pantheon chart.
- Himiko’s ritual-political authority is a separate Chinese-record problem; “female shaman” is an interpretive translation/category, not a surviving indigenous job description (Kidder 2007).
- Calling all this an unchanged, systematized “Shinto” projects later institutions backward; specify local cult, court compilation or later reception instead.
- Strong coupling exemplar: the cave episode assembles recited words, performance, textiles, jewels and a mirror; speech efficacy is institutionally situated, not proof of a universal ancient “kotodama doctrine.”

## Magatama, jewels, and the symbolic economy

- *Magatama* (勾玉 / 曲玉) denotes curved, perforated beads/jewels; shape, material, provenance, association and deposition must be recorded separately.
- Curved-bead histories reach into Jōmon contexts; do not assume every earlier bead had the standardized shape or court meaning of later examples. Fine typological staging is available in Nishimura (2018), who classifies 157 provenienced beads into six types (A–F, with subtypes) and places the household-to-grave shift in Late Jōmon Tōhoku/Hokkaidō.
- “Jade” is not a blanket identification for every bead: obtain a specimen’s material determination rather than coloring all curved jewels green.
- An object can be an ornament, exchange good, grave deposit and later sacred heirloom at different moments; those are hypotheses about its biography, not interchangeable labels.

| Object / setting | Evidence-aware reading | Do not infer automatically |
|---|---|---|
| Curved jewel or bead strand | Bodily display; archaeological context may support status/exchange interpretations | Embryo, crescent, claw, soul or yin–yang as its single original meaning |
| Bronze mirror | Manufactured/imported prestige object, with its own exchange and inscription history | Every mirror was solar regalia or every owner literate |
| Sword | Weapon, gift, authority object, or deposited treasure depending on context | Later katana silhouette; identification with the literary Kusanagi |
| Jewel + mirror + sword in descent narrative | Court-text bundle transmitting authority | Every excavated assemblage enacted the same enthronement rite |
| Haniwa displayed around a mound | Publicly staged relationships and boundaries are plausible questions | A literal soul-container or one fixed afterlife doctrine |

- Aston, [book I, cave episode and variants](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_I): branches hold jewels, mirror and offerings; variants explicitly connect their production/handling to ritual and craft ancestors.
- Aston, [book II, descent-of-Ninigi variant](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_II): Ninigi receives the Yasaka curved jewel, mirror and Kusanagi sword, with ancestral attendants.
- The familiar regalia names are Yasakani no magatama, Yata no kagami and Kusanagi no tsurugi; the later bundle must not be treated as an excavated Yayoi constitutional document.
- Object-class antiquity ≠ continuous ownership of a particular surviving sacred object; neither a myth nor an illustration verifies the present regalia’s manufacture or uninterrupted material history.
- Interpretations of the curve as fetus, tooth (canine or wolf fang), crescent moon or soul remain proposals — Nishimura (2018), “Explanations Regarding Magatama,” surveys them after Takioto (2012) — and the Jōmon-period functions and meanings are stated to be unclear; visual resemblance establishes no specific ancient explanation.
- Compare peninsula and archipelago jewel networks without assigning a modern national owner to every shape; priority and transmission claims need dated, provenanced assemblages.
- Fictional application: separate jewel source, workshop, wearer, keeper and ritual speaker; contested custody can join trade vocabulary, descent claims and costume without copying sacred names.

## Haniwa costume evidence — model, material, and social role

- Haniwa are not Jōmon dogū; distinguish archaeological class, context and era before treating either as a costume reference.
- Many haniwa stood on exposed mound surfaces rather than being grave goods hidden beside a body; spatial arrangement matters to interpretation (Shirai, “Funerary objects meant to be seen”).
- Cylindrical examples and later human/animal/building forms have different histories; a sixth-century warrior cannot supply every third-century costume.
- Anchor object: **Tokyo National Museum J-36697**, sixth century, excavated at Iizuka, Ōta, Gunma; a regional late-Kofun example, not a universal Yamato uniform.
- The Japanese catalogue identifies *keikō* armor built of overlapping small plates, a bow, a sword and a back quiver; this is a museum interpretation of the modeled equipment, not metal surviving inside the clay.
- Shirai describes helmet details, forearm protection, chest ties, quiver and sword suspension, while marking some materials and fastening reconstructions as uncertain.

| Record from a haniwa | Reasonably usable | Additional evidence needed |
|---|---|---|
| Modeled upper/lower garment outline | Visible silhouette, belts, overlaps and relative coverage where preserved | Cutting pattern, hidden seams, garment’s spoken name |
| Incised lines and applied loops | Position of modeled details; competing fastening interpretations | Exact stitch, weave, plate count or underlying material |
| Armor/weapon assemblage | A represented equipment combination and embodied military role | Actual service rules; frequency among non-elite fighters |
| Hair arrangement, headgear, bead placement | Visible arrangement on the particular figure | Age, ethnicity, sex, office or rank assignment without independent support |
| Clay color or surviving paint | Present surface condition and documented pigment evidence | Original textile dye, universal color symbolism, standardized rank palette |
| Figure grouping beside houses/animals | Spatial relationships if excavation records preserve them | A complete ceremony reconstructed from isolated museum figures |

- Describe “belted upper garment,” “trousers,” “skirt/wrap” or “headgear” before asserting a later garment term; stylized clay is not a sewing pattern.
- Separate represented fiber from inferred fiber: bast textiles, silk, leather or padding must be justified object-by-object; a plausible fabric choice remains a reconstruction.
- Do not import Heian court layers, Edo silhouettes, modern kimono conventions or samurai plate-armour ensembles into a generic “ancient Japan” sheet.
- A figure labelled “priestess” is not proof of a pan-archipelago priestly uniform; role identifications require context, comparison and explicit uncertainty.
- Aston, book VI, Suinin year 32, narrates Nomi no Sukune substituting clay figures for living attendants: **attested origin story**, not an archaeological demonstration of haniwa’s invention.
- Fictional application: commission funerary figures that exaggerate insignia while simplifying bodies; later historians can over-read those figures, but the reference sheet must preserve the distinction.

## Uji, kabane, and naming practices

- *Uji* (氏): a named descent/corporate grouping; “clan” is useful shorthand, not proof of a simple biological pedigree or equivalence to a later surname-bearing household.
- *Kabane* (姓): hereditary status/title associated with such groups; distinguish group name, inherited title, office and personal name.
- Genealogy can authorize a ritual/craft service, while the court confirms or changes the title: myth, labor organization and address forms reinforce one another.
- Do not backdate a single neatly codified uji–kabane system to the beginning of Yayoi; the clearest textual descriptions are later than the earliest tomb networks.

| Term / layer | Use in a reference sheet | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Uji name | Group affiliation such as Nakatomi, Mononobe, Ōtomo or Soga | Not itself a personal name or a rank |
| *Omi*, *muraji* | Hereditary status labels appearing with named groups | Avoid fixed English equivalents such as duke/baron |
| *Ōomi*, *ōmuraji* | Leading court positions/styles associated with elite leadership | Do not flatten into ordinary kabane or a universal merit ladder |
| *Kuni no miyatsuko* | Local/regional leadership title | Not automatically a later provincial governor; jurisdiction changes |
| *Tomo no miyatsuko*, *be* | Questions about service-group leadership and organized services | Neither a modern guild nor one homogeneous caste by default |
| *Mabito*, *ason*, *sukune*, etc. in the 684 reform | A specifically later re-ranking of hereditary titles | The new ordering is not the original Kofun hierarchy |

- Aston, book XIX, opening accession passage, places Ōtomo no Kanamura and Mononobe no Okoshi as *ōmuraji*, and Soga no Iname as *ōomi*: named textual anchors, not invented rank examples.
- Naming-template options: `[uji] no [personal name]`; fuller identification may include `[kabane]`, office and honorific. Their order varies with source and context; this is not a universal naming regex.
- The particle *no* marks affiliation/relationship in these conventional renderings; do not treat it as a detachable European nobiliary “von” with identical law.
- Divine *kami* / *mikoto*, hereditary titles and personal names are different categories; a title translated “lord” does not establish deityhood.
- Aston, book XXIX, Tenmu year 13 (684), tenth month, first day: eight hereditary titles are instituted; *omi* and *muraji* remain but move below new leading titles.
- The same book’s 681 costume edict and 685 color regulations show a later textual coupling of rank and dress; they do **not** decode the colors of sixth-century haniwa garments.
- Naming practice for fiction: store `group`, `claimed ancestor`, `hereditary title`, `current office`, `personal name`, `address form`, and `era of grant` separately; title reform can then change introductions without changing everyone’s ancestry.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Asynchronous coupling:** a language shift, material fashion and legitimating myth can have different dates; record both survival and reinterpretation.
- **Jewel biography:** give an ornament a source/workshop history before giving it an oath or succession role; derive its vocabulary from makers, traders and custodians.
- **Materialized grammar of rank:** design which body locations show affiliation, office and personal wealth; do not make every sign encode the same hierarchy.
- **Mythic service charter:** a lineage’s ancestral performance can justify who may recite, weave, polish, guard or transfer sacred things; preserve rival versions.
- **Frontier plurality:** use intermarriage, bilingual households and specialist economies instead of two sealed “races” merging into one culture.
- **Canon boundary:** hand lore-shokunin alternative coupled systems, source pointers, era constraints and open questions; this entry establishes no fictional people, god, garment or historical event as canon.

## Provenance cautions

- Chinese dynastic accounts categorize outsiders through diplomatic and literary conventions; later Japanese chronicles legitimate a court. Neither is a transparent census of speech, religion or dress.
- Aston’s translation is checkable and historically important, but its “barbaric stages,” racial comparisons and confident sacrificial-survival arguments are obsolete evolutionary framing, not modern archaeological findings.
- Reject the “Ainu as Caucasian relic” racial taxonomy and timeless homogeneous-Japanese narratives; Hudson et al. (2020) trace their ideological histories explicitly.
- Neither a harmonious Jōmon/Yayoi binary nor ancestry percentages should become a psychological or aesthetic essence; Cooke et al. (2021) state substantial sampling limitations.
- Smarthistory’s blanket statement that Kofun Japan lacked a writing system is too broad: its costume analysis is useful, but metal inscriptions and restricted literacy must remain visible in the account.
- Museum labels also date: J-36697’s catalogue/essay claims of unique National Treasure status are not used here; verify present designation counts independently if needed.
- Thin areas remain early spoken registers (the extensive Japanese record begins in the eighth century; Frellesvig 2010: 2), precise ordinary garment construction, local divine names and individual substrate etymologies; unverified specifics must not be filled by later folklore.
- For new claims, require a site/object/text locator and a source at the right chronological scale; book existence, a modern reconstruction and visual resemblance are three different kinds of evidence.
