## Scope, verification, and calibration
- **Scope:** principally Central Thai and the Ayutthaya/Bangkok court traditions; neither an inventory of every culture in Thailand nor a pan-Tai civilization template.
- **Tai** names a linguistic grouping extending beyond Thailand; **Thai** here usually means Standard/Central Thai or a specified historical court. Lao, Lanna, Shan, Khmer, Malay, and other traditions are not interchangeable variants of Bangkok [S1–S2].
- **Established:** modern Thai phonological contrasts; socially sensitive reference vocabulary; differentiated khon role types; the early Bangkok court's Ramakien patronage [S1–S2, S5–S7].
- **Interpretive/contested:** whether Ramakien is best classified as Hindu, Buddhist, or differently framed in different media; the explanatory reach of “feudalism” for sakdina [S5, S9].
- **Thin here:** pre-1767 performance reconstruction, provincial/local ritual variants, exact legal clauses, historical textile inventories, and workshop-specific mask consecration. Do not fill these gaps with court stereotypes.

## Language family, sound, and writing
| Layer | Attested reference point | Coupling consequence / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Central Thai belongs to Southwestern Tai within Tai-Kadai/Kra-Dai [S1–S2] | Sanskrit/Pali vocabulary does not make Thai Indo-Aryan; a Khmer-derived script does not make it Austroasiatic. |
| Tone | Standard Thai contrasts mid, low, falling, high, and rising tones [S1 §1.2.4] | Tone distinguishes lexical items; it is not a musical mood label or an aristocratic speech level. |
| Segments | Vowel length, aspiration, restricted codas, and tone all matter [S1 §1.2] | Retain these contrasts in a naming lexicon; substituting decorative accents does not create a tonal language. |
| Grammar | Predominantly isolating; classifiers, particles, serial constructions, and discourse context carry substantial work [S1–S2] | A language can elaborate social hierarchy without European-style case or agreement paradigms. |
| Script | Indic-derived writing developed through a Khmer model; consonant classes interact with tone marks, syllable type, and vowel length [S1 §1.3; S2 §1] | A written name can preserve historical distinctions that everyday pronunciation has merged. |
| Contact | Khmer, Pali, Sanskrit, Chinese varieties, and other sources contribute different, overlapping lexical layers [S2 introduction] | Distribute prestige by historical institution and genre, not by assigning one “pure” language to each social caste. |

- **Orthographic trap:** four tone marks do not simply encode four of five tones; a mark's pronunciation depends on the other spelling factors. “High/mid/low consonant class” describes an orthographic category, not a person's social rank [S1, pp. 7–8].
- **Diachronic lever:** loss of earlier consonant voicing distinctions helped reorganize tone contrasts; inherited spelling can preserve the old division. Exact timings and dialect outcomes are debated [S2 §1].
- **Era caution:** do not give a thirteenth-century character the entire modern Bangkok sound system by default. A historical language stage, a modern performance pronunciation, and a reader-facing transcription are different specifications.
- The traditionally early Ramkhamhaeng inscription has an authenticity/dating controversy; Jenny explicitly flags it. “One king invented the entire writing system on one date” is not a safe uncontested origin story [S2, p. 559 n. 1].
- **Fictional application:** specify underlying phonemes, phonotactics, lexical tones, and historical sound changes first; only then design a conservative prestige orthography. Keep pronunciation, spelling, and social register as separate database fields.

## Rachasap: rank-sensitive vocabulary, not another language
- **ราชาศัพท์**, often romanized *rachasap* or *raachaasap*, concerns special vocabulary associated with royalty; broader instructional treatments also discuss monastic vocabulary alongside it [S2 §4.3].
- Referent status matters: words may change when speaking **about** a royal person, not only when addressing that person. Do not model the system solely as “the king is listening.”
- Jenny's table 12 gives the following contrasts; these are illustrative lexical pairings, **not** complete protocols for every royal rank, tense, or grammatical construction [S2, p. 600].

| Meaning | Common vocabulary | Royal vocabulary | Separate monastic example |
|---|---|---|---|
| eat | กิน *kin* | เสวย *sawoei* | ฉัน *chan* |
| sleep | นอน *non* | บรรทม *banthom* | จำวัด *cham wat*, literally staying in the temple |
| go | ไป *pai* | เสด็จ *sadet* | Do not manufacture a monastic equivalent by analogy. |
| be ill | ป่วย *puai* | ประชวร *prachuan* | อาพาธ *aphath* (monastic register; not royal) [S11]. |

- Romanizations above are readable labels without full tone/length notation; S2 supplies the phonological transcriptions. They are not sufficient pronunciation instructions.
- Royal vocabulary includes nouns and verbs and derivational elements: **ทรง** *song* works as a verbal prefix and **พระที่นั่ง** *phra thinang* as a nominal suffix; it is not produced by mechanically adding *song* to every verb — instructional norms prohibit *song* before ready-made royal verbs (not *song prachuan* or *song sawoei*), while *song* + an ordinary verb or noun, and *song phra* + certain royal nouns, are regular [S2 §4.3; S11].
- Many elevated forms have Khmer and Sanskrit connections; monastic vocabulary has a substantial Pali layer. This is historical contact and institutional specialization, not evidence for a biologically separate elite language [S2].
- Pronoun choice, kinship address, titles, particles, omission, and the social situation also encode relationships. Rachasap is not the entirety of Thai politeness [S1, p. 9; S2 §4; S10].
- **Design schema:** `speaker → addressee → referent → office/royal status → intimacy → public/private genre → era`; select lexical and address forms from those fields.
- **Useful test:** narrate the same eating event to a sibling, to an official, and in a report about a sovereign. The referent may stay constant while address changes; conversely, the audience may stay constant while the referent changes.
- Do not invent “five tones = five ranks,” “royals always speak the highest register about themselves,” or “every elevated word is Sanskrit.” None follows from the evidence.

## Sakdina: juridical ranking and its registers
- **ศักดินา** *sakdina/saktina* refers to a numerical status system associated with Ayutthaya and early Bangkok society; its land-measure idiom must not be treated as a transparent inventory of land actually possessed — the “rai” unit reading itself is contested [S3; S12; comparison debated in S9].
- Separate **numerical legal standing**, **title**, **office**, **royal descent**, **monastic standing**, and **interactional seniority**. Their effects overlap; they are not one interchangeable ladder.
- Here “rank register” means the channels through which status is made legible. It does **not** assert that every sakdina number had its own distinct speech variety.

| Status channel | What it can organize | Unsafe inference |
|---|---|---|
| Numerical legal rank | Institutional precedence and legal differentiation; particular schedules require a dated law | A number equals an individually surveyed estate of that size. |
| Appointed title and office | Public identification, entitlement, responsibilities, patronage | A bestowed title is simply a hereditary modern surname. |
| Royal status and rachasap | Appropriate reference vocabulary and ceremonial address | One can calculate a royal verb from a sakdina number. |
| Monastic position | Specialized vocabulary and religious deference | Monks are merely nobles wearing another costume. |
| Relational seniority | Address between persons, including kinship-like terms | All interactions are dictated by one fixed state rank. |
| Stage hierarchy | Crown/mask distinctions, posture, seat order or height, and court etiquette [S6] | Every dramatic device reproduces an actual statute verbatim. |

- **Comparison calibration:** Waters analyzes Kukrit's contrast between European land-centered feudalism and Siamese control of people and service. This is a useful question generator, not a universal Europe/Asia binary [S9].
- The modern political use of *sakdina* also belongs to arguments about hierarchy and domination. Do not substitute a present polemical meaning for a particular historical legal arrangement.
- Neither “Thai feudalism was exactly European feudalism” nor “Thai hierarchy was uniquely harmonious” is a neutral finding. Preserve coercion, dependency, and political disagreement in the research questions.
- Exact numerical ranks, a single foundation date, fines, exemptions, corvée quotas, and costume privileges are **not reconstructed here**; the schedules themselves survive in the *Phra Aiyakan Tamnaeng Na* sections of the Three Seals law (attributed to the Ayutthayan court and recompiled under Rama I), so cite any specific figure to a named recension and edition [S12], and any interpretation to its named argument [S3, S9].
- **Fictional application:** define rights and obligations before choosing numerical ranks; then decide which differences become audible, visible, spatial, or written. Leave mismatches and contested privileges possible.

## Mythic core: overlapping religious and narrative frames
| Figure / institution | Ramakien or court reference | Do not flatten into |
|---|---|---|
| Phra Ram / Rama | Royal hero and incarnation of Phra Narai/Vishnu; order-restoring epic protagonist [S5, S7] | The sole supreme god of every Thai community. |
| Phra Narai; Phra Isuan | Vishnu and Shiva in Thai naming; Shiva has a major framing role in the court text [S5] | Proof that all Thai worship follows one identical Hindu hierarchy. |
| Phra In / Indra | A figure meaningful in Buddhist cosmology as well as the adapted narrative [S5] | An exclusive “Hindu import” with no locally developed significance. |
| Nang Sida / Sita | Central to abduction, loyalty, suspicion, separation, and reunion narratives [S4–S5] | A silent costume emblem whose interpretation is settled across all versions. |
| Thotsakan / Tosakanth / Ravana | Royal adversary with his own courtly order [S6, S8] | A biological equation between a masked species and absolute evil. |
| Hanuman | Resourceful monkey warrior; prominent martial, comic, and amorous action in Thai tellings [S4] | A universal version of Hanuman, or an assertion that every Indian telling is celibate. |
| Buddhism and kingship | Temple setting, patronage, moral interpretation, and dynastic symbolism frame epic reception [S5] | A requirement that this Ramakien is itself a Buddha-narrated jataka. |

- Reynolds explicitly distinguishes the court Ramakien from the *Dasaratha Jataka* and Lao *Phra Lak/Phra Lam*: do not copy their Buddha/bodhisatta identification into it as an automatic equivalence [S5].
- Local spirit practices, Brahmanical court ritual, Buddhist institutions, and other communities' traditions require their own evidence. “Syncretism” names a question about relations; it does not explain which actors authorize which rites.
- **Interpretive disagreement:** S4 emphasizes variation and enjoyment; S5 argues for Buddhist framing through authorship, epilogue, temple, and dynastic cult. Specify the text, medium, patron, and audience before assigning a religious label.

## Ramakien as an indigenization case study
- Use Ramanujan's **multiple tellings**, not a hierarchy of authentic original versus corrupted copy. Compare selected tellings and performances; do not treat “India” as one invariant source [S4].
- Early routes through regional networks are difficult to recover precisely. Reynolds notes affinities with South Indian, Javanese, and Khmer traditions without pretending that these establish one complete transmission chain [S5, p. 55].
- Ayutthaya's destruction in 1767 and uneven survival of records limit reconstruction; this does not mean that every prior text, object, or tradition vanished [S1, S5].

| Transformation | Evidence / comparison unit | Generative mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Vernacular rearticulation | Thai character names, verse, and local court idiom | Borrow a narrative while translating its social world, not only its nouns. |
| Royal recension | Rama I's supervised recomposition in the early Bangkok court [S5] | Reconstituting cultural authority can accompany a new capital and dynasty. |
| Performance selection | Rama II-associated episodes and later khon scripts [S5–S6] | Repertoire, dancers, music, and occasion determine which epic becomes publicly familiar. |
| Character reweighting | Hanuman's expanded dramatic possibilities in Thai tellings [S4] | Local audience preferences alter emphasis without erasing recognizable relations. |
| Institutional reframing | Ramakien murals around the Emerald Buddha temple and court celebrations [S5] | Story, sacred place, and patronage become mutually interpreting signs. |
| Material translation | Thai-style court regalia, masks, gesture, and spatial etiquette [S6–S8] | The visual vocabulary makes a transregional narrative locally legible. |

- **Worked comparison procedure:** select one episode; record who acts, who speaks, who commissions it, what moral is explicit, what is danced, and what the costume identifies. Compare the same fields in another dated telling.
- Preserve the distinction between **recension**, **script**, **mural cycle**, and **current performance**. A later restoration or stage cut cannot silently stand for the entire Rama I text.
- A ruler's association with Rama does not prove that every spectator interprets the performance as an unqualified endorsement of the reigning court.

## Symbolic system: read combinations, not a colour dictionary
- **Mask identity is composite:** colour + eye/mouth form + tusks + head/crown configuration + costume + movement + narrative context [S6–S8]. A colour swatch alone is insufficient.
- S8 describes a white Hanuman mask and a green Thotsakan mask; S6 also illustrates green Phra Ram and golden Phra Lak masks in an old-style reconstruction. Thus “green means evil” fails within this very corpus.
- Treat those examples as documented character conventions, not permission to infer every mask from one photograph; roles, disguises, workshop traditions, and performance forms need checking.
- Gilding, glitter, crowns, and ornaments participate in idealized courtly display. “Gold = goodness” is inadequate when opposing courts share royal magnificence [S6].
- Seating order, podium height, permitted posture, and messenger etiquette can make rank visible before a character speaks [S6]. These are embodied and spatial signs, not merely decorative scenery.
- The Emerald Buddha functions as a dynastic palladium in Reynolds's analysis; its relationship to Ramakien murals is institutionally specific, not a universal rule that every epic needs a sacred gemstone [S5].
- **Fictional application:** make a sign legible through redundancy; reserve misleading combinations for disguise, disputed succession, or a production's reinterpretation. Label these possibilities as design options, not Thai ethnography.

## Khon: costume, gesture, voice, and material signature
- **โขน** *khon* combines epic, dance, recitation, music, ritual, and craft. Narrators/singers supply much of the text while dancer-actors embody it; do not reduce it to either silent mime or masked spoken dialogue [S6–S7].
- The piphat ensemble, narrators, chorus, and dancers form a coordinated sign system. A costume design detached from movement and musical cueing loses part of the role's identification [S6, S8].

| Role / production layer | Typical visual or bodily channel | Practical reference limit |
|---|---|---|
| Heroic/noble humans | Refined gesture, regal ornaments and crowns; normally unmasked in many modern productions [S6] | “Masked dance” does not mean every performer currently wears a mask. |
| Heroines | A differentiated movement and dress vocabulary [S6] | Historical all-male casting does not establish present casting or performers' gender identities. |
| Yak/demon roles | Full-head masks; powerful, open-legged movement, stamping, and differentiated facial/crown forms [S6–S8] | Do not make one generic demon head serve every named character. |
| Monkey roles | Distinctive masks, playful/acrobatic movement; costume can mark fur [S6, S8] | Actor flexibility and bodily training are part of the material design problem. |
| Mask manufacture | Papier-mâché, paint/lacquer, gilding and reflective decoration; techniques vary [S6–S8] | A reported workshop recipe is not a timeless universal standard. |
| Regalia ensemble | Glittering court/military dress, crowns, jewelry, attributes, and weapons [S6] | Theatrical idealization is not an inventory of daily wear or usable battlefield armor. |
| Ritual object | Teacher/rishi and character masks on altars; respectful handling before performance [S6] | A commercially sold mask and an initiated ritual object need not have the same permissions. |

- The often-proposed khon genealogy linking **nang yai** shadow theatre, martial techniques, and court ritual is a model of several contributing practices, not a securely documented single invention [S6].
- S6 and S8 do not give identical accounts of which early roles wore masks: S6 states that originally all characters wore masks and that only demons and monkeys have worn them since the nineteenth century; S8 states that goddesses, female humans, and some female demons were already unmasked “originally.” Record early masking per source rather than resolving the discrepancy by invention [S6, S8].
- Court-derived costume can communicate class, character, and ideal anatomy together. Do not invent a direct one-to-one mapping from embroidery motifs to legal sakdina numbers.
- **Material gap:** the sources above describe performance at role and mask level (S6, S8) and do not establish component-level Thai garment terminology, weaving origins, metallic-thread specifications, labor time, or sumptuary entitlements for a chosen century; treat those as research questions until a dated textile or legal source is named.
- **Working brief:** specify wearer/role, silhouette, mask visibility and breathing, crown balance, range of movement, maintenance, makers, ritual status, and production date. Treat these as design questions, not an unsourced traditional construction recipe.

## Era model: stage systems separately
| Stage | Language / authority / performance reference | Anachronism check |
|---|---|---|
| Thirteenth–fourteenth-century Tai polities | Early Thai writing; multiple centers, not a ready-made modern nation [S1–S2] | Do not project modern Standard Thai, borders, or present regalia backward wholesale. |
| Ayutthaya, 1351–1767 | Court contact, hierarchy, and developed theatrical traditions; early details remain uneven [S1, S3, S6] | Court continuity is not proof that every modern costume component existed unchanged. |
| Post-1767 transition | Reconstitution between Ayutthaya's fall and Bangkok's foundation in 1782 [S1, S5] | Do not describe preservation as either perfect continuity or total invention after total loss. |
| Early Bangkok/Rattanakosin | Rama I's cultural reconstruction; Rama II-associated performance writing [S5–S6] | Date the recension, the script, and the object separately. |
| Nineteenth-century change | Formal-language standardization and changing theatre/stage technologies [S2 introduction; S6] | Do not assume ceremonial archaism implies technological stasis. |
| Twentieth century to present | Post-1932 institutional changes, education, revivals, modern staging; UNESCO inscription in 2018 [S6–S7] | A revival advertises an earlier model but remains a newly mediated production. |

- These are comparison stages, not a claim that language, institutions, textiles, and religion change on the same date. A fictional era matrix should give each system its own chronology.
- For an image record **depicted story-time / production date / restoration date / documentation date**; temple painting is evidence of representation, not automatically a contemporary clothing photograph.

## Naming practices: administrative, relational, and ceremonial identities
- Contemporary naming commonly distinguishes an official given name, family name, and **chue len** nickname; everyday address often uses a given name or nickname rather than a surname [S10].
- Surnames became officially established by the Surname Act (พระราชบัญญัติขนานนามสกุล), promulgated 22 March 1913 and in force from 1 July 1913, and replaced by the Persons' Name Act B.E. 2505 (1962), under which a surname remains unique to a family line [S10, S13]. Do not issue every Ayutthayan commoner a contemporary-style inherited surname by default.
- Auspicious formal names may draw on Pali/Sanskrit and other lexical sources; nicknames may come from ordinary objects, animals, bodily traits, or modern English. Names are historically layered, not all “sacred Sanskrit” [S2, S10].
- Some naming explanations concern protection from spirits or changes of fortune; treat these as reported practices with variation, not the hidden cause of every nickname or name change [S10].
- A title, office-name, personal name, royal style, and later historical designation must occupy separate fields. Do not assume a ruler's familiar English label was the form used by contemporaries.
- **Khun + given name**, occupational titles, and kinship-like terms illustrate relation-sensitive address [S10]; exact use depends on setting. English “Mr + surname” is a poor default dialogue generator.
- **Design method:** build an identity record with public legal name, household name, occupational/ritual title, who may use each, and dated changes. Generate forms only after the fictional language's sound and borrowing rules are set.

## What a worldbuilder can take
1. **Institutional contact layers:** make ordinary speech, court vocabulary, liturgy, and administrative naming share a language while retaining different histories; do not copy Thai roots as decorative noise.
2. **Relational grammar:** define speaker/addressee/referent separately; test the same event across public reporting, intimate speech, ritual, and performance.
3. **Embodied rank:** couple title, permitted word choice, seating, posture, crown, and service obligations without assuming that they always agree.
4. **Indigenization as production:** track who rewrites an inherited epic, what medium selects, how clothing localizes it, and which institution funds or contests the result.
5. **Composite symbols:** combine colour, face, headdress, movement, and narrated identity; allow one colour on opposed characters rather than a universal moral palette.
6. **Asynchronous eras:** model conservative writing, changing speech, restored murals, revived theatre, and new costume technology on separate timelines.
7. **Handoff, not canon:** send lore-shokunin the proposed coupling, alternatives, era limits, source pointers, and unresolved checks; leave all world-bible decisions to that lane.

## Provenance cautions and stop rules
- **No single-origin national ownership claim:** related Rama performance traditions cross modern borders. UNESCO inscription establishes a safeguarding record, not proof that neighboring traditions are derivatives or unauthorized copies.
- **Court/archive bias:** royal law, murals, and sponsored theatre overrepresent elites. Their ideals do not establish everyday speech, provincial costume, or unanimous belief; chronicles are especially poor substitutes for missing workshop and audience evidence.
- **Colonial/outsider calibration:** early European descriptions and older comparative theatre accounts are situated observations, not transparent inventories; terms such as “harem,” “devil,” and “primitive” can impose an observer's categories. Do not inherit their social explanations unexamined.
- **Scholarly disagreement stays attributed:** Reynolds's Buddhist framing, Ramanujan's broad comparisons, and Kukrit's sakdina argument as analyzed by Waters answer different questions. None is a timeless native consensus or a license to erase coercion.
- **No pop shortcut:** a monomyth checklist, universal colour psychology, or a generic “god-king” template cannot replace a named recension, production, institution, and date.
- **Thin knowledge stays visible:** early khon origins remain a question rather than a date (S6 records a 1691 French-delegation reference and infers older practice); consecration protocols and historical garment components lie outside S6–S8's coverage; specific rank figures require a named legal recension [S12]; and local non-court traditions await their own sources rather than the court record. Seek Thai-language scholarship and appropriate practitioners before detailed reuse.
- **Stop before assertion:** if the only support is an undated mask photograph, tourist gloss, or analogy to another kingdom, record a hypothesis or research question—not a historical fact and never fictional canon.
