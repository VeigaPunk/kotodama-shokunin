## 1. Evidence hierarchy: what each witness can establish

| Witness | Strong use | Inference to refuse |
|---|---|---|
| Dated inscription, with K-number, face and line/stanza | Named donors, titles, transactions, gods, institutional claims at a particular time | A complete census, neutral royal biography, or verbatim everyday speech |
| Sanskrit royal praise, or *praśasti* | Elite genealogy, learned comparison, intended image of sovereignty | Every comparison with a god proves literal identity with that god |
| Old Khmer foundation/administrative record | Land boundaries, service obligations, gifts, disputes, named personnel | “Vernacular” means spontaneous speech, universal literacy, or freedom from ideology |
| Relief with monument and panel context | Represented garment arrangement, ornaments, gesture, narrative distinctions | Fiber, dye recipe, unseen tailoring, or a universal dress code |
| Survey, excavation, hydraulic connections | Settlement fabric, constructed waterworks, spatial relationships | Every mapped feature is contemporary or controlled by one royal office |
| Zhou’s account | A foreign visitor’s late-thirteenth-century observations | A transparent Khmer self-description, or evidence for eighth-century practice |

- Record **date of composition**, **date narrated**, **findspot**, **original setting if known**, **edition**, and **reading confidence** separately. A stele can have been moved.
- [J09], Introduction, explicitly distinguishes written forms from reconstructed pronunciation and warns that many glosses remain conjectural; preserve those warnings when extracting vocabulary.
- Scarce continuous contemporary narrative history makes retrospective genealogies disproportionately influential. “Chronicles say” must name the manuscript tradition, redaction date and claimed earlier event; none is supplied as an all-purpose Angkorian eyewitness here.

## 2. Language, writing, and the Sanskrit–Khmer digraphia distinction

- Khmer belongs to **Austroasiatic**, conventionally placed within “Mon-Khmer”; it is not descended from Sanskrit, Pali, Thai or Vietnamese. Sanskrit is Indo-Aryan; shared vocabulary records contact, not common immediate ancestry [H11; J09].
- Old Khmer is securely represented in seventh-century inscriptions; pre-Angkorian and Angkorian are useful corpus divisions, not instantaneous linguistic replacements [V98; J09, Introduction].
- Khmer commonly combines relatively analytic syntax with inherited derivational prefixation and infixation. Do not erase the older derivational system by copying only a modern tourist phrasebook [J09, Introduction and headword analyses].
- Inscriptions use Brahmic alphasyllabic writing: consonant signs, dependent vowel signs and conjunct/subscript mechanisms. Their historical shapes and sound values must be staged; modern Khmer reading is not a recovered recording of Old Khmer.
- Preserve three layers: **diplomatic text** → **normalized transliteration** → **proposed pronunciation/gloss**. Sanskrit spelling inside Khmer text does not establish fully Sanskrit pronunciation.

| Configuration | Actual distinction | Craft consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Sanskrit praise + Khmer administrative text | Bilingualism, often with differentiated literary functions | Design different audiences, genres and claims; do not make the two passages automatic translations |
| Khmer containing Sanskrit titles and technical words | Borrowing and register differentiation | Loans can carry prestige while Khmer grammar remains Khmer |
| Related regional hands used for both languages | Script history, not two languages magically equaling two scripts | Record language and script in separate fields |
| Yaśodharāśrama stelae, including K.362 | Genuine digraphia: substantially repeated Sanskrit texts on two faces in different scripts; the series can also have a short Khmer addition | One prestige language can be displayed through two graphic traditions [ES11:331 n.6, 333 n.10] |

- [DH26] classifies K.362 face A as Siddhamātṛkā and face B as regular Cambodian script. This is a concrete **two-script** example; its draft script classification should be checked against photographs/estampages before a paleographic reconstruction.
- Thus “Sanskrit–Khmer digraphia” must specify whether **Khmer names a language or a script**. The actual digraphic example is not simply “Sanskrit alphabet versus Khmer alphabet.”
- Sanskrit/Khmer functional differentiation is not an absolute sacred/secular partition: both can name gods, donors, rights and historical events [E23:424–425]. Do not infer exclusive priestly ethnicity from language choice.
- In fiction, decide independently who speaks each language, who composes each genre, who cuts each hand, and whether ritual correctness, local accessibility or royal reach motivates duplication.

## 3. Mythic and pantheon core: plural patronage, not one frozen religion

| Reference complex | Attested anchor | Coupling to model |
|---|---|---|
| Śaiva institutions | Śiva, liṅga consecration, preceptors and temple foundations | Learned ritual expertise + royal authorization + endowed property [S03; E23] |
| Vaiṣṇava imagery and worship | Viṣṇu/Nārāyaṇa; epic and churning imagery prominent at Angkor Wat | Cosmological narrative + monumental program; not proof that every subject was Vaiṣṇava [R05] |
| Mahāyāna patronage | Especially visible under Jayavarman VII; Buddha and bodhisattva imagery | A change in court emphasis can reconfigure foundations without abolishing all other traditions [S03; R05] |
| Place-bound deities | Divine titles, locality-linked names and networks of sacred property | Sacred terrain organizes patronage and affiliation [E23:425–428] |
| Theravāda expansion | Increasing importance in later Angkorian and post-Angkorian contexts | Stage Pali learning, monastic patronage and new uses of older monuments; no single universal conversion date |

- **Pluralism versus syncretism:** Estève [E23] argues that co-residence, shared resources and multi-denominational donors need not entail fused theology. This is a named interpretive proposal, not a decree that religious combination never occurred.
- “Animism under Hinduism” is too crude: it turns local agents into a passive substrate. Modern *neak ta* practices offer comparative questions, not automatic documentation of every Angkorian village’s beliefs [E23:425–426].
- Never distribute a modern all-India pantheon chart unchanged across Khmer places and centuries. Identify the local foundation, cult image, deity’s epithet, affiliated people and patronage history.

## 4. Devarāja: sovereignty, priestly claims, and contested reconstruction

- **Primary anchor:** Sdok Kak Thom, K.235, conventionally cited as 1052 CE [CD43; J09], gives a priestly lineage’s retrospective account of Jayavarman II and a sacral-sovereignty foundation conventionally associated with c.802. It is not an eyewitness charter carved in 802.
- The Khmer expression *kamrateṅ jagat ta rāja* belongs in this discussion alongside Sanskrit *devarāja*; retain the title rather than silently fixing its disputed interpretation as “the king is God.”
- **Older influential model:** the “god-king” becomes a master explanation of Angkor. **Calibration: inadequate as a blanket identification**, not proof that sacral kingship itself was fictitious.
- Kulke [K78] distinguishes the royal tutelary deity from a simple deification of each reigning king. His mobile-image reconstruction is an interpretation, not an excavated certainty; his later account explicitly calls the inference conjectural.
- Sanderson [S03] places royal Śaivism within identifiable ritual and textual traditions; “generic Hindu mysticism” loses the actual specialists and institutional mechanisms.
- Bourdonneau [B11] reopens formation history through Prasat Thom at Koh Ker and its tenth-century evidence. Do not present the whole cult’s object, origin and continuity as settled by one retrospective inscription.
- The tradition’s references to “Java” and sovereignty are not permission to invent a fully documented Javanese occupation with modern borders; the referent and political reconstruction require separate verification.

**Fictional application:** separate the deity’s cult title, the ruler’s political title, a consecrated object, the priestly office’s succession, and the origin story that later legitimates them.
- Test whether the cult can outlive one king, move with a court, or be claimed by a rival lineage; these are design alternatives, not assertions of Khmer historical fact.
- Couple legal endowment to ritual authority: who supplies land and cloth, who receives offerings, who repairs the shrine, and whose genealogy makes those rights appear ancient?

## 5. Symbolic system: treat an image as a situated claim

| Form | Secure contextual anchor | Avoid / fictional design lever |
|---|---|---|
| Mountain and temple elevation | Particular monuments articulate divine residence and royal-sacred centrality | “Every tower = Meru” is not an adequate architectural analysis; model ascent and controlled access |
| Water, moats and reservoirs | Water has practical, consecratory and cosmographic uses | No automatic choice between “only irrigation” and “only cosmic ocean”; allow overlapping functions |
| Liṅga and named divine image | Consecrated presence, named foundation, property and service relations | Not merely a fertility prop, nor invariably the king’s portrait |
| Nāga and garuḍa | Identifiable figures in Khmer iconography and architectural programs | Not a universal serpent/evil versus bird/good code; meaning depends on the represented episode |
| Churning of the Ocean | Specific narrative deployment at Angkor Wat | Model coordinated cosmic labor and contested immortality; do not infer an actual reservoir ceremony from the relief alone |
| Repeated floral/medallion designs | Some architectural patterns have close textile comparanda [G07] | A motif can circulate across trade networks without importing a complete theology |

- Build a **motif ledger**: monument/panel → depicted episode or figure → formal attributes → proposed use → competing reading → date. Use [R05] for identification and [E23] for institutional context; neither replaces object documentation.
- Moral universals (“lotus always means X”) and inherited ethnic labels are weak substitutes for contextual interpretation. Religious titles, property and terrain often couple more securely than colors or numerical symbolism.

## 6. Era staging: overlapping tracks, not a costume-per-dynasty chart

| Working stage | Useful anchors | Language / myth / material coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-Angkorian, especially seventh–eighth centuries | Regional inscriptions and foundations, including the Īśānapura milieu | Old Khmer records and Sanskrit learning already coexist; “pre” does not mean unwritten [V98] |
| Early Angkorian, ninth century | Hariharālaya/Roluos; later Yaśovarman I and Angkor | Temple endowments, reservoirs and āśrama networks couple royal reputation to territory [ES11] |
| Tenth–eleventh centuries | Koh Ker, subsequent Angkor foundations, K.235 | Competing centers and priestly histories; stage title distributions and image styles separately [B11; CD43] |
| Early–mid twelfth century | Sūryavarman II and Angkor Wat | Vaiṣṇava monumental program and a specific relief costume corpus; not a template for all earlier Khmer dress |
| Late twelfth–early thirteenth centuries | Jayavarman VII, Bayon and major foundations | Mahāyāna royal patronage; compare narrative everyday scenes with Angkor Wat’s mythic/courtly programs |
| Later thirteenth–fifteenth centuries and after | Zhou’s 1296–1297 visit; changing centers and religious patronage | Multiple transitions; older monuments remain usable, and the record does not reduce to “everything ended in 1431” [Z07; EV07] |

- Conventional dates bracket research, not synchronized changes in household speech, cloth production and religious allegiance. A carved style, recorded donation and hydraulic modification may have different chronologies.
- Avoid a causal cartoon of one invasion, one religious conversion, or one failed reservoir explaining every transformation. [EV07] establishes networks and cumulative landscape change, not a complete single-cause collapse history.

## 7. Costume and material signature: apsara evidence without projection

- **Separate depiction classes:** moving/airborne heavenly dancers are commonly called *apsaras*; standing female divine attendants are commonly catalogued as *devatās*. These are useful art-historical distinctions, not secure original labels for every figure [R05; G03].
- Angkor Wat’s female figures provide a repertoire of arranged lower-body cloth, belts, pleats or hanging panels, elaborate hair/headdresses, ear ornaments and jewelry; represented exposed torsos must be read within that iconographic setting.
- Compare **Angkor Wat divine/courtly imagery**, **Bayon narrative occupations**, **independent sculpture**, **inscribed inventories**, and **Zhou’s later observations** as different witnesses, not interchangeable costume photographs.

| Visible or textual feature | Defensible use | Unestablished extension |
|---|---|---|
| Carved wrap, waistband, pleats and pendant cloth | Record silhouette, placement and surface treatment | Exact cutting diagram, closure hidden behind the body, or number of loom widths |
| Diadem, coiffure, necklaces, armlets and ear ornaments | Code represented rank/divinity and compare figure classes | Every ornament is solid gold or every coiffure identifies an ethnic group |
| Incised pattern on a garment | Record repeat geometry and its location | Silk versus cotton, ikat versus brocade, color, or local manufacture |
| Architectural medallions compared with surviving foreign textiles | Green’s [G07] argument for luxury-textile connections | Imported textile patterns on a doorjamb directly identify a dancer’s garment |
| Ranked display in a late visitor’s account | Test differential access to cloth and adornment using [Z07] | A universal, unchanged sumptuary law for the entire Angkorian period |
| Yaśodharāśrama rules for modest dress, with rank exceptions [ES11:340] | Textual evidence for regulated adornment at an institutional threshold | An āśrama’s access rules automatically describe all temple or everyday dress |
| Mostly stone sacred survivals beside an extensive inhabited landscape | Keep timber, roofs, household goods and perishable dress in the model | Stone temples constitute the whole city [EV07] |

- [ES11:340] discusses dress rules permitting kings, Brahmins and royal sons to retain their ornaments, alongside restrictions on vehicles and parasols near the āśrama. This couples elite display to ritual access, rather than establishing timeless “Khmer modesty.”
- *Sampot* is a useful modern garment-category label, not proof that every carved wrapper bore that precise contemporary name or used today’s production techniques. Period-specific terminology needs its own attestation.
- “Apsara costume” in modern performance is evidence for that performance tradition; resemblance to reliefs is not independent proof of uninterrupted medieval tailoring, movement or ritual role.
- To reconstruct for fiction, make a **representation sheet** and a separate **manufacturing hypothesis**: fiber supply → spinning/weaving → dyeing → draping/fastening → maintenance → authorized wearers. Mark hidden construction choices as design choices.
- Keep court, deity, ritual worker, soldier, trader and agricultural worker as distinct comparison sets. Do not outfit an entire population as temple attendants.

## 8. Hydraulic-state vocabulary: attested words, not invented ministries

[J09] headwords below support the lexical distinctions; [EV07] supports the archaeological network, not the historical identification of every named office with a particular canal.

| Term and provenance | Bounded working gloss | Generative coupling / caution |
|---|---|---|
| Old Khmer *dik¹* | Water, liquid; also running water/waterway | Drinking, ablutions and channels share a lexical field; distinguish the Sanskrit-derived homograph *dik²*, “side/direction” |
| Old Khmer *travāṅ*, variants *travaṅ*, *trāvaṅ* | Standing water; pond, pool, reservoir/tank | Settlement naming and storage; neither automatically a monumental reservoir nor a purely artificial pond |
| *taṭāka*, with attested variants, from Sanskrit | Pond, pool, reservoir | Prestige naming can coexist with ordinary water vocabulary; loans do not by themselves prove imported engineering |
| Old Khmer *sre* | Ricefield, conventionally glossed as irrigated ricefield | Land transfer and supply obligations; one word does not identify the source or scale of irrigation |
| Old Khmer *sruk¹* | Community and its territory; also an administrative center/unit | Territorial affiliation; not mechanically the size or jurisdiction of a modern district |
| Old Khmer *khloñ* | Chief, head, director | Read the complement: *khloñ sruk*, community head; *khloñ vnaṃ*, sanctuary head; *khloñ kāryya*, director of works |
| Old Khmer *vraḥ* | Divine/royal being or object; sacred/royal usage in titles and phrases | Ritual and political authority overlap lexically; not a one-word English adjective usable in every context |
| Old Khmer *khñuṃ* | Slavery, forced labor or dependent sanctuary service, according to context | Endowments allocate human labor as well as goods; do not sanitize coercion or collapse all dependency into one legal status |
| *Baray*, current archaeological/topographic label | Large reservoir in discussions of Angkor | Do not silently treat the modern label as the Old Khmer headword for every excavated basin |

- **Hydraulic city: strongly supported as infrastructure** [EV07]. **Wittfogel-style hydraulic despotism: outmoded deterministic model**, not established by the existence of large waterworks.
- Network functions include catching, storing and redistributing water; embankments can also carry roads and settlement. Mapping connections is firmer than assigning one exclusive function or centralized command chain to every feature.
- In fiction, distinguish household ponds, endowed reservoirs, canals, bunded fields, ritual washing and transport routes; then specify seasonal labor, maintenance rights, supply claims and dispute vocabulary. Do not invent a Khmer “water minister” title and present it as attested.

## 9. Naming practices: identify the bearer before translating the name

| Layer | Reference pattern | What to retain structurally |
|---|---|---|
| Royal learned names | Sanskrit compounds ending in *-varman*, as in Jayavarman and Sūryavarman | Royal name is not a modern hereditary surname; repeated components do not establish close kinship |
| Divine and foundation names | Named liṅgas, deity epithets, place-linked gods; *-īśvara* compounds | Distinguish deity, founder, dedication and locality instead of treating the whole compound as a person |
| Office and address | *vraḥ*, *kamrateṅ*, *khloñ* and their combinations | Titles are institutional evidence, not ornamental syllables; rank and distribution can change [J09; E23] |
| Local place names | Terrain/community vocabulary alongside Sanskrit prestige formations such as *-pura* | Layer local geography and commemorative renaming rather than applying one language to every place |
| Named service personnel | Inscribed dependent-person lists and status labels | These are institutional recordings, not a neutral inventory of self-chosen everyday names |

- Distinguish birth/personal name, bestowed court name, office, divine dedication and posthumous designation where the source permits. Do not assume that every bearer has all five.
- Roman numerals distinguishing kings are modern scholarly apparatus, not syllables to put into ancient dialogue. Do not derive a fictional society’s phonotactics solely from its Sanskrit royal name list.
- [J09], Introduction, excludes a large body of proper names from full dictionary coverage: it is not an exhaustive name generator. Return to inscriptions for bearer, gender/status, date and naming context.
- For a fictional naming system, define **native personal-name morphology + learned title register + place-name layer + rules of conferral**; hand alternatives to lore-shokunin, not a list presented as settled fictional history.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **An institution has several voices:** learned praise, vernacular legal record, graphic display and embodied ceremony can make different claims about the same foundation.
2. **Language and script are independent design axes:** bilingual complementary text and monolingual digraphic repetition are different, historically grounded mechanisms.
3. **Sacred terrain has an economy:** deity titles, endowed fields, workers, cloth and water connect cosmology to enforceable obligations without making belief a mere disguise for economics.
4. **Dress is distributed evidence:** derive clothing from represented body arrangements, supply chains and rank contexts, while leaving fiber, color and hidden construction uncertain where evidence does.
5. **Change is asynchronous:** court patronage, everyday worship, graphic habits, reservoir maintenance and costume fashion need not turn together at a dynastic boundary.
6. **Handoff packet:** reference → dated observation → inference → competing explanation → fictional transformation → uncertainty. Lore-shokunin decides which, if any, become world-bible canon.

## 11. Provenance cautions and limits

- **Colonial archive bias:** [IC/CD43] remain indispensable editions, but their royal, Sanskritic and monumental emphasis is not the society itself. Use later Khmer scholarship and re-editions, not colonial categories as neutral native self-description.
- **Dating calibration:** K.235 records its date as **974 śaka** together with a Sanskrit horoscope stanza [CC25]; the DHARMA edition’s translation glosses that year as 1052 A.D., and because Śaka 974 spans the civil years 1052–1053, 1053 citations also occur. The text supplies a horoscope rather than a month and tithi, so exact civil-date conversion is a reconstruction resting on calendrical and astronomical assumptions; this entry carries both citation forms and does not adjudicate them. Likewise 802 is a conventional reconstruction, not an independently observed birth certificate of an empire.
- **Source-access calibration:** [J09] Introduction and cited headwords, [ES11], [E23], [G07] selected pages and [EV07] provide directly checked working evidence. [CD43/S03] online openings identify their arguments and editions; the full critical apparatus has not been collated here. [V98/G03/R05/K78] are identified standard references, not claims that every page was re-read. [CC25] was checked in its draft edition and translation, [KL22] in full text; [AC86] is bibliographically verified, not read.
- **Translation calibration:** no new Old Khmer or Sanskrit translation is offered. Headword glosses are bounded paraphrases of [J09]; damaged readings, literal quotations and exact title segmentation require the cited edition and inscription location.
- **Thin areas (calibrated, not filled):** non-elite regional dress, Khmer-language scholarship, oral performance history, detailed post-Angkor continuities and everyday local spirit practice are underrepresented here, and no continuity claim is supplied for them. The reason is evidentiary: local epigraphy nearly disappears from the thirteenth to the sixteenth century and the royal chronicles were compiled mainly in the eighteenth and nineteenth, so post-Angkor primary sources are scarce [KL22], while *neak ta*-type practice is documented chiefly by twentieth-century ethnography [AC86]. Both classes seed comparative questions only; modern folklore and performance costume are not used as documentation.
- **Chronicle scarcity and observer bias:** elite inscriptions are interested records; Zhou is a brief, mediated outsider witness whose transmitted text is incomplete. Neither supplies a continuous social history. Missing testimony is not proof that a practice was absent.
- **Digital and visual caution:** [DH26] is explicitly a draft; record its revision when using it. Carved pattern resemblance supports a comparison, not an automatic identification of trade route, dye, weave, ethnicity or religious doctrine.
- Borrow relationships and institutional questions, not Khmer sacred names and living performance dress as interchangeable exotic decoration; keep reference facts, scholarly hypotheses and fictional choices visibly separate.
