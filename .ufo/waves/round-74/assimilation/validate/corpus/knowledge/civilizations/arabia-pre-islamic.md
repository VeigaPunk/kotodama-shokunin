## Scope and evidence calibration
- Arabia is a region, not one polity, language, religion, or wardrobe. Distinguish the Ḥijāz, northern basalt desert, oasis kingdoms, southern Arabia, and imperial borderlands before combining features.
- Jāhiliyyah is a retrospective Islamic moral-historical category, not an archaeological stage of universal illiteracy or cultural emptiness (Hoyland 2001, chs. 8–9; Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 1).
- Evidence labels below distinguish **EP** contemporary epigraphy, **PO** transmitted poetry, **Q** early seventh-century Qurʾānic discourse, **LT** later literary tradition, and **DESIGN** fictional inference. These are evidence types, not a numerical confidence ranking.

| Evidence | What it can support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| EP: Safaitic prayers, names, narrative graffiti | Particular writers' affiliations, requests, seasonal activities, ritual words | A pan-Arabian creed, sixth-century Meccan practice, or universal nomadic custom |
| PO: poems attributed to pre-Islamic poets | Poetic repertoire, social ideals, remembered lexicon; assess individual transmissions | Verbatim recordings of everyday speech or equal authenticity of every attributed verse |
| Q: sanctuary, trade, divinities, jinn | Vocabulary and arguments recognizable at the Islamic transition | An unmediated description of opponents' beliefs or a complete earlier ritual manual |
| LT: sīrah, genealogies, idol reports, lexica | Later memory, competing genealogies, interpretations worth testing | Eyewitness status merely because a narrative describes an earlier period |
| Images, garments, objects | Dated local silhouettes, techniques, materials, representations | Identical clothing across regions, genders, centuries, and social strata |

- **Thin coverage:** continuous indigenous myth narratives, sixth-century Meccan commercial documentation, and everyday women's dress are poorly represented in this entry's evidence base; do not fill those gaps with invented historical detail.

## Era model: stage by region and institution
| Working horizon | Language/writing and religious setting | Worldbuilding control |
|---|---|---|
| First millennium BCE | Oasis and southern Arabian polities; multiple local alphabets and cults | Choose a particular oasis or kingdom; do not call all inscriptions Arabic |
| Safaitic conventional horizon, roughly first century BCE–fourth century CE | Literate mobile groups in southern Syria/northeastern Jordan and adjoining deserts; personal rock texts | Chronological limits are uncertain; do not convert the conventional range into exact dates for every graffito |
| Fourth–sixth centuries CE | Jewish and Christian affiliations, especially visible in southern Arabia; Roman/Byzantine, Aksumite, and Sasanian connections | Allow religious and material changes to overlap; no synchronized peninsula-wide conversion stage |
| Sixth–early seventh centuries CE | Period to which much surviving early poetry is attributed; Meccan sanctuary and Quraysh appear through uneven evidence | Keep poetic attribution, Qurʾānic witness, and later Meccan narrative on separate source tracks |
| Islamic transition and subsequent compilation | Qurʾānic reformulation; later collection of poetry, genealogies, and histories | Compilation date is not composition date; neither automatic fabrication nor automatic authenticity follows |

- Basis: Hoyland (2001, regional chapters and ch. 6); Al-Jallad (2022, ch. 1, especially chronology cautions). This is a comparison grid, not an evolutionary ladder from “tribal” to “civilized.”
- In a fictional era ledger, track script adoption, shrine administration, religious affiliation, textile imports, and lineage politics independently; their turning points need not coincide.

## Language family, scripts, and registers
- Arabic belongs to the Semitic branch of Afroasiatic; **Old Arabic** denotes pre-Islamic Arabic varieties, not everything written anywhere in Arabia (Al-Jallad 2015; Hoyland 2001, ch. 8).
- Safaitic is a modern name for a writing tradition/alphabet, not the name of a single ancient nation. Al-Jallad treats its language as varieties of Old Arabic; older “Ancient North Arabian” classifications should not be read as a settled linguistic family tree.
- **Ancient North Arabian** is a conventional umbrella for scripts; it does not establish a single religion or ethnicity. Dadanitic and Taymanitic require their own linguistic descriptions; Ancient South Arabian languages such as Sabaic are not simply Arabic in a different font.
- The Arabic alphabet developed through the Nabataean Aramaic writing tradition; Safaitic is not its earlier typographic stage. Language, script, political identity, and religious allegiance can cross-cut one another (Hoyland 2001, ch. 8; Al-Jallad 2015).
- Safaitic normally records consonants without a full vowel notation, permits varying writing directions, and has no obligatory modern spacing. Retain editorial transliterations rather than inventing vowels to make every name look Classical Arabic.
- Register design: distinguish local conversation, public genealogical identification, petitionary inscription, heightened ode, and ritual utterance. Their existence does not imply separate languages or mutually unintelligible speech.
- The surviving poetic language is conventionally described as an elevated, supra-local repertoire; its exact relation to spoken dialects and editorial normalization is debated. Do not use the phrase “poetic koine” as a proved account of how all Arabs spoke.

## Qaṣīdah: authority through ordered speech
- **Qaṣīdah**, plural **qaṣāʾid**: a sustained ode conventionally using one quantitative metre and a recurring rhyme. A **bayt** has two hemistichs; later prosodic terminology and classification are not evidence of a surviving pre-Islamic classroom syllabus (Arberry 1957; Stetkevych 1993).
- Arabic metre organizes syllabic quantity, not English stress counting. In fiction, choose whether translated verse reproduces imagery, performance function, or a formal analogue; do not pretend all three survive unchanged.

| Component or mode | Lexical/imagistic work | Social coupling and calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Nasīb: remembered beloved and departed camp | Aṭlāl, traces of habitation; place-names, absence, remembered intimacy | Loss establishes a speaker situated in remembered relationships; not every ode contains the same opening |
| Raḥīl: journey | Camel anatomy, endurance, terrain, weather, movement | Specialized vocabulary establishes competence and bridges memory to public assertion |
| Fakhr: self/group praise | Descent, courage, generosity, hospitality | Language makes claims on rank and collective reputation; an idealized boast is not a statistical social description |
| Madīḥ: praise of another | Patronal merit, giving, protection | Links poetic authority to redistribution and elite recognition; court and tribal settings differ |
| Hijāʾ: invective | Disgrace, cowardice, failed hospitality or descent claims | Reputation is contested through language; do not turn every insult into an ethnographic fact |
| Rithāʾ: elegy | Named dead, virtues, grief, remembered obligations | Mourning can sustain social memory; elegies need not follow the full ode's sequence |

- Nasīb → raḥīl → praise/boast is a useful teaching model, not a mandatory three-act law; read particular odes attributed to Imruʾ al-Qays, Ṭarafah, or Zuhayr before generating a template (Arberry 1957).
- Stetkevych's **ritual-poetic reading**, using separation/liminality/reintegration, helps ask how an ode negotiates social belonging. **Status: interpretive and debated**, not a universal initiation script or a Campbell-style monomyth.
- **DESIGN:** distribute archaic words by performance domain: kin terms in praise, hydrology in journeys, garment terms in honor imagery. Maintain a plainer everyday register instead of making everyone speak in ethnographic exposition.
- Poetry, divination, and revelation are contested kinds of authoritative speech, not interchangeable jobs: Q 69:41–42 distinguishes poet and soothsayer while denying those labels to the revelation.

## Divinities: regional cults before a synthetic pantheon
| Name or transcription | Witness and usable core | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Allāt, Safaitic ʾlt/lt | EP: frequent petitions for safety, reunion, and help | AWS 283 and AWS 291 call her daughter of Roḍaw; do not universalize that genealogy |
| Baʿal-Samīn, bʿlsmn | EP: rain/storm association; LP 722 and C 1240 connect withheld rain to distress | Specialized roles do not prohibit petitions to other gods for the same relief |
| Roḍaw/Roḍay, rḍw/rḍy | EP: aid, life, protection, vengeance | A fixed “moon god” identification is a hypothesis, not a secure label inferred from the name |
| Dusares/Dushara, dśr | EP: Nabataean-linked deity invoked by Safaitic writers alongside others | A borrowed or shared divine name does not prove political incorporation or identical theology |
| Allāh, ʾlh in cited Safaitic texts | EP: petitions, e.g. KRS 2018; Q: central divine name | The name predates Islam; neither one universal pre-Islamic office nor Qurʾānic theology follows from a name alone |
| Al-Lāt, al-ʿUzzā, Manāt | Q 53:19–23 names these goddesses in polemic | The passage is not a manual of their cults; fixed planetary assignments or a universal maiden–mother–crone triad are unsupported here |

- Sources: Al-Jallad (2022, ch. 3); Q 53:19–23. Divine kinship, astral interpretations, localized shrines, and protection of social groups require separate evidence rather than a Greek-style family chart imposed on every site.
- Gadd names can link tutelary fortune/protection to groups: Gadd-Ḍayf and Gadd-ʿAwīḏ are not simply interchangeable “gods of luck” detached from their communities (Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 3 §2).
- Later idol narratives can preserve genuine names while reorganizing them into a polemic about lost monotheism. Their narrative completeness does not make them more contemporary than a laconic inscription (Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 1).

## Jinn vocabulary: early witness is not a fantasy taxonomy
| Term | Meaning/function supported here | Staging caution |
|---|---|---|
| Jinn; related jānn | Q 72 treats jinn as hearers and speakers; Q 55:15 describes fiery creation | Q describes them within its own theology, not a complete recoverable pre-Islamic cosmology |
| Rijāl min al-jinn | Q 72:6 mentions humans seeking refuge with “men” among jinn | Supports a relationship of protection/refuge in the discourse, not a documented universal spirit-clan constitution |
| Shayṭān / shayāṭīn | Satan/devil vocabulary; Q 37:7 describes a rebellious shayṭān | Do not automatically equate all jinn with evil: Q 72:11 distinguishes moral orientations |
| ʿIfrīt | Q 27:39: a powerful figure “from the jinn,” offering extraordinary service | Not evidence of a universal species rank with a fixed color, element, or costume |
| Mārid | Rebellious/defiant descriptor in Q 37:7 | A descriptive word is not automatically the proper name of a separate elemental race |
| Kāhin; shāʿir | Soothsayer; poet, distinguished in Q 69:41–42 | Stories of a poet's personal spirit require a particular transmitted witness, not an assumption about every poet |
| Ġūl | Attested in archaic poetry with edited witnesses (Taʾabbaṭa Šarran's two ghūl poems; ʿAntara's Algol line; a simile in Imruʾ al-Qays) — Zakharia 2015; EI2 s.v. “Ghūl” | Mentions are relatively rare and often figurative; do not build a fixed species taxonomy or spirit ranks out of later folklore |

- **Calibration:** lamp-bound wish-granting genies, elaborate spirit ranks, and later regional folklore cannot be smuggled into this base as an ancient Arabian consensus.
- **DESIGN:** make protection, speech attribution, disputed inspiration, and dangerous places relational systems. A traveler, poet, shrine keeper, and religious reformer can categorize the same encounter differently; these are design options, not claims of historical belief.
- Safaitic refuge formulas, such as ASFF 260 invoking Roḍaw, are comparative evidence for protective language, not proof that gods and jinn were one category (Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 3 §3).

## Symbolic system: acts and contexts before meanings
| Carrier | Evidence-grounded reading | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|
| Nṣb, standing/cult stone | Erection can precede sacrifice; divine presence is a scholarly reconstruction of its function | Every standing stone was the same god, or every nṣb was a grave marker |
| Nfs(t), funerary installation | Commemoration of dead humans; construction and mourning can be recorded together | A fully specified soul doctrine from cognate vocabulary alone |
| Camel image | Some images are explicitly dedicated: LP 317 = IS.M 92 | Every camel engraving was religious, or “camel = resilience” everywhere |
| Named trace and protected inscription | RSIS 126 combines genealogy, grief, benefit for readers, and a curse on effacers | All writing was magical, or all graffiti was idle doodling |
| Garment and camp trace in poetry | Clothing can figure honor; ruined habitation can figure absence and memory | A fixed heraldic code, a dress regulation, or literal costume documentation in every simile |

- Sources: Al-Jallad (2022, ch. 1; ch. 2 §§2, 6.3); Stillman (2003, p. 9). Keep object identification, textual association, and inferred meaning as separate fields.
- **DESIGN:** connect symbol to upkeep: who erects the stone, replaces an offering, recites a name, supplies cloth, or protects a text? Recurring obligations make symbolism social rather than decorative.

## Meccan pilgrimage economy: lexicon without an assumed trade empire
- **Baseline:** Q 106 links Quraysh, seasonal journeying, the House, food, and security; Q 28:57 evokes a secure sanctuary receiving produce; Q 9:19 recognizes water provision to pilgrims. None supplies commercial turnover, cargo lists, or a complete pre-Islamic office chart.
- **Contested:** Crone (1987, especially chs. 3–8) challenges the projection of much earlier incense/spice commerce onto Muhammad's Mecca. Her challenge does not establish that Mecca had no exchange, pilgrimage, or regional provisioning; her wider reconstruction is not settled consensus.

| Lexeme | Minimal sense and witness | Language–institution coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Ḥaram; ḥarām | Sacred/inviolable precinct; prohibited or sacred status; Q 28:57 | Distinguish a bounded place from an adjective/status, even where meanings overlap |
| Bayt; Kaʿbah | House, Q 106:3; Kaʿbah, Q 5:97 | A shrine name can anchor service, guardianship, and public identity without establishing its exact ancient building form |
| Ḥajj; ʿumrah | Pilgrimage observances distinguished in Q 2:196 | A shared sacred journey need not mean identical rites at every Arabian sanctuary |
| Riḥlah | Journey, Q 106:2, in winter/summer pairing | Named seasons are not proof of precise destinations, goods, or convoy size |
| Īlāf | Q 106:1–2; renderings include accustomed security, composing, and covenants | Its exact institutional meaning is disputed; do not silently translate it as a documented interstate trade treaty |
| Siqāyah | Giving water to pilgrims, Q 9:19 | Service can generate prestige; detailed hereditary office allocations require separate LT evidence |
| Rifādah | Feeding/provisioning pilgrims; later Meccan accounts attribute the office, with siqāya and dār al-nadwa, to Quṣayy b. Kilāb (scheme reviewed in Ghabin 2008:67, citing Watt 1953:13 and Kister) | The reports are late and imprecise; financing details and office-holders are not read out of Q 9:19 |
| Sūq, plural aswāq | Market; Q 25:7, 20 supplies early markets vocabulary; literary sources give the ʿUkāẓ–Majannah–Majāz sequence in Dhū l-Qaʿda–Dhū l-Ḥijja (Al-Muaikel 1995) | The calendar rests on later reports, and excavation found no pre-Islamic installations at ʿUkāẓ — keep dates and operating rules source-dependent |
| Hady; qalāʾid | Dedicated sacrificial animals and garlands, Q 5:2, 97 | Animals and visible markers link ritual recognition to material obligations; not generic clan heraldry |
| Ashhur ḥurum; nasīʾ | Sacred months and condemned postponement, Q 9:36–37 | The earlier calendar and interpretations of nasīʾ are contested; do not assume an unchanged modern calendar |

- Q 2:198's “seeking bounty” is commonly interpreted as trading during pilgrimage; translations that insert “trade” are interpreting rather than reproducing an explicit tijārah in that verse.
- **DESIGN:** model arrivals → water/fodder/food demand → protection and mediation → reputational credit for providers. This is an institutional hypothesis for fiction, not a quantified reconstruction of Meccan revenue.
- Keep pilgrimage and markets analytically separable: their co-location is a question to test, not the definition of either institution. A prestigious sanctuary need not be an international luxury-goods entrepôt.

## Safaitic: compact models of social and sacred speech
- The basalt-desert inscriptions commonly combine **l- genealogy → w- narrative → w-/f- prayer or curse**. Al-Jallad (2022, ch. 1) argues that the narrative and petition should be interpreted together, not mined as unrelated vocabulary lists.
- **AMSI 71:** `l bny bn bny bn nẓr w ḏbḥ f h lt slm` records an author and paternal chain, sacrifice, and an invocation to Allāt for security (Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 1 §2). Preserve the consonantal names; supplied vocalizations would be another inference.
- **ZEGA 1:** `l gfft bn kn ḏ-ʾl ʾty` identifies Gfft, son of Kn, of the lineage ʾty, before mentioning a cult stone and petition (Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 2 §2.1). This is a real excerpt, not a generated inscription.
- Lexical coupling: **slm**, security; **qbll**, reunion; **rwḥ**, relief/wind in context; **ḏbḥ**, sacrifice; **wgm**, grieving. Select meanings from an edited context and the dictionary, not a modern Arabic cognate alone.
- Safaitic **ḥg/ḥgg** is pilgrimage vocabulary, but the named destination Sʿʿ in BRENV.A 1 is Seia/Sīʿ in the south Syrian region—not Mecca (Al-Jallad 2022, ch. 2 §4). Cognacy does not identify a destination or a ritual sequence.
- **DESIGN:** let ordinary economic activities motivate petitions: delayed rain → relief; separation → reunion; threatened journey → security. Formulaic language can be compact and personal without being mechanically identical.

## Qabīlah and naming: identification is not a modern surname
| Element | Practical reference pattern | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Personal name, ism | An individual identifier, retained with its source spelling | Do not auto-vocalize unvowelled inscriptions or modernize every old form |
| Nasab, descent chain | Arabic ibn/bin, “son”; bint, “daughter”; Safaitic bn/bnt | Chain length varies by context; an ancestor's name is not necessarily a hereditary surname |
| Qabīlah, plural qabāʾil | Tribal affiliation; Banū N, “sons/descendants of N,” with Banī in oblique forms | A tribe can be a political affiliation; genealogical presentation is not a genetic test |
| Lineage affiliation in Safaitic | ḏ-ʾl + group name, as in ZEGA 1 | Do not force every group into later Arabic genealogists' ranked branch taxonomy |
| Nisbah, relational adjective | Affiliation by group/place; Arabic -ī, feminine -iyyah | Not every name has one; Safaitic affiliation forms need their own grammar |
| Kunyah, Abū/Umm + name | An additional relational form in Arabic naming traditions | Do not impose a fixed multi-part medieval naming template on every earlier attestation |
| Theophoric name | Divine element within a personal name; ʿbdʾl occurs in LP 460 | A name may be inherited; it does not establish a person's complete cultic allegiance |

- Basis: Hoyland (2001, ch. 5); Al-Jallad (2015); Al-Jallad (2022, ch. 2 ZEGA 1; ch. 3 LP 460). Keep claimed descent, political membership, residence, patronage, and a narrator's identification distinct.
- **DESIGN naming procedure:** define the local name stock and sound history; choose the social relationship relevant to the scene; add only the affiliation that the speaker needs; maintain an alternate record for outsiders' spellings.
- Avoid mechanically assigning everyone *given name + ibn + father + tribe*. Inscribed author, mourning relative, diplomatic representative, and praised patron have different identification needs; gender and social position must not disappear from the roster.

## Costume and material signature: bounded evidence, not a timeless uniform
| Item or context | Reference basis | Safe craft use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Izār | Stillman (2003, pp. 7–10): wrap/waistcloth; representations and literary comparisons | Choose drape and use separately; a garment label does not recover an exact cut or fiber |
| Ridāʾ, burd, mirṭ, shamla | Mantle/wrap vocabulary discussed with early poetry, pp. 9, 13 | Clothing can carry honor, ornament, and occasion; terms overlap and historical usage varies |
| Qamīṣ, thawb | Early Islamic descriptions, pp. 11–12; thawb also broadly “garment” | Useful transition-period comparison, not proof of the modern Gulf white thawb's silhouette in antiquity |
| Sirwāl and Persian-connected dress | Stillman pp. 8, 10–11: imperial-borderland depictions and disputed early use of trousers | Treat imported forms, adoption dates, and distribution as questions; no identical peninsula-wide outfit |
| Sandals, head coverings, jewelry, layering | Sparse images and texts; Stillman pp. 7–13; Hoyland ch. 7 | Build wear, fastening, repair, and access to materials; exact local combinations need dated witnesses |
| Pilgrim garments | Stillman's ancient-wrap comparison and early Islamic consecration context | Separate ritual status, garment prescription, and documented practice; do not project every later iḥrām rule backward |

- **Source-bias warning:** Stillman's pp. 8–9 employ a “higher civilizations” contrast and strong Bedouin-continuity language. Reject the hierarchy and the inference that modern nomadic dress preserves antiquity unchanged; retain the individually cited evidence.
- Fibers, dyes, embroidery, and gendered color rules need local dating. Do not give every clan an attested tartan, equate all veiling with one status, or treat modern keffiyeh patterns as a recovered pre-Islamic heraldic system.
- **DESIGN coupling:** recurring cloth imports can produce garment loanwords; a poet may turn a mantle into an honor metaphor; sanctuary service may require recognizable ritual presentation. These are mechanisms to specify, not historical costume prescriptions.

## What a worldbuilder can take—and provenance cautions
- Take **coupled mechanisms**, not a cultural reskin: reputation sustained by heightened speech; kinship made public through names; precarious journeys linked to protective language; sanctuary service tied to provisioning; durable writing prolonging remembered relationships.
- For each proposed feature record **region → horizon → evidence type → exact witness → alternative interpretation → fictional transformation**. Cross-region borrowing needs an explicit contact route; it is not explained by the label “Arabian.”
- Prototype one naming exchange, one short commemorative text, one performance-register contrast, and one garment description. Check that all use the same era assumptions without requiring identical registers or religious interpretations.
- Hand lore-shokunin options and constraints, not canon: retain source identifiers, mark DESIGN decisions as proposals, and leave acceptance and world-bible recording to that sibling.
- The former uncertainty flags around ġūl attestation, rifādah, and named-market chronology were resolved 2026-10-17 against the sources added above (Zakharia 2015; Ghabin 2008; Al-Muaikel 1995); keep their calibrated wording. Do not “complete” the reference by inventing anecdotes, pantheon biographies, or commercial figures.
- Calibration rule: an inscription secures an occurrence, not universal practice; a poem represents an artistic claim, not a census; a polemic is evidence of a dispute, not neutral ethnography; a scholarly reconstruction remains revisable.
