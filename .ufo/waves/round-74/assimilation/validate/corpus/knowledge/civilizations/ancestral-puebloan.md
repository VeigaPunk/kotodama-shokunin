## 1. Scope and confidence: an archaeological grouping is not one people-language

- **Ancestral Puebloan** is an archaeological-cultural umbrella across regions and centuries, not the name of a single state, ethnicity, language, or uniform religion (Cordell and McBrinn 2012).
- Use **Ancestral Pueblo people** rather than treating “Anasazi” as an ancient self-name. The latter is a Navajo-derived label with disputed translations and differing community preferences (NPS 2018).
- Pueblo peoples are living descendants, not survivors of a vanished race. Leaving a settlement did not end relationships with ancestral places (NPS, “People”; “History & Culture”).
- Distinguish Mesa Verde/northern San Juan, Chaco, ancestral Zuni districts, Hopi areas, and Rio Grande communities; a feature documented in one is not automatically evidence for the others.
- **O = observation:** excavated form, dated occupation, identifiable material; **E = ethnographic/public account:** a named community or historical narrator's interpretation; **H = hypothesis:** argued connection between records; **D = design:** proposed fictional mechanism.
- These are entry-local evidence labels, not grades of a community's knowledge. Oral histories need attribution and appropriate access, not automatic demotion beneath outsiders' written records.
- Direct historical analogy asks how documented descendants illuminate an earlier practice; it does not license projecting every modern institution backward unchanged.

## 2. Language family, contact, and writing

| Linguistic unit | Secure baseline | Boundary that matters to craft |
|---|---|---|
| Keresan / Keres | No generally accepted external genealogical relationship; described as an isolate with varieties or a small family | Eastern and Western differentiation is internal history, not evidence for a Keresan–Zuni parent language |
| Western Keres | Acoma and Laguna varieties | Do not turn either community's usage into a pan-Keresan standard |
| Eastern Keres | Cochiti, Kewa, San Felipe, Santa Ana, and Zia varieties | Present distributions are not a map of languages in thirteenth-century ruins |
| Zuni | Language isolate: no demonstrated surviving genetic relatives | “Isolate” does not mean culturally isolated, unchanged, or never involved in multilingual contact |
| Hopi | Uto-Aztecan language | Shared Pueblo practices do not make Hopi a Keresan or Zuni dialect |
| Tewa, Tiwa, Towa | Tanoan languages, conventionally grouped with Kiowa as Kiowa–Tanoan | Ortman's Tewa argument cannot simply be relabeled Keresan or Zuni |

- Classification baseline: Campbell (1997); Keresan internal comparison: Miller and Davis (1963); Zuni isolate definition: Hill (2008).
- **Comparative method:** establish recurring sound correspondences and shared innovations; resemblance in a ceremonial word can instead reflect borrowing, translation, or chance.
- **Pueblo linguistic area:** interaction can spread vocabulary or speech practices across unrelated languages. This is a contact model, not a new family tree (Campbell 1997).
- Historical proposals linking Zuni or Keresan to large speculative stocks are not established genealogy; never use “Hokan,” “Penutian,” or “Amerind” as a settled parent node here.
- The precontact record used here does not supply a deciphered corpus of language-encoding texts that identifies speech at each archaeological site. Rock art and pottery designs must not be treated as a readable phonetic script.
- Distinguish **orality**, **graphic symbolism**, and **writing that encodes language**; sophisticated historical memory does not require an alphabet (NPS 2018; Cushing 1896, with mediation cautions below).
- Modern language documentation and practical orthographies belong to later histories; copying a modern spelling does not authenticate a medieval pronunciation.
- Local Keresan preferences about recording, publishing, or teaching must be checked community by community: Pueblo communities run their own school and immersion programmes (e.g. the Keres-medium Keres Children's Learning Center at Cochiti; New Mexico districts teaching Keres, per U.S. Senate 2018), so a universal “Pueblo ban on writing” would be an unsupported generalization either way.
- **D application:** use separate descent trees and contact networks; shared architecture and translated ritual concepts can coexist with mutually unrelated languages.

## 3. Era staging: use the Pecos sequence as scaffolding, not destiny

Approximate conventional boundaries below follow the regional synthesis tradition represented by Cordell and McBrinn (2012); boundaries vary geographically and are not named Indigenous eras.

| Analytical stage | Approximate span | Material/settlement emphasis | Linguistic inference allowed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Basketmaker II | Before c. 500 CE; starting dates vary by regional sequence | Farming, baskets, fiber technologies, pithouses; regional variation | No automatic ancestral language assignment |
| Basketmaker III | c. 500–750 CE | Pottery and bow-and-arrow use; pithouse communities | Technology change does not prove language replacement |
| Pueblo I | c. 750–900 CE | More aboveground room blocks alongside pit structures | Architectural aggregation may create contact, not necessarily a new family |
| Pueblo II | c. 900–1150 CE | Masonry villages; Chacoan great-house networks in part of the region | Network participation need not imply one official language |
| Pueblo III | c. 1150–1300 CE | Aggregation, some cliff settlements, regional reorganization and outmigration | Language identities of departing groups require separate arguments |
| Pueblo IV | c. 1300–1600 CE | Larger aggregated settlements and new regional configurations | Mixture, maintenance, and shift are competing possibilities |
| Colonial and later histories | Spanish incursions from the sixteenth century onward | Missions, violence, resistance, new goods, and continuing Pueblo agency | Colonial naming and loans must not be backdated into precontact strata |

- Chaco's major construction and regional prominence preceded the final Mesa Verde departures; do not compress both into one simultaneous “collapse” (NPS, “History & Culture”).
- Cliff dwellings are a particular late settlement form, not the timeless home of every ancestral Pueblo community (NPS 2018).
- **D staging rule:** track settlement, language contact, ceremonial institutions, and clothing technology on separate timelines, then specify their actual links.

## 4. Kiva vocabulary: engineering description before cosmological reading

“Kiva” and “sipapu” are Hopi-derived terms in the NPS public account; their archaeological use does not identify the excavated residents as Hopi speakers. Most other terms below are English/European analytical vocabulary, not a recovered Keresan or Zuni lexicon.

| Term | Architectural referent | Coupling / calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Kiva | Chamber classified through context and features; often subterranean and circular at Mesa Verde | Domestic, social, and ceremonial activities need not form exclusive categories; modern kivas are sacred spaces |
| Banquette / bench | Ledge around the interior wall | Record its dimensions and construction before assuming seating or a social ranking |
| Pilaster | Projecting masonry support associated with roof beams | Some Mesa Verde roofs used pilasters; others used posts; do not universalize one count |
| Cribbed roof | Successive layers of crossing/offset timbers spanning the chamber | Generates construction, maintenance, timber-procurement, and skilled-labor vocabulary |
| Ventilator shaft and tunnel | Air inlet connecting exterior and chamber | Describe the air path separately from the entrance and smoke opening |
| Deflector | Barrier between ventilator outlet and hearth | Airflow management; its presence is not independently a theological statement |
| Hearth / firepit; ashpit | Fire installation and a distinct ash-related feature where present | Keep observed combustion features separate from their possible ritual uses |
| Sipapu | Small floor opening identified by that archaeological label | Public Hopi interpretation relates it to emergence; a particular ancient use is an inference, not an excavated spoken explanation |
| Roof hatch and ladder | Access arrangement reconstructed or preserved at particular sites | Vertical movement can acquire symbolic force without every ladder reenacting the same myth |
| Southern recess / wall niche | Recessed architectural features distinguished in Mesa Verde descriptions | Orientation, form, and use must be recorded locally; not universal altars |
| Great kiva | Larger communal architectural category, prominent at Chaco and elsewhere | Not simply the room of a bigger household; authority and participation remain interpretation problems |

- Form and sequence: Lancaster and Pinkley (1954), especially Site 16 plans and kiva descriptions; multiple-use and sacred-place calibration: NPS (2025).
- Lancaster and Pinkley's architectural observations remain useful; their “primitive → climax” vocabulary and confident ceremonial identifications are historical interpretation, not neutral facts.
- **D application:** inventory air, smoke, roof load, access, light, repair, and gathering scale; only then assign invented ceremonial meanings and local-language terms.

## 5. Emergence cosmology and the limits of a “pantheon” chart

| Attributed record | Publicly documented core | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Hopi explanation summarized by NPS (2025) | Sipapu as a place-of-emergence reference; movement from a previous world into this one | The name or exact meaning of every ancestral floor hole; a Keresan or Zuni ritual vocabulary |
| Cushing's Zuni narrative (1896), p. 379 onward | Awonawilona; Earth-mother and Sky-father; origins and movement through successive worlds | A verbatim medieval text or a consensus statement from all Zuni narrators |
| Cushing's Zuni synthesis, pp. 373–374 | The “middle,” ordered relationships, and links among creation, migration, and ceremonial organization | His historical explanations or English theological categories being accepted community doctrine |
| Keresan communities | Community-specific traditions require their own authorized sources; mid-20th-century ethnography (White 1943) records Acoma officers, katsina names, and a naming section, but it is outsider documentation, not community-authorized teaching | This entry does not build a Keresan divine-name catalogue from that literature; use community-authorized, purpose-specific sources |

- Keep **emergence** distinct from migration between historically occupied settlements: they can be related within a tradition without being the same sort of event or date.
- “Pantheon” is an outsider's organizing convenience. Do not force ancestors, rain-related beings, places, and ceremonial relationships into a Greek-style list of gods with exclusive job portfolios.
- Cushing himself notes that his English terms for sacred and religious roles are approximations (1896:374); retain that translation problem instead of treating his labels as transparent equivalences.
- NPS's comparison with Noah's Ark is visitor interpretation, not evidence that Hopi emergence is derived from a biblical flood or is the same narrative type.
- **D application:** couple an invented origin account to practiced spatial relations—inside/outside, below/above, departure/arrival—without borrowing the actual sipapu, divine names, or ceremonial scripts.
- Make disagreement possible: a household story, an institutional account, and a public explanation can differ without one being a degraded version of the others. This is a design option, not a claim about a specific Pueblo.

## 6. Symbolic systems: relationships, not a universal motif dictionary

| Evidence domain | Useful analytic question | Unsafe shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Direction and center | Which attributed account orders places, persons, colors, or actions together? | One Pueblo's direction–color correspondences are universal across the Southwest |
| Pottery geometry | What recurs by vessel type, production context, place, and period? | A stepped form always means rain, or every spiral records an emergence route |
| Architectural orientation | Is an alignment measured, repeated, and chronologically secure? | Any approximately cardinal building proves one fixed cosmology |
| Turquoise, shell, feathers | What are their provenances, routes, uses, and contexts? | Imported prestige material automatically identifies priests, ethnicity, or language |
| Murals and rock imagery | Who can interpret this image, for which setting and audience? | A modern outsider can decode a whole prehistoric belief system by visual resemblance |

- Cushing (1896) documents an elaborate Zuni arrangement; the historical existence of his account is secure, but its portability across communities or backward across centuries is not.
- NPS's Chaco account supports planned monumental architecture, long-distance exchange, and some astronomical orientation; exact functions and political organization remain debated.
- **D design worksheet:** `motif → medium → maker → permitted audience → occasion → local interpretation → later reinterpretation`.
- Change the medium and audience before deciding whether meaning travels: a bowl, wall painting, personal ornament, and restricted ceremonial object need not share one reading.

## 7. Drought-era migration linguistics: what each evidence stream can bear

The Mesa Verde region depopulated during the thirteenth century; drought, resource pressures, social changes, kinship connections, and opportunities elsewhere belong in the explanation. A monocausal “the rain stopped, therefore the people vanished” account is inadequate (NPS 2018; NPS, “People”).

| Evidence / method | Supports | Does not independently support | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tree-ring dating and moisture reconstruction | Construction sequences and regional environmental stress | The language of a timber's users; one motive shared by every migrant | O, with sampling and reuse limits |
| Settlement and ceramic sequences | Changes in residence, interaction, and learned practices | “This pottery equals Keresan speech”; a migration route uniquely fixed by a decorative style | O → H |
| Comparative Keresan phonology | Internal linguistic reconstruction through correspondences | An absolute split date coinciding with a particular drought | Linguistic analysis; no such date inferred here |
| Shared or borrowed vocabulary | Contact after inheritance and chance are tested; sometimes relative sequencing | One borrowing event in a specific drought year without independent anchors | H; individual etymologies need verification |
| Oral histories and place relationships | Attributed accounts of origins, journeys, obligations, and affiliations | An outsider's right to publish restricted detail or force every account into one itinerary | E; authority and genre matter |
| Historical anthropology across several records | A testable, convergent population-history proposal | Treating correlated evidence as wholly independent, or biology as a proxy for language | H |

- Ortman (2012) argues for relationships between Mesa Verde migration and Tewa formation using several evidence streams. Cite it as a specific argued model, not proof that all northern migrants were Tewa.
- Do not infer a Keresan or Zuni migration route by substituting those labels into Ortman's title or using present-day language geography as the missing premise.
- **Zuni isolate ≠ population isolate:** the linguistic classification neither dates arrival nor rules out incorporating people who previously spoke other languages.
- **Keresan internal diversity ≠ a drought clock:** linguistic divergence may predate, accompany, or postdate a settlement shift; matching labels on two timelines is not a demonstrated causal link.
- **D alternatives to test:** migrants maintain their language; switch to hosts' language; form a multilingual neighborhood; or share ceremonial terminology while ordinary speech remains different.
- For each fictional alternative, state who marries whom, where children learn speech, which institutions persist, and which vocabulary domains move. No outcome follows from aridity alone.
- Output a claim ledger: `proposed route/contact → dated material anchor → linguistic evidence → attributed history → rival explanation → unresolved gap`; leave a missing bridge visible.

## 8. Costume and material signature: assemble a dated production kit

| Material / object | Grounded reference | Coupling opportunity and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Cotton and woven cloth | Cotton moved into Mesa Verde through exchange networks (NPS 2018) | Fiber supply and cloth terminology may travel; cotton at a site does not prove local cultivation or the producer's language |
| Feather-cloth robes | NPS's winter reconstruction includes feather-cloth, hides, and warm footwear | Model insulation and seasonality; do not convert a reconstruction into one universal uniform |
| Plant-fiber sandals, cordage, basketry | NPS's artifact gallery identifies yucca-fiber sandals at Mesa Verde and relates yucca fiber to rope, mats, and baskets (NPS 2025); Kent (1983) is the specialist textile reference | Fiber and object class are publicly documented; a particular weave or site-date still needs the specialist literature before it is treated as diagnostic |
| Hide and bone-working tools | Hides, bone awls, and hide scrapers appear in the NPS material | Vocabulary can distinguish material, technique, repair, and finished garment rather than just costume silhouette |
| Turquoise and shell ornaments; imported feathers | Long-distance networks are documented at Mesa Verde and Chaco | Model access, exchange, and repair; symbolic interpretation requires an attributed source |
| Masonry, plaster, timber, and pottery | Regionally and chronologically differentiated built/material environments | Clothing must work with actual rooms, ladders, cold seasons, carrying, and field labor |

- NPS (2018) and the current “People” page support this public baseline; Kent (1983) is a reference for deeper textile work, not a claim that every technique was checked here.
- Do not back-project sheep-wool blankets, horses, Spanish garments, or later commercial silver-jewelry styles into a precontact setting merely because they are associated with the modern Southwest.
- Living ceremonial regalia is not a kit of detachable fantasy accessories. Record only public descriptive scope; do not extract restricted designs, mask construction, or performance instructions.
- **D production chain:** climate and fiber access → processing and labor → cut/wrap/weave possibilities → work versus ceremonial use → repair and inheritance vocabulary.
- Avoid assigning all weaving or pottery work to one sex across all periods without site- and community-specific evidence; the old brochure itself marks some such roles as conjectural.

## 9. Naming practices: separate address, personal identity, and map labels

| Name layer | Evidence / calibration | Fictional method |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday address | Kroeber (1917:70, 72–73) describes Zuni teknonymy: identifying an adult through a younger relative | Separate a person's identity from the kin-based expression used to address or mention them |
| Personal names and discretion | Kroeber interprets avoidance of personal names in relation to their intimacy and sacred significance | Design audience-specific naming rules; do not harvest names from his genealogies |
| Colonial/public labels | “Pueblo” and “Mesa Verde” are Spanish labels, not proof of precontact self-designation (NPS 2018) | Maintain distinct local, foreign, administrative, and archaeological names |
| Historic Keresan versus Zuni practices | Documented separately: Zuni teknonymy and name avoidance (Kroeber 1917); Keresan naming at Acoma recorded by White (1943, “Naming,” p. 319, located via its contents list) | No shared template; village-specific naming, permission, and name-changing rules need each community's own sources |

- Kroeber's observations belong to his fieldwork period and interpretive apparatus; they do not establish uniform contemporary usage or medieval Mesa Verde etiquette.
- A place-name's survival can reflect memory, translation, reuse, or outsiders' recording. It is not by itself proof of continuous residence by one language community.
- **D naming worksheet:** `referent → language/stage → ordinary address → intimate/restricted form → exonym → recorder's spelling → permitted audience`.
- Derive invented names through an independently designed phonology and sound-change history; do not concatenate Keresan, Zuni, and Hopi words into a supposedly authentic ancestral name.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

- **Coupled diversity:** unrelated languages can share architectural techniques and translated ceremonial concepts without becoming one civilization-language.
- **Architecture with memory:** a built form can have engineering, household, social, and cosmological lives; specify who interprets which layer.
- **Migration without disappearance:** relocation can preserve affiliations while reorganizing neighborhoods, institutions, vocabulary, and clothing supply.
- **Separate clocks:** environmental events, language differentiation, ceramic change, and mythic time do not automatically align.
- **Relational naming:** kin-based address and audience restrictions offer more depth than a list of exotic-sounding personal names.
- **Material causality without determinism:** cold seasons and trade constrain dress; they do not dictate a pantheon, grammar, or political constitution.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: reference citations, alternative mechanism tables, confidence labels, and unresolved questions. All fictional proposals remain D; no world-bible or canon claim is made here.

## 11. Provenance cautions and remaining thin ground

- **Chronicle scarcity:** the source base here lacks contemporaneous, readable precontact narratives assigning languages and named ceremonial systems to the excavated settlements. This is not an absence of Indigenous history or historical knowledge.
- **Colonial-source bias:** Cushing and Kroeber wrote through unequal research relationships and outsider categories. Cushing's universal cultural-evolution claims and “Bible” analogy are not adopted as explanations of Pueblo thought.
- **Archaeological bias:** Lancaster and Pinkley's excavation observations must be separated from their progress narrative; public NPS summaries simplify debate and sometimes rely on reconstructed scenes or descendant analogy.
- **Thin coverage:** detailed Keresan cosmologies, community-specific kiva terminology, current naming protocols, and specific prehistoric language distributions are not covered by the source base cited here; no gap is filled with a neighboring Pueblo's practice.
- **Verification boundary:** relevant passages in Cushing, Kroeber, Lancaster and Pinkley, NPS public materials, White 1943, and the 2018 Senate hearing were inspected; bibliographic/publisher checks are not claims of having read the entire Campbell, Cordell–McBrinn, Ortman, or Kent volumes.
- Hill's article was inspected in the open issue PDF (Hill 2008:3): it argues very long-term differentiation and possible social boundaries, not a migration itinerary; do not attribute a specific migration argument to it.
- Publication, digitization, museum display, or public-domain status does not itself establish permission to repurpose sacred knowledge. Community authority and the intended use require their own consideration.
- Reject the discredited “pots = peoples = languages” shortcut, fixed-rate vocabulary dating as a precise migration clock, and the popular lost-race story; retain explicit uncertainty instead of replacing it with invented certainty.
