## Scope and calibration

- **Lakota is not a synonym for Plains.** “Plains” is a regional/comparative category containing distinct languages, nations, histories, and ceremonial authorities. Use Lakota evidence first; label every comparison. [D01]
- Lakota, Dakota, and the related Assiniboine and Stoney traditions must not be collapsed into one interchangeable “Sioux culture.” Political affiliation, language classification, and a collector’s ethnic label are different fields. [RT96; D01]
- Select a community, locality, speaker/artist, and historical interval before making a cultural claim. “Oglala account recorded by Walker” is more defensible than “the Plains believe.”
- **Established:** linguistic contrasts, the existence of multiple orthographies, event-pictograph history keeping, and attributed ledger manuscripts. **Situated:** particular ritual explanations, symbolic correspondences, and clothing entitlements.
- **Source-limited:** the earliest histories represented in later copies, detailed naming protocols, and current ceremonial access rules. This entry is thin on community-specific contemporary practice; those details remain `(to-verify)`.
- Living communities are not survivals of a finished prehistoric stage. Language teaching, contemporary art, ordinary clothing, religious diversity, and historical reclamation belong in the same model as nineteenth-century evidence. [WH99; NMAI; PILA]

## Language family, linguistic texture, and writing

| System | Evidence-grounded distinction | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Lakota belongs to the Dakotan grouping within the Siouan family; Dakota is closely related. [RT96] | Model related varieties through regular sound correspondences and shared grammar, not interchangeable vocabulary lists. |
| Regional comparison | Plains cultures include non-Siouan languages; Cheyenne and Blackfoot are Algonquian. [D01] | Shared dress materials or ceremonial comparisons do not establish linguistic ancestry. |
| Sound | Oral/nasal vowels, stress, and plain/aspirated/ejective stop contrasts matter. [RT96] | Preserve distinctions when designing names; diacritics are not optional “exotic” ornament. |
| Grammar | Predominantly subject–object–verb order, postpositions, and substantial person marking on verbs. [RT96] | Translate complete constructions; English noun strings do not automatically become grammatical Lakota-style names. |
| Orthography | Roman-letter systems differ; White Hat’s Sicangu teaching tradition is one documented system. [WH99] | Store source spelling and normalized search form separately; record who authorizes a proposed standard. |
| Graphic history | Winter-count pictographs cue event names and fuller historical accounts. [GT07; NMAI] | Do not classify them as a defective attempt at alphabetic transcription. |

- Lakota/Lakhota/Lakȟóta spellings can reflect different transcription conventions. Retain the form used by a cited source rather than silently blending conventions.
- The familiar l/d/n correspondence is not sufficient to build a politically or linguistically accurate three-box chart of all “Sioux” peoples. Assiniboine/Stoney require their own treatment. [RT96; D01]
- NMAI’s poster calls the Yanktonai community “Nakota” and presents a simple three-dialect chart. Treat that as the poster’s historical terminology, not the controlling classification: the Yankton and Yanktonai peoples use *dakhota* as their self-designation, and “Nakota” is no longer in scholarly use for them [AHD22]. Dialect-mapping details still belong with a dedicated linguistic source.
- Contrast **alphabetic spelling**, **name/event pictography**, and **oral performance**. These can coexist without performing the same task.
- For fictional application, assign each medium its own affordance: pronunciation-preserving script, portable event cue, authoritative recital, or administrative identifier. This is a design inference, not a claim that Lakota media fit exclusive boxes.

## Wakȟáŋ: semantic field and sacred register

- *Wakȟáŋ* is commonly rendered “sacred,” “mysterious,” or “holy”; no single English gloss exhausts its contextual use. Do not turn it into a measurable magical fuel or a synonym for “wizard.” [P86; B53]
- *Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka* has been translated as “Great Spirit” and “Great Mystery.” Those renderings carry interpretive histories; neither licenses a simple equation with a European creator-god. [P86; W17; B53]
- Sacred discourse must be investigated through **speaker, addressee, genre, occasion, authority, and interpretation**, not just a list of special words. Powers supplies a linguistic-anthropological entry point; White Hat prevents grammar from being detached from cultural teaching. [P86; WH99]
- Do not infer one secret, fully separate language spoken by all ritual specialists. The distribution of particular archaic forms, substitutions, song vocables, and restricted expressions requires a specific attestation `(to-verify)`.

| Register question | What to record | Fictional-system use, not ethnographic assertion |
|---|---|---|
| Who may speak? | Named role, community, source, and limits on public discussion | Let authority depend on relationships or entrusted responsibilities, not vocabulary possession. |
| To whom? | Addressee and any kin/address term in its actual context | Build relational address without importing sacred Lakota names or prayers. |
| Which genre? | Conversation, narration, public oratory, prayer, song, translation | Make the same lexical item behave differently across genres. |
| What does translation conceal? | Literal rendering, explanatory gloss, translator’s religious assumptions | Preserve an unresolved semantic range rather than imposing “god/spell/priest.” |
| What may circulate? | Public explanation versus restricted text or performance | Model access constraints; do not publish a ritual phrasebook. |

- **Coupling test:** a ceremonial garment or object does not authorize its wearer to speak every sacred genre. Keep material possession, office, knowledge, and permission as separate variables.

## Mythic core without a borrowed Greek pantheon

| Publicly documented node | Bounded reading | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Wakȟáŋ Tȟáŋka | A central sacred concept whose analysis and translation vary by account. [P86; B53] | A universally agreed single deity with a fixed character sheet. |
| White Buffalo Calf Woman | In Black Elk/Brown’s published account, the coming of the sacred pipe grounds obligations and ritual relationships. [B53] | Reducing a sacred narrative to an interchangeable “goddess of buffalo.” |
| Sun / sunward orientation | The Sun Dance links embodied prayer, sacred orientation, and community responsibilities in the cited Oglala accounts. [W17; B53] | Assuming every Sun Dance is worship of an identical solar god. |
| Bison | Material dependence and sacred narrative significance can intersect; the pipe narrative is one situated example. [B53; D01] | Explaining every bison image as the same religious symbol. |
| Visionary representations | Black Hawk’s ledger includes catalogued “Vision” images alongside social, animal, and ceremonial scenes. [PILA] | Treating one artist’s vision as a universal illustrated catechism. |
| Iktomi | Walker’s account associates Iktomi with pranks and disruptive consequences; retain this narrative setting. [W17] | A generic “chaos god,” or a key that makes all Indigenous trickster figures equivalent. |
| Wakinyan | Walker’s “Winged God” terminology links this being with visions and differentiated social roles; see his pp. 68–69. [W17] | Equating the being with an ordinary bird or a European thunder deity. |

- Walker’s ordered accounts and Brown’s presentation of seven rites are source-specific articulations, not proof of an untouched, universally standardized pre-contact theological system.
- Walker’s capitalized “God,” “Shaman,” and ranked divine categories are his explanatory apparatus. Separate the attributed Lakota account from the English taxonomy imposed on it.
- A fuller comparison of creation narratives and variant relationships among named beings still needs narrator-specific texts; the small sample above is not a complete pantheon `(to-verify)`.
- **Craft extraction:** derive relationships among origin narrative, entrusted object, obligations, and authorized speech. Do not copy sacred beings into a fantasy pantheon and claim cultural equivalence.

## Sun Dance: public structural model, not performance instructions

- Topic spelling: **Wi-wanyag wačípi**. Nineteenth-century sources record *wiwanyag wacipi* [D94]; transcriptions vary across publications, and a normalized form and pronunciation must still be checked against the selected community’s orthography `(to-verify: community orthography)`. Do not present this mixed-source form as a linguistic standard.
- Public descriptions commonly gloss the name along the lines of “sun-gazing dance.” An exact morpheme-by-morpheme analysis is not established here `(to-verify: morpheme analysis)`.
- W17 and B53 concern Oglala accounts. Neither defines what all Plains nations, all Lakota communities, or present-day ceremonial leaders must do.
- The table below is an analytical grouping of public descriptions, not an indigenous checklist, schedule, or order of operations. [W17; B53]

| Structural relationship | Public descriptive level | Language–symbol–material coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Commitment and support | An undertaking involves dancers and a supporting community. | A vow/prayer is embedded in obligations; food, gathering, and support are part of the social setting. |
| Preparation and authority | Ceremonial leadership and preparation distinguish this from ordinary dancing. | Competent speech and entrusted responsibilities cannot be inferred from costume alone. |
| Ceremonial space | Published Oglala descriptions feature a central tree/pole and organized dancing space. | Material center and spatial orientation participate in meaning; not just stage decoration. |
| Embodied offering | Dancing, fasting, prayer, and endurance appear in public accounts. | Bodies and time are meaningful media, not merely visual spectacle. |
| Communal significance | Accounts explain the undertaking in relation to others, not solely personal achievement. | Do not recast it as an individual warrior’s automatic rank-up. |

- Piercing is described in some published traditions; it is neither the definition of the whole ceremony nor an element to generalize to every Plains people. This entry provides no techniques or reenactment directions. [D01; W17]
- Specific numbers of days, songs, offerings, directional colors, garment prescriptions, and eligibility rules are **not inferred** from the table; community and era details remain `(to-verify)`.
- Ethnographic publication is not permission to reproduce a ceremony. Present-day access and recording expectations must come from the relevant ceremonial authorities `(to-verify)`.
- **Fictional application:** use the abstract architecture of commitment → authorized preparation → bounded space → embodied responsibility → communal consequence; invent independent language, imagery, and institutions rather than reskinning the Sun Dance.

## Symbolic system: relationship before universal code

| Visible element | Evidentiary question | Safe inference boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Pipe | Which narrator, context, keeper, and associated obligation? [B53] | A sacred relationship in the cited account, not a universal “peace prop.” |
| Circle, center, direction | Which ceremonial explanation or spatial plan? [W17; B53] | Geometry may organize relationships; a circle alone does not identify a doctrine. |
| Feather, headdress, shield | Which wearer, object, represented occasion, and documented entitlement? [D01; PILA] | Recognition and responsibility need case evidence; no invented “one feather per kill” decoder. |
| Bison, horse, bird | Subsistence scene, social distinction, personal narrative, or ceremonial context? [PILA] | Animal identity alone cannot distinguish literal description from sacred reference. |
| Color | Pigment/material, artist’s convention, object decoration, or explicit interpretation? [PILA] | No universal red/yellow/black/white “Native meaning” chart. |
| Pictograph | Name cue, annual event cue, action, or curatorial identification? [GT07; PILA] | Similar-looking marks need not carry the same function. |

- Four-direction/color correspondences circulating in popular “medicine wheel” diagrams are not a portable key to every Lakota or Plains object. Any specific mapping adopted from them is `(to-verify)` against a named community/source.
- Distinguish **depicted**, **identified by a curator**, **explained by a named knowledge holder**, and **inferred by the researcher**. Never silently promote one level into another.

## Era model: local change, not a ladder of civilization

| Research stage | Questions to stage together | Evidence and caution |
|---|---|---|
| Before widely established equestrian lifeways | Mobility, food acquisition, household materials, neighboring languages | Use D01’s regional histories; do not project nineteenth-century mounted dress backward. Adoption chronologies are under active revision and vary locally [T23, summary checked 2026-10-17]; treat any single date as provisional. |
| Equestrian and expanding trade networks | Horse access, bison use, trade cloth, metal goods, ornament materials | Model overlapping old/new materials and unequal access, not a single replacement year. [D01] |
| Nineteenth-century warfare and colonial constraint | Coercion, disrupted movement, material scarcity, new drawing media | PILA places paper-ledger drawing within these pressures; medium change is not disappearance of Native agency. [PILA] |
| Reservation-era representation and adaptation | Remembered events, courtship, ceremony, schooling, public presentation | A drawing’s production date may postdate the dress or event represented. [PILA; NMAI] |
| Twentieth century to present | Language teaching, history recovery, contemporary artists, ordinary and ceremonial dress | Include self-directed continuity and change; do not end the culture’s timeline at confinement. [WH99; NMAI; PILA] |

- Keep **event date**, **image-production date**, **copy date**, **collection date**, and **catalog attribution date** in separate fields. This is the core antidote to anachronistic costume reconstruction.
- Treat transitions as asynchronous by community, household, and access route. “Traditional” can name an inherited practice using traded materials; it is not a reliable manufacturing date.
- Never translate colonial disruption into a neutral technological “upgrade.” Changes in language use, textile supply, ceremony, and archival survival may share a coercive cause. [NMAI; PILA]

## Costume and material signature: ledger evidence in use

- Candidate material vocabulary includes hide garments and robes, leggings, moccasins, cloth garments and wraps, worked ornament, and horse equipment. Each reconstruction still needs a dated Lakota object or image, not a generic Plains silhouette. [D01; PILA]
- Separate **garment construction**, **decorative technique**, **material identification**, **occasion**, and **wearer entitlement**. A colored drawing can support outline or placement without proving fiber, bead composition, or stitch technique.
- Walker specifically distinguishes the insignia a candidate is entitled to wear from other provisions, pp. 92–93. This supports asking about entitlement in that account; it is not a universal Plains clothing law. [W17]
- Quillwork and beadwork are not interchangeable material diagnoses; a drawn dotted or geometric surface cannot by itself establish which technique was used. Specific object identifications remain `(to-verify)`.
- Reserve “regalia” for contexts in which it is appropriate; do not rename all historic clothing “costume,” or imply that contemporary ceremonial dress is theatrical disguise.

### Black Hawk: a bounded comparison set

- PILA’s [ledger listing](https://plainsledgerart.org/ledgers/index/list) records that William Edward Caton collected the Black Hawk drawings and that they were bound in **1881**. That binding date is not proof that every represented event occurred in 1881. [PILA]
- The catalog distinguishes manuscript **page** from digital **plate**; record both. This prevents an image citation from becoming unfindable after a viewer redesign.
- Useful catalogued comparisons: page 3, plate 5, [“Sun Dance audience 1”](https://plainsledgerart.org/plates/view/172); page 4, plate 6, [“Sun Dance audience 2”](https://plainsledgerart.org/plates/view/173); page 6, plate 8, [“Lakota headmen - 1”](https://plainsledgerart.org/plates/view/175); page 9, plate 11, [“3 woman from behind | Courting couple”](https://plainsledgerart.org/plates/view/178). [PILA]
- Those are catalog titles, not independent confirmation of every figure’s identity or office. They identify a research set, not a ready-made list of garment meanings.
- The page 9 viewer currently states “No Ethnographic Notes For This Plate.” Do not invent an explanatory tradition, textile identification, or dress entitlement to fill that archival gap. [PILA; checked 2026-10-17]

| Research operation | Record | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|
| Compare audience and headmen pages | Silhouette, wrapping, headgear, carried objects, figure grouping | A uniform rank system from one contrast. |
| Compare front/profile and rear views | Which garment parts remain visible in each view | A complete tailoring pattern from a flat drawing. |
| Compare social and ceremonial scenes | Repeated and context-specific forms | That every depicted accessory was worn daily. |
| Compare within an attributed manuscript | Artist’s repeated graphic conventions | That repeated pigment equals repeated textile material. |
| Triangulate with dated objects or photographs | Agreement, disagreement, custody, attribution | That a posed photograph is automatically more truthful than a drawing. |

- **Ledger art is not merely an illustrated settler account.** It continues Native representational practices while using acquired paper and drawing tools; subjects include warfare, courtship, animals, and ceremony. [PILA]
- Conversely, no drawing is a transparent photograph. Selection, recollection, patronage, coercion, intended audience, and later captions can shape what is visible.
- Whole-book order matters. PILA documents how sales of detached sheets can destroy narrative associations; an isolated market image may lose precisely the context needed to interpret clothing. [PILA]
- Fictional application: let one inherited emblem persist while garment material changes, but document the proposed continuity as a design hypothesis—not a Lakota historical fact.

## Winter counts: event names, keepers, and era evidence

- A winter count links an annual event pictograph with a remembered name/explanation and a keeper’s historical knowledge. The image does not contain the entire account by itself. [GT07; NMAI]
- NMAI describes community discussion of a salient event and keeper responsibility for recording and explaining it. Do not extrapolate one universally identical selection procedure to every count.
- First-snowfall-to-first-snowfall is the annual framing given in the NMAI explanation; conversion into Gregorian year pairs must be checked for the particular count. [NMAI]
- Hide, cloth, and paper versions can carry continuing histories. A copied or renewed support is not automatically a new historical tradition. [GT07; NMAI]

| Example in the Lone Dog teaching resource | What it can anchor | What it cannot establish |
|---|---|---|
| Whooping cough, 1813–14 | A community’s remembered epidemic year | Disease incidence, diagnoses, or mortality totals by itself. |
| La Framboise’s trading store, 1817–18 | A particular remembered encounter with trade infrastructure | The first trade contact of every Lakota band. |
| Missouri River flood, 1825–26 | An environmental event and remembered loss | Complete regional flood extent without other evidence. |
| November 1833 meteor storm | A cross-comparable astronomical anchor; GT07 discusses 1833–34 correlations | That other entries are automatically free of missing years, copying differences, or dating disagreements. |

- These examples come from the **Lone Dog/Yanktonai** resource; do not silently relabel them Oglala events. The poster’s “Nakota” designation needs the linguistic caution above. [NMAI]
- Keep two layers: **year name** as a locally selected mnemonic label; **historical narration** as the fuller explanation. Neither equals an exhaustive annual report.
- Compare counts through event identities, accompanying explanations, sequence, and keeper/copy relationships. Similar pictures may reflect shared history or copied exemplars; they are not automatically independent corroboration. [GT07]
- Chronicle evidence is uneven: histories may survive as later copies and collector translations; early represented dates do not demonstrate annual contemporaneous inscription. Specific early sequences require count-by-count assessment `(to-verify)`.
- Absence of a garment, conflict, ritual, or population in the selected annual image is not proof of historical absence. Event selection sharply limits negative inference.
- **Era-design lesson:** a culture can name time through remembered experiences while outsiders impose numbered administrative years. Preserve both without equating difference with inaccuracy.

## Naming practices and identity matching

- Separate a person’s language-form name, translated English name, administrative spelling, and source-specific nickname. Do not merge individuals solely because translated strings match. [RT96; WH99; D01]
- “Black Hawk,” “Black Elk,” and “Lone Dog” are English forms in the sources cited here; they do not establish an “adjective + animal” grammar for every Lakota name.
- Name construction must respect the language’s morphology and phonological contrasts. Do not generate English compounds and pronounce them through an undifferentiated “Native” sound palette. [RT96]
- Who can confer a name, whether it is inherited or changed, and what may be publicly explained are family/community/era questions; no universal lifecycle protocol is verified here `(to-verify)`.
- Keep community or band identification separate from personal name and language variety. Collector labels and later tribal affiliations can refer to different historical moments. [D01]
- Personal naming and the naming of a winter are distinct institutions. The demonstrated parallel is that words can cue fuller narratives—not that both use the same authority or rules. [GT07; NMAI]
- Fictional naming worksheet: `form | grammatical analysis | literal gloss | social explanation | conferring authority | public/private status | spelling source | aliases | date/context`.
- Leave unsupported fields unknown. A method that makes uncertainty visible is more useful than an invented authentic-sounding etymology.

## What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Relational semantics:** design a sacred semantic field whose translation changes with speaker, genre, and relationship; do not borrow *wakȟáŋ* as a magic-system resource.
2. **Layered records:** give pictographs, oral accounts, alphabetic writing, and administrative names different jobs without ranking their users as more or less civilized.
3. **Event-time:** construct proposed year names around community-selected experiences; include both keeper explanations and a separate calendar-conversion table.
4. **Material histories:** make trade, forced displacement, access, and household continuity affect language and clothing together; avoid a single “tribal outfit” across centuries.
5. **Role distinctions:** separate garment, office, entrusted object, knowledge, and speech permission. Their partial overlap can generate plausible institutions without copying ceremonies.
6. **Competing viewpoints:** let records disagree because of selection, memory, translation, or attribution—not because oral history is intrinsically unreliable.
7. **Handoff, not canon:** send lore-shokunin proposed constraints, alternatives, evidence, uncertainty, and sensitivity limits. The sibling decides what enters the world bible.

## Provenance cautions and stop rules

- Walker, missionary lexicons, collector captions, and institutional catalogs preserve evidence through colonial relationships. Critical use is not the same as discarding them; retain who said what, through whom, and when.
- Black Elk/Brown is a named, mediated account. Its publisher’s claims of singular priestly authority or a single “true” theological system are not neutral descriptions of all Lakota religious life.
- NMAI’s educational poster is valuable for keeper testimony and image-linked events but oversimplifies linguistic classification. A reputable institution can still carry an outdated chart.
- Bibliographic checks and public descriptions do not verify every page-level ritual, naming, or costume claim. Detailed prescriptions remain `(to-verify)` until a suitable text/object record and, where relevant, community authority support them.
- Do not combine Sun Dance, Ghost Dance, powwow, and unrelated Plains ceremonies into one undated ritual package. Any comparison needs its own provenance and era.
- Reject the “timeless warrior tribe,” universal medicine-wheel decoder, generic pan-Indigenous shaman, and salvaged “vanishing people” frame. They erase the distinctions this entry is designed to preserve.
- Publicly accessible images are not automatically unrestricted for reuse. PILA’s project overview explicitly separates educational viewing from authorization for other uses; check the specific image and custodian.
- Stop before reproducing prayers, songs, restricted designs, or ceremonial procedures. Describe the evidence boundary and seek appropriate guidance; a published source is not blanket permission.
