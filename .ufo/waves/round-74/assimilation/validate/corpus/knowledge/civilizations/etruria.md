## 1. Evidence model: a city network, not one timeless ethnic costume

- Scope: Etruria proper lies in central Italy; Etruscan-speaking communities and cultural networks also reached beyond it. Separate city, region, date, medium, and social setting before comparing an inscription with a garment or god ([B02]; [H00]).
- The surviving record is rich in objects and short inscriptions but thin in continuous indigenous historical narrative. Greek and Roman authors preserve indispensable information through their own political, literary, and religious agendas ([REL06]; [C23]).
- Working labels here: **attested** = readable inscription or observable object feature; **supported** = convergent scholarly interpretation; **contested** = material alternatives remain; **design** = a fictional transformation, not an Etruscan fact. `(to-verify)` marks an unchecked detail, not permission to use it as settled evidence.

| Evidence class | Strong use | Weak or forbidden inference |
|---|---|---|
| Epitaph, dedication, ownership inscription | Name slots, formulas, orthographic changes, specific relationships | Complete everyday grammar, universal literacy, or a whole kinship constitution |
| Painted chamber, tumulus assemblage, sarcophagus | Selected funerary display, represented dress, household memory | A census of ordinary wardrobes or proof of identical regional practice |
| Inscribed liver model, ritual calendar | Divine names, classification, repeated ritual language | An intact common theology or a mechanically recoverable prediction system |
| Greek/Latin account of Etruscan practice | Reception, claims of expertise, specific reported traditions | Unmediated Etruscan speech, neutral ethnography, or a native chronicle |

- Apply the contextual and object-biographical methods in `material-culture-inference-method.md`: a book, garment, and tomb each have production, use, reuse, and recovery histories. Their final archaeological association need not reproduce their original cultural association.

## 2. Language family, script, and diachrony

| System | Calibrated reference knowledge | Coupling lever |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy | Etruscan is non-Indo-European; relationship with Raetic and Lemnian under **Tyrsenian/Tyrrhenian** is well supported. Exact branching, separation dates, and migration scenarios are less secure ([R98]; [B02]). | A local language can share a cultural arena with unrelated neighbors without becoming genealogically related to them. |
| Script ancestry | An alphabet adapted from the Euboean Greek tradition, transmitted through contact in Italy; script descent is not language descent ([M22]). | Merchants, imported vessels, feasting, and elite display can carry writing before a centralized bureaucracy does. |
| Sign selection | Ordinary Etruscan spelling uses a/e/i/u, not Greek o; Greek voiced-stop letters were not retained as a matching b/d/g series. Regional practices differ ([M22]). | Redesign the borrowed alphabet for the receiving phonology; do not retain every source-language contrast. |
| Innovation | Early vh/hv for /f/ gives way to the figure-eight sign; c/k/q distributions and sibilant notation vary by region ([M22]). | Workshops and scribal schools can advertise region and era through the same personal name. |
| Direction | Right-to-left is characteristic; direction and punctuation must be checked on the particular inscription ([B02]). | Keep display typography distinct from an editor's left-to-right transliteration. |
| Morphology | Suffixing case morphology is visible; genitives include -s and -l series. Short formulaic texts are much better understood than many extended passages ([B02]). | Names, donor relations, and divine recipients can be legible when the ritual sentence remains uncertain. |
| Sound change | Later Etruscan exhibits syncope: Maras illustrates Menerva → Menrva and turuce → turce ([M22]). | Let divine names, old dedications, and living pronunciation preserve different historical layers. |

- **Not “an undeciphered alphabet”:** letters and numerous formulas can be read; much vocabulary and connected-text interpretation remain uncertain. Readability, grammatical analysis, and fluent translation are different achievements ([B02]; [T08]).
- Do not turn syncope into “delete random vowels.” Establish a fictional stress system and conditioned changes, then let conservative ritual spelling and names lag behind speech (**design**).
- Shared alphabet, shared loanword, similar deity, and shared ancestry are four different relationships. A Greek-looking name or myth does not make Etruscan Indo-European; Latinization is a historical process, not a language-family classification.

## 3. Mythic and pantheon core: local gods, translated identities

- Divine names are better attested than complete narratives. Inscribed mirrors, sanctuary dedications, ritual texts, and later literary testimony do not constitute one surviving Etruscan mythological scripture ([REL06]; [B02]).

| Name or figure | Evidence-calibrated core | Design boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Tinia; Uni; Menrva | Important gods commonly compared with Zeus/Jupiter, Hera/Juno, and Athena/Minerva; names and iconographies are attested ([REL06]). | Correspondence is not identity; do not project one uniform city-state triad or a Roman institution backward everywhere. |
| Nethuns | Water-associated deity prominent in the Liber Linteus; often compared with Neptune ([LL07]; [T08]). | Avoid making every invocation an ocean cult; inland water, place, and local ritual matter. |
| Turan; Fufluns | Conventionally associated with Aphrodite and Dionysos respectively, within a strongly interconnected Mediterranean visual culture ([REL06]). | Translation and selective adoption are more useful than a table of interchangeable “god slots.” |
| Charun; Vanth | Funerary supernatural figures; Charun's hammer and Vanth's winged/torch-bearing imagery belong to particular depictions ([REL06]). | Charun is not simply Greek Charon with spelling changed; attributes do not establish a universal afterlife itinerary. |
| Tages | Cicero reports a plough-revealed child with a seer's wisdom teaching an art committed to writing; Cicero then attacks the story ([C23], 2.50–51). | Treat revelation as an authorization narrative and its hostile retelling as another layer, not as witnessed history. |
| Vegoia | Prophetic authority associated in later Latin transmission with sacred order and boundaries ([REL06]). | Do not treat the transmitted Latin boundary teaching as a verbatim early Etruscan manuscript. |

- Myth–language coupling: preserve the difference between a local divine name, a borrowed narrative character, a foreign equivalence, and an epithet attached to a place. These can accumulate without producing one tidy pantheon chart.
- Myth–costume coupling: identify a figure by **attribute + inscription + scene + date** where possible; neither wings nor a pointed cap alone licenses a divine or priestly identification.

## 4. Disciplina etrusca: reading signs through authorized categories

- *Disciplina etrusca* is a Latin designation for Etruscan religious expertise. “Augury discipline” is too loose if it merges all divination into Roman augural office; entrails, lightning, prodigies, and auspices require distinctions ([REL06]; [LS79]).
- The conventional book-group labels are **libri haruspicini** (entrails), **libri fulgurales** (lightning), and **libri rituales** (ritual and civic ordering). This is a transmitted classification, not an intact three-volume native library available to reconstruct ([REL06]).

| Channel | Object of interpretation | Institutional and symbolic coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Haruspicy / extispicy | Sacrificial viscera; the liver is especially important | Animal selection, anatomy, trained expertise, consultation, and ritual response belong together. |
| Lightning interpretation | Lightning's occurrence and position within a classified space | Cosmography, orientation, divine jurisdictions, and civic action can share a spatial vocabulary. |
| Brontoscopy | Thunder, including calendrical interpretation | Date + atmospheric sign + predicted outcome; do not relabel it liver-reading or modern weather forecasting. |
| Prodigy interpretation | Anomalous events recognized as religiously significant | Authorities decide which events require consultation and remedy; not every unusual event is automatically a state portent. |
| Roman augury / auspices | Authorized observation and interpretation of signs, notably birds | A neighboring and interacting institutional system; an augur and a haruspex are not interchangeable job titles. |

- **Piacenza liver:** an inscribed bronze model of a sheep's liver, generally assigned to the late pre-imperial period; exact dating varies. Divine names occupy compartments, with **sixteen peripheral divisions** ([VM87]; [REL06]).
- A training/reference function is plausible, but its actual use is not directly documented. Do not describe it as a proven portable prediction calculator, a surgical chart, or a recovered full ritual script ([VM87]; [T08]).
- Its subdivisions can be compared with transmitted celestial divisions; reconstructing a single exact alignment between every liver compartment, heavenly region, season, and calendar invocation is **contested**, not automatic ([T08]).
- The useful symbolic principle is **jurisdictional mapping**: the position of a sign helps classify its divine relevance. Sixteen is not evidence of a zodiac, a universal numerological code, or an alphabetic cipher.
- Fictional procedure (**design**, not a reconstructed rite): recognized sign → identify channel/date/place → consult trained interpreter and precedent → issue an authorized reading → prescribe a response → preserve or dispute the record.

## 5. Haruspicy vocabulary: keep the language labels visible

| Term | Language and usable gloss | Calibration / source |
|---|---|---|
| haruspex; haruspices | Latin; practitioner, plural practitioners | Do not present the Latin title as an attested Etruscan self-designation ([LS79]; [REL06]). |
| haruspicina | Latin; the divinatory art or expertise | A practice/domain term, not another priestly rank ([LS79]). |
| exta; extispicium | Latin; sacrificial viscera; examination/divination through them | Broader than the liver alone; “hepatoscopy” is the narrower modern Greek-derived technical label ([LS79]). |
| iecur; caput iocineris | Latin; liver; its conventionally named “head” | Ancient anatomical classification, not a translation into modern clinical anatomy ([LS79]). |
| fulgur; prodigium | Latin; lightning; a religiously significant portent | Separate the observed event from the interpretive category and its civic recognition ([LS79]; [REL06]). |
| templum; lituus | Latin; ritually delimited space; the augur's curved staff | Neither means a universal Etruscan liver-reading room or standard equipment for every haruspex ([LS79]). |
| ais; aiser | Etruscan; god; gods | Useful divine vocabulary, not anatomical terminology ([B02]). |
| cepen | Etruscan; a priestly designation | Exact office structure and all compounds are not recovered; do not equate every occurrence with haruspex ([B02]). |
| fler; flerχva | Etruscan ritual words conventionally associated with offering/victim and a plural or collective formation | Nuance and contexts remain debated; not a secure universal word-for-word ritual translation ([LL07]; [T08]). |
| netśvis | Etruscan term matched to Latin *haruspex* in the Pesaro bilingual of Larth Cafates, 1st century BCE: Latin HARUSPEX FULGURIATOR beside Etruscan NETŚVIS TRVTNUT FRONTAC, cited as TLE 697 = ET Um 1.7 ([REL06]); the standard glossary also glosses netsvis “haruspex” ([B02]). | The equation rests on one late bilingual from outside Etruria proper, and the word's etymology remains disputed; do not build an invented “liver + seer” reading on it ([REL06]; [B02]). |

- Transliteration warning: θ/χ and th/ch conventions, and regional sibilant spellings, must not be mixed as if they were different ritual offices. Retain the edition's spelling when quoting; record any normalization ([B02]; [M22]).
- Build a fictional technical lexicon by slots: **organ part / spatial sector / anomaly / divine addressee / interpreter / verdict / remedy / record**. Keep imported administrative titles separate from native cult vocabulary; leave genuinely unknown historical slots unknown.

## 6. Era staging: synchronize spelling, institutions, and silhouette

- Dates below are approximate conventional archaeological/art-historical stages, not Etruscan self-named epochs; boundaries vary by city and scholarly scheme ([H00]; [D75]; [M22]).

| Stage | Language, contact, and religious evidence | Costume/material staging |
|---|---|---|
| Villanovan horizon, c. 900–720 BCE | Early Iron Age antecedents; do not invent a written native chronicle for this whole span | Cremation assemblages, impasto, metal fittings; “Villanovan” is a material-cultural label, not proof of a separate vanished language. |
| Orientalizing, c. 720–580 BCE | Alphabetic uptake, aristocratic exchange, imported prestige objects and motifs | Monumental tumuli, wealthy grave assemblages, elaborate metalwork and gold fittings; select a named city and deposit rather than an undated “Eastern” style. |
| Archaic, c. 580–480 BCE | Regional alphabets; extensive contact with Greek and Phoenician cultural networks | Painted terracotta, banquet imagery, distinctive headgear and footwear in selected representations; Greek-connected forms acquire local uses. |
| Classical, c. 480–300 BCE | Syncope reshapes written words; civic histories diverge rather than following one empire-wide clock | Tarquinian painted banquets and performance dress offer dated display evidence, not an everyday uniform. |
| Hellenistic / incorporation into Roman power, c. 300–1st century BCE | Etruscan inscriptions and ritual texts persist amid increasing Latin influence; conquest and language shift are not one simultaneous event | Funerary portrait traditions and changing civic display; the late linen book and liver model must not be placed automatically in an early tumulus assemblage. |

- Maintain a coupled era ledger: `city → date range → inscriptional spelling → political relationship → cult evidence → depicted dress → surviving material`. A revised date can require changing every downstream reference.
- Do not infer a precise date of language death from one late inscription, or a sudden end to local religious expertise from political incorporation.

## 7. Tumulus and costume evidence: three different records

- **Architecture first:** Cerveteri's Banditaccia contains tumuli and house-like rock-cut tombs; Tarquinia's Monterozzi is especially important for painted chambers. A tumulus is a mound form, not a garment style or a synonym for every Etruscan tomb ([WH]).

| Reference case | What it supports | What it does not support |
|---|---|---|
| Regolini-Galassi tomb at Caere/Cerveteri, conventionally c. 675–650 BCE | Elite funerary display through conspicuous gold ornaments, including a large fibula and pectoral ([D75]; [H00]) | A complete surviving sewn outfit, universal women's dress, or a law assigning each ornament a fixed rank |
| Sarcophagus of the Spouses, Rome version, c. 520 BCE | A carefully staged reclining couple; represented headwear, clothing, bodily presentation, and intimate banquet posture ([D75]; [OBJ]) | A literal portrait, a representative household survey, or proof of universal legal gender equality |
| Tomb of the Triclinium, Tarquinia, c. 470 BCE | Sumptuous robes, dancers, musicians, and a funerary banquet; painting and performance make status visible ([OBJ]; [H00]) | Original cloth surviving on those bodies; a direct match between painted pigment and textile dye chemistry |

- Source-to-costume ladder: **metal fitting** establishes an object; **placement and associations** suggest attachment/use; **depiction** supplies a represented silhouette; **reconstruction** proposes a cut. Do not quietly promote the final proposal into the first kind of evidence.
- Wool/linen choices, spinning and weaving, dye access, fasteners, and jewelry production belong to different production chains. Gold surviving beside a burial does not preserve the fiber, color, seam, or daily wear frequency of its lost clothing ([D75]; `textile-technology.md`).
- *Tebenna* is the conventional name associated with an Etruscan mantle; *tutulus* and *calcei repandi* are familiar labels used for characteristic headgear and upturned footwear. Label terminology and representational context; do not assume these are all recovered contemporary Etruscan garment names ([D75]).
- Etruscan women's visibility in names and banquet scenes differs from familiar Athenian elite conventions. This matters, but the inference “therefore matriarchy” or “therefore equal rights in every class” is unsupported ([B02]; [H00]).
- Painting uses conventions, including gendered skin tones; color cannot be read directly as ancestry, labor exposure, or a fabric palette. Missing pigment and modern restoration further constrain reconstruction ([OBJ]).
- No secure universal uniform for every Etruscan diviner is established here. Any prescribed cap, cloak, border color, or office badge for a fictional priesthood is **design**, not a recovered pan-Etruscan costume code.

## 8. Liber Linteus: book → cut textile → mummy wrapping → museum text

- **Attested object biography:** the longest surviving Etruscan text is written on linen that was cut into strips and reused as wrapping for a female mummy acquired in Egypt in the nineteenth century; mummy and linen are now associated with the Archaeological Museum in Zagreb ([LL07]; [T08]).
- Text, textile, and burial need separate dates. Van der Meer proposes writing around **200–150 BCE** from letter forms and a north-Etruscan context; textile dating and the later mummy's dating are separate questions. Do not turn this proposal into an exact production place or year ([T08], discussing [LL07], p. 23).
- The surviving layout comprises twelve inscribed columns, damaged and incomplete, with black writing and red divisions/rubrics. Calendar dates, repeated formulas, divine recipients, and offerings support interpretation as a ritual calendar, not an Egyptian funerary composition ([LL07]; [T08]).
- Citation discipline: quote **column.line** and an identified reading, not a seamless fabricated English “prayer.” Repetition helps reconstruction but does not make every intervening word understood.
- **Demonstrable disagreement:** at LL 8.3, Turfa contrasts Bonfante's “twenty-sixth” with van der Meer's “24th” and differing renderings of the ritual verbs. That is evidence of translation uncertainty, not two securely attested festival dates ([T08]).
- The mummy does **not** establish that its deceased owner was Etruscan, a priestess, the book's author, or a carrier of an Etruscan mission to Egypt. The route and circumstances of the linen's transfer remain uncertain ([T08]).
- Nor is the book securely identifiable as the complete *disciplina etrusca*, a “Book of the Dead,” a bilingual key, or a text whose linen material proves all cult books were garments.
- Coupling use (**design**): the same textile can be sacred information, portable institutional property, discarded cloth, burial material, and finally an archaeological puzzle. Decide who reads it at each stage, whether anyone can still read it, and which associations later observers falsely infer.

## 9. Naming practices and the social memory carried by inscriptions

| Layer | Attested pattern / limit | Fictional adaptation |
|---|---|---|
| Personal and family names | Personal name + gentilicial is common; additional relationship, age, and office information depends on date and text type ([B02]). | Define a slot grammar by context; do not impose one fixed multi-name formula across all eras and classes. |
| Gendered forms | Names such as Larth/Larthi and Vel/Velia illustrate related masculine/feminine forms, not a productive rule covering every name ([B02]). | Build a restricted name inventory and explicit derivations rather than feminizing arbitrary vocabulary. |
| Affiliation | Epitaphs can identify paternal and maternal connections; women retain identifiable personal/family names ([B02]). | Household and tomb membership can preserve several descent claims without requiring matriliny. |
| Relationship lexicon | clan “son,” seχ “daughter,” and puia “wife” are useful conventional glosses in inscriptional contexts ([B02]). | Relationship terms anchor reading; they do not themselves prove inheritance or residence rules. |
| Place-name transmission | Tarch(u)na / Latin Tarquinii / modern Tarquinia illustrate differently transmitted names ([OBJ]; [B02]). | Maintain separate endonym, exonym, and modern/editorial labels; do not treat them as interchangeable contemporary spellings. |
| Object voice | mi “I” appears in short object inscriptions, including ownership formulas ([B02]). | A vessel can carry a grammatical speaker-position without proving belief that every inscribed object is literally sentient. |

- Keep diplomatic transcription, normalized reading, proposed translation, language, date, city, and source identifier in separate fields. Do not manufacture a missing gentilicial, matronymic, or divine epithet to make a genealogy feel complete.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **A coupling engine, not a culture kit:** choose a city network whose language remains genealogically distinct while script, luxury goods, divine stories, and administrative vocabulary cross its borders.
2. **A sign bureaucracy:** specify who recognizes a portent, who has authority to interpret it, what records authorize the reading, and what material response follows. These are design questions, not claims that every Etruscan city answered them identically.
3. **One spatial grammar across scales:** let fictional divine jurisdictions inform observation spaces, ritual diagrams, and boundary language. Invent the mapping deliberately; do not claim an exact historical sky–liver–calendar concordance.
4. **Era-linked words and wardrobes:** derive one sound change, one scribal revision, one trade change, and one display change together; allow old liturgy, heirloom jewelry, and family names to survive the transition at different speeds.
5. **Ancestral display with a visibility filter:** design elite funerary clothing separately from labor wear, priestly equipment, and living civic dress. State which of those an excavator or traveler could actually observe.
6. **A recycled sacred medium:** use the linen book's documented change of function as a model for artifact biography, not as a template for copying a real deceased person's identity or inventing an Etruscan-Egyptian doctrine.
7. **A non-canon handoff to lore-shokunin:** provide linked options, dates, evidence class, source, confidence, and downstream naming/costume dependencies. Lore decides which fictional propositions become world-bible facts.

## 11. Provenance cautions and thin coverage

- **Thin here:** precise priestly office hierarchies, native garment terminology, regional everyday dress, and the chronology of individual ritual doctrines. Full excavation catalogs, newer inscription editions, and specialist textile work are needed before tightening those claims; the netśvis equation above rests on a single late bilingual (§5), which is a scope limit rather than an open flag.
- **Mediated chronicles:** Cicero's Tages passage both reports a tradition and ridicules it. Do not remove the polemical frame; equally, do not adopt Roman “superstition” rhetoric as an objective description of Etruscan intelligence or religion ([C23]).
- **Asymmetric survival:** elite tombs, durable fittings, named dedications, and literate cult practice are over-visible. Women, poorer households, migrants, and everyday speech cannot be reconstructed simply by extending elite funerary patterns to everyone.
- **Contested models:** remote language-family extensions, exact Tyrsenian migration routes, universal sky/liver alignments, seamless Liber Linteus translations, and “Etruscan matriarchy” are not settled knowledge. Shared motifs and script ancestry do not repair missing evidence.
- **Museum and restoration history:** nineteenth-century collection, excavation, dispersal, reassembly, and removal of wall paintings alter what can be compared. Treat photographs, restored monuments, and early copies as distinct records, not interchangeable originals ([WH]; [OBJ]).
- **Verification scope:** public references [M22], [T08], [C23], [OBJ], [WH], and [PES] were inspected for this entry; print works identify the scholarly reference shelf, not a claim that every page was checked. Recheck the specified edition and object record before reproducing an exact inscription, ritual instruction, garment pattern, or scholarly quotation.
