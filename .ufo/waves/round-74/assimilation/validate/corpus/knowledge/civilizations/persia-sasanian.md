## 1. Evidence layers: date the witness before borrowing its world

| Layer | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Dated inscriptions, coins, seals, administrative documents | Attested titles, spellings, public claims, offices, iconographic combinations | Everyone's beliefs, pronunciation, ordinary conversation, or the whole population's dress |
| Excavated buildings and objects with recorded contexts | Construction, use, repair, material, deposition; sometimes institutional function | A complete theology decoded from a floor plan or animal ornament |
| Armenian, Syriac, Greek, and other external witnesses | Encounters, rival representations, and information otherwise missing | An impartial census of Iranian doctrines or a timeless national character |
| Post-conquest Middle Persian compilations | Priestly preservation, argument, selection, and reworking of older learning | The unedited voice of a particular third- or sixth-century court |
| Later Persian epic and Islamic-era transmission | Reception, transformed royal memory, new audiences and literary aims | Direct testimony for Sasanian costume, speech, or an unchanged state religion |

- Use **event date / composition or redaction date / surviving witness date** as separate fields; a late copy may preserve early matter, but attribution is not automatic dating (Boyce 1984; Sundermann 1989).
- Much surviving Book Pahlavi literature was compiled or reworked after the conquest; the *Dēnkard* and *Bundahišn* have layered transmission. Their learned cosmologies are indispensable but not photographic descriptions of 224–651 society.
- This dossier is strongest on elite, priestly, and represented systems; village speech, women's everyday clothing, regional variation, and workshop explanations of imagery are comparatively thin.
- Sasanian rule encompassed multiple languages and religions. Payne (2015) shows Christian participation in Iranian institutions within unequal inclusion: neither perpetual universal persecution nor modern religious equality is an adequate default.

## 2. Language family, writing, and institutional registers

- Middle Persian, often called **pārsīg** in its own tradition, is a Southwestern Iranian language: Iranian → Indo-Iranian → Indo-European. Parthian is a distinct Northwestern Iranian language, not simply its informal register (Sundermann 1989).
- Avestan is an older Iranian liturgical language, not “very formal Persian.” **Zand** denotes interpretation/translation/commentary, not a separate language called “Zend.”
- “Pahlavi” may label a script tradition or Zoroastrian Middle Persian literature; specify which. Do not collapse language, alphabet, religion, and ethnicity into one variable.
- Inscriptional and Book Pahlavi derive from Aramaic writing traditions; heterograms preserve Aramaic written forms read as Iranian words. Book Pahlavi also has merged letter shapes and conservative spellings (MacKenzie 1971; Sundermann 1989).
- Example of the mechanism, not a pronunciation puzzle: a written Aramaic-derived dog heterogram **KLBʾ** can be read Middle Persian **sag**. A fictional scribe can therefore possess reading knowledge unavailable to someone who merely recognizes letter shapes.
- Manichaean Middle Persian uses a different script tradition and religious lexicon; the Christian Pahlavi Psalter adds another manuscript context. One language need not imply one institution or one sacred alphabet.
- Early royal multilingualism is concretely visible in Šābuhr I's inscription at the Kaʿba-ye Zardošt, in Middle Persian, Parthian, and Greek; do not project that exact combination across every later reign (Daryaee 2009).

| Domain / audience | Attested textual footing | Register mechanism to model | Coupled visual or institutional cue: design option, not historical law |
|---|---|---|---|
| Royal proclamation | Monumental inscriptions and coin titulature | Genealogy, accumulated titles, Mazda-worshipping self-presentation, claims to sovereignty | Crown, seal, monument, and title may identify the same office in different media |
| Administration and law | Documents, sealings, legal literature | Technical distinctions, office labels, named parties, conservative formulae | An official's seal authenticates an act that clothing alone cannot authorize |
| Ritual performance | Avestan recitation and Middle Persian explanation | Different competences for exact recitation, translation, and adjudication | Ritual preparation regulates who can speak and handle implements |
| Learned cosmology | *Bundahišn* and related Pahlavi books | Taxonomy, correspondences, origins, divisions of cosmic time | A diagram or sanctuary lecture teaches an institutional interpretation |
| Andarz instruction | *Dēnkard* VI and attributed advice collections | Maxims, prohibitions, balanced alternatives, lists, exempla | Teacher/learner relation can outrank spectacle or expensive dress |
| Manichaean teaching | Hymns, sermons, doctrinal writing | Shared Iranian words can receive different theological referents | A script and book community can mark affiliation without a unique ethnic costume |
| Household and market conversation | Much less directly recoverable than elite written forms | Do not invent an attested slang grammar from literary contrast | Any proposed everyday dialogue is reconstruction/design, not a recovered transcript |

- Apply Halliday's **field–tenor–mode** from `sociolinguistics-registers.md`: priest-to-initiate explanation and priest-to-priest dispute are different registers even when both are Middle Persian.
- Keep **transliteration** (written signs), **transcription** (proposed linguistic reading), and **translation** distinct. Fix a convention for š/x/ā and historical names before generating a fictional naming corpus.

## 3. Mythic core without a flattened “Persian pantheon”

| System element | Reference distinction | Coupling opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Ohrmazd / Ahriman | Good creation and destructive opposition; different textual formulations of their relation must remain distinguishable | Ethical vocabulary, ritual maintenance, and the explanation of damage can reinforce one another |
| Amahraspands and yazads | Learned categories of beneficent beings/powers; not interchangeable “department gods” | Calendar names, personal names, protected creations, and professional obligations can intersect |
| Mihr, Anāhīd, Wahrām | Historically important divine names with different roles and media of attestation | A name can occur in devotion, kingship, and personal naming without every occurrence having identical force |
| Mēnōg / gētīg | Spiritual and material modes of existence; the material creation is not intrinsically evil | Material care and skilled work can be morally positive rather than escape from a worthless world |
| Creation / mixture / final renovation | Learned accounts place the present struggle within a finite cosmic history; **frašgird** names final renovation | Ritual, household ethics, and historical expectation can operate on different timescales |
| Xwarrah | Glory/fortune associated with legitimacy and success, with a complex textual and visual history | Public praise may claim legitimate power while narratives describe its loss; no single motif is a guaranteed translation |

- Read these through Boyce (1984), Shaked (1994), and the *Bundahišn* editions; they are distinctions within traditions, not a license to merge every era's formulation into one creed.
- The *Bundahišn* organizes sky, water, earth, plants, animals, humanity, and fire within a connected creation. Its bodily microcosm discussion (Greater recension, chapter 28) supplies a **textual correspondence system**, not proof that every building embodies the same diagram.
- A worldbuilder may use a finite conflict to couple stewardship vocabulary, agricultural work, and purification; the imagined causal linkage must be labelled a design choice, not an undocumented Sasanian institution.

## 4. Zurvanism: an evidence problem, not a ready-made sect package

- **Secure distinction:** Zurwān means time; a text mentioning time, destiny, or astrology does not by that fact attest the specific myth in which Zurwān fathers Ohrmazd and Ahriman.
- The birth myth is reported prominently through polemical external witnesses, including fifth-century Armenian Eznik of Kołb; later Syriac testimony, such as Theodore bar Kōnay, must not be silently redated into the Sasanian period (witnesses translated/discussed in Zaehner 1955).
- **Contested reconstruction:** how widely such ideas circulated, whether “Zurvanism” designates a separate organized sect, and whether any period had a uniformly Zurvanite state religion. Neither broad royal patronage nor a distinct institutional church follows from the myth's attestation.

| Proposal or shortcut | Calibration | Safer treatment |
|---|---|---|
| “Every Sasanian worshipped Time above Ohrmazd” | Unsupported universalization | Specify witness, place, date, and what the witness actually reports |
| A single perfectly uniform orthodoxy across four centuries | Scholarly disagreement; compare Boyce 1996 and Shaked 1994 | Track court claims, priestly norms, local practice, and rival reports separately |
| Zaehner's materialist/ascetic/fatalistic varieties are documented denominations | Modern analytical reconstruction, not recovered membership lists | Attribute the typology to Zaehner; never manufacture sect temples or uniforms from it |
| Every use of Zurwān in Manichaean Middle Persian proves Zoroastrian sect membership | Cross-tradition lexical conflation | Identify the referent within that text's own system |
| Fatalism explains an eternal “Persian mentality” or inevitable conversion | Essentializing civilizational psychology, not a defensible historical mechanism | Seek institutions, coercion, patronage, economic conditions, and specific transmission |

- Boyce (1957), Zaehner (1955), Shaked (1994), and Boyce (1996) are a **debate shelf**, not interchangeable authorities for one settled conclusion.
- Fictional application: distinguish a philosophical proposition, a polemical accusation, and a public ritual obligation. They can overlap without producing separate peoples or costume codes.
- Do not invent a Zurvanite color, haircut, or liturgical language. Any such choice is **DESIGN**, and evidence of time symbolism alone cannot authenticate it.

## 5. Fire sanctuaries and cosmology: keep text, archaeology, and inference apart

- The assignment's expression **“senith fire-temple cosmology”** is not an Iranological technical term: “senith” is an archaic English spelling of **zenith** (O.Fr. *cenith*; Stratmann, *A Middle English Dictionary*, s.v.; Chaucer, *A Treatise on the Astrolabe* i.19). Treat the phrase as a sky/zenith reference, not a priestly term; do not invent a Persian etymology, priesthood, or doctrine for it, and a celestial alignment still needs site-specific evidence.
- **Ātaxš-kadag**, “fire-house,” is useful Middle Persian institutional vocabulary; do not replace every historical name with modern Iranian or Gujarati temple terminology (MacKenzie 1971; Boyce 1975).
- Fire participates in the good creation and purity practices; “fire worship” by itself explains neither the theology nor sanctuary operations. Distinguish cosmic fire, a named sacred fire, its maintained physical flame, and its building.
- Greater *Bundahišn* chapter 18 treats the fires; compare chapter 1's cosmogony and chapter 36's reckoning of time. Iranian and Indian recensions have different chapter numbering: chapter 18 here is not Indian chapter 18.

| Named great fire | Traditional social association | Evidence discipline | Fictional coupling to consider |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ādur Farnbag, also rendered Farro-bay | Priests | A learned social association is not a complete map of actual worshippers | Clerical genealogies, teaching authority, temple archives |
| Ādur Gušnasp | Warriors; strong royal association | Taḵt-e Solaymān provides a major archaeological anchor; do not transfer every site's history to it | Patronage, royal pilgrimage, military legitimacy, endowed maintenance |
| Ādur Burzēn-Mihr | Cultivators | Exact localization and reconstructed institutional history are less secure | Agricultural benefaction and competing claims to ancient foundation |

- These associations are transmitted in learned traditions, including *Dēnkard* VI; they are not evidence that society consisted of exactly three sealed castes. Compare Shaked (1979), Boyce (1975), and Greater *Bundahišn* 18.
- **Čahārṭāq** describes a four-arched architectural form; a surviving domed bay is not automatically a fire temple. Require context, installations, access arrangements, stratigraphy, and comparison before assigning function (Schippmann 1971; Boyce 1975).
- Do not deduce a sky map from a dome, a universal zenith rite from an opening, or a three-tier cosmos from the three great fires: alignment evidence is real but site-specific and not uniform (pre-Sasanian temples “not built consistently on either a cardinal nor ordinal alignment,” while the early, mid-, and late Sasanian groups he treats share an ordinal/intercardinal alignment and entrances vary by site — Hozhabri 2013, p. 29), the sacred fire is kept shielded from direct sunlight (Tahmasbi 2012, after Huff), and the three great fires carry estate associations rather than cosmic tiers (Boyce 1975; Shaked 1979). Such readings need independent site-specific textual or archaeological support.
- Do not back-project the full modern classification and consecration procedures of Ātaš Bahrām/Ādarān/Dādgāh onto every Sasanian sanctuary; distinguish later ritual evidence from period documentation.
- Useful design chain: **protected element → fuel and water requirements → trained service → endowment and access rules → ritual vocabulary → clothing/handling constraints**. Every arrow is a design question, not an automatic historical deduction.
- Ritual mouth-coverings and purity garments require their own dated attestations. A modern Parsi ritual outfit is not a complete reconstruction of a sixth-century Iranian priest's wardrobe.

## 6. Senmurv silk: four claims that must not be collapsed

- **Textual bird:** Middle Persian **sēnmurw** belongs to the history behind New Persian **sīmorḡ** and Avestan Saēna bird traditions. The *Bundahišn* links the bird with the many-seeded tree; this is not simply a generic phoenix (Schmidt 2002; Greater *Bundahišn* 16 and 24).
- **Conventional image label:** “senmurv” commonly names a winged composite quadruped with a canine-looking head and elaborate tail. Naming the image does not prove that its makers identified it with the textual bird; that identification is disputed (Schmidt 2002; Compareti 2015).
- Do not import ʿAṭṭār's much later **sī murḡ / thirty birds** wordplay as the historical etymology or as a Sasanian design instruction. Later Persian mystical reception is a separate era and interpretive context (Schmidt 2002).

| Claim | What is observable or checkable | What remains interpretive |
|---|---|---|
| “This textile has a repeated composite creature in roundels” | Object, technical report, image, repeat layout, surviving colors | The creature's indigenous name and the wearer's explanation |
| “This is Sasanian-period silk” | Fiber/weave analysis, dating, excavation or collection history, comparisons | Pattern alone cannot identify a workshop, polity, or century |
| “This image was elite or court-associated” | Particular represented garments and luxury-object contexts | Exclusive royal ownership or a universal legal restriction |
| “This means cosmic renewal, protection, or royal glory” | A named scholar's argument and its supporting texts/context — e.g. Compareti (2015) reads the dog-faced winged composite as *farr* (xwarrah, “glory”), on Sogdian countermarks bearing *farn*; Schmidt (2002) keeps the sēnmurw label | A securely decoded meaning for every occurrence; a meaning claim without such a named argument stays design, not attestation |

- Images conventionally called senmurvs appear in discussions of late Sasanian court dress, including Ṭāq-e Bostān; verify the particular figure, relief phase, and proposed date before claiming an exact ruler's personal emblem (Peck 1992; Compareti 2015).
- Pearl roundels, confronted animals, ribbons, and vegetal fillers travel through Eurasian textile repertoires. Shared pattern vocabulary does not settle production origin, ethnic identity, or religious affiliation.
- Record **fiber / weave / dye evidence / repeat / findspot / proposed workshop / object date / reuse date / proposed meaning** independently; a silk preserved in a later treasury can have a different use-history from its manufacture.
- Safe fictional borrowing: let weavers, court patrons, clergy, merchants, and foreign buyers interpret one expensive repeat differently. The plurality is a design mechanism, not a recovered Sasanian conversation.
- Costume-to-language hook: distinguish the workshop's shape-name, the court's praise-name, and an imported market label; semantic drift can survive a dynasty without preserving its original explanation.

## 7. Andarz wisdom: authority is a register, not merely “ancient proverbs”

- **Andarz** means counsel/admonition; collections link conduct, self-command, social obligations, religious merit, and judgment. The category includes varied forms and audiences, not one immutable genre voice (Shaked 1979; Boyce 1984).
- *Dēnkard* VI is an anthological collection of sayings, anecdotes, and advice. Its sages' attributions and repeated introductory formulae manufacture/preserve authority; they do not turn the whole book into contemporary transcripts of named Sasanian speakers.
- Advice attributed to Ādurbād ī Mahraspandān and Wuzurgmihr offers models of remembered priestly and courtly authority; attribution, historical person, and extant text date require separate entries.

| Register operation | Formal resource | Social effect | Fictional exercise, not an original translation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Senior instructs junior | Short injunction; prohibition; antithesis | Makes advice memorable and correction repeatable | Write a work rule, then a paired warning about abusing it |
| Sage is asked to discriminate | Question–answer and ranked alternatives | Stages judgment as learned expertise | Ask which obligation takes precedence when two duties conflict |
| Anonymous wisdom receives a pedigree | Introductory attribution and transmission chain | Makes remembered authority portable | Preserve one saying in rival teachers' attributed versions |
| Ethics joins religious accounting | Consequence, merit, soul, right action | Connects mundane decisions with cosmic stakes | Contrast immediate advantage with the obligation's longer horizon |
| Enumeration organizes knowledge | Numbered virtues, dangers, duties | Enables recitation and examination | Give a teaching list a stable order, then show a novice's omission |

- Distinguish **wisdom instruction** from cosmological exposition, legal case reasoning, and courtly accomplishment display; overlap does not erase their different audiences and speech acts.
- *Mēnōg ī Xrad* supplies a question–answer wisdom comparator; *Xusrōw ī Kawādān ud rēdag* supplies courtly testing of accomplishment and taste, not an unmediated transcript or simply another maxim collection (Boyce 1984; Sundermann 1989).
- For fictional dialogue, change **speaker relation + authority source + sentence architecture**, not just vocabulary: an elder's terse admonition, a clerk's qualifying clause, and a cosmologist's classification should remain recognizably different.
- Avoid faux translations. Label newly written counsel **DESIGN: English drafting analogue**; invented Middle Persian, reconstructed slang, or a claimed exact historical formula is unattested by default and must be checked against the lexicon and grammar (MacKenzie 1971; Durkin-Meisterernst 2014) before it is presented as anything more.

## 8. Era staging and material signature

The following bins are designer staging tools, not an indigenous periodization or a claim of synchronous change everywhere (Daryaee 2009; Peck 1992).

| Stage | Evidence / institutional emphasis | Language–symbol–dress staging move |
|---|---|---|
| Third century: foundation and expansion | Early royal inscriptions, coins, reliefs; competing royal/priestly self-presentations | Keep multilingual proclamation visible; attach regalia to a named reign rather than a generic Persian king |
| Fourth–fifth centuries | Long imperial continuity with changing frontier and religious relationships | Let offices and communities retain different memories; do not assume one crown, court style, or linguistic balance throughout |
| Late fifth–sixth centuries | Political contestation and reforms associated with Kawād I and Xusrōw I | Model institution-building and patronage; do not date every later wise-king anecdote to its reputed speaker |
| Late sixth century–628 | Late court imagery and intense inter-imperial entanglement | Rework luxury repertoires through named routes and patrons; exact relief attribution may remain disputed |
| 628–651 | Dynastic disruption, warfare, conquest, end of Sasanian kingship | Regalia, coin legends, and claims of continuity need not change at the same speed |
| Post-651 transmission | Continued communities, later Pahlavi compilation, transformed Persian royal memory | Separate political rupture, language change, manuscript survival, and changes in narrative meaning |

- Represented dress includes trousers, belted long-sleeved garments, coats, boots, caps, elaborate crowns and ribbons; silhouette, fabric, fastening, headgear, and ornament need separate evidence (Peck 1992).
- **Royal:** crowns and coin portraits provide reign-sensitive comparison; images communicate claims, not a complete inventory of actual outfits. Reused motifs and uncertain identifications forbid a one-symbol/one-king decoder.
- **Elite and martial:** riding-compatible silhouettes and conspicuous belts can be useful comparisons, but a depicted belt does not by itself establish an exact office or sumptuary statute.
- **Textiles:** silk is a luxury medium; do not clothe an entire population in the surviving elite figured repertoire or infer weave structure from carved ornament alone.
- **Women and non-elites:** divine, royal, and ceremonial representations overrepresent selected bodies and occasions — ordinary women at domestic work are essentially undepicted (Malekān & Mohammadifar 2013) and the surviving material allows little generalization (Peck 1992). Everyday cut, workwear variation, dye access, and regional differences remain design space here.
- Design comparison sheet: **occasion / social position / permitted action / speech register / garment construction / headgear / motif interpretation / object provenance**. Do not treat costly material, sacred purity, and legal rank as synonyms.

## 9. Naming practices: separate inherited name, devotion, relation, and title

| Name layer | Historical reference pattern | Safe generative use / failure check |
|---|---|---|
| Theophoric and protective elements | Mihr-, Ohrmazd-, Wahrām-, Ādur- occur in Iranian naming traditions | Make devotional vocabulary available to names without assuming every bearer has the same religious biography |
| Repeated royal names | Ardašīr, Šābuhr, Wahrām, Yazdgird, Xusrōw recur | Separate person identity from name identity; modern regnal numerals are editorial disambiguation, not surnames |
| Historical forms | Middle Persian Šābuhr and later Persian Šāpūr belong to a name's transmission history | Fix an era and transliteration policy; mixed forms are not evidence of different fictional peoples |
| Kinship and affiliation | Genealogy and relational constructions identify persons in texts | Do not assume every **ī** means “son of”; state the relation explicitly when it matters |
| Office and honorific | Šāhān šāh, priestly designations, scribal and administrative titles | Track an office separately from a hereditary personal name; verify each title's period and jurisdiction |
| Place and foundation names | Royal names participate in settlement naming and political memory | Give each renamed place an older/local form and a later official claim; exact historical etymologies need a lexical source |

- MacKenzie (1971) is the lexical check; Daryaee (2009) supplies historical context. A recognizably Iranian-looking concatenation is not proof of grammatical or historical validity.
- Do not populate a village by recycling only kings' and high priests' names; the source selection is elite-heavy. Women's seals are identified chiefly by female names, most often *-dukht* compounds (Malekān & Mohammadifar 2013); broader female, regional, and non-elite frequencies need the dedicated onomastic corpora (Gignoux 1986; Supplément 2003), not extrapolation from royal lists.
- For a fictional culture, record **name form / pronunciation stage / naming relation / institutional title / alternate-language rendering** separately; deliver options, not a canonized dynasty list.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

- **A distributed authority system:** monumental titulature, scribal orthography, exact ritual recitation, and portable maxims can authorize different kinds of action without all belonging to one speech level.
- **Maintenance as cosmology:** good creation can be defended through material care; connect fuel, water, labor, endowment, handling rules, and technical vocabulary rather than drawing only a mystical flame logo.
- **A contested luxury sign:** one composite animal can acquire workshop, court, devotional, and foreign readings; leave the real motif's identification unresolved while deliberately specifying the fictional alternatives.
- **Asynchronous eras:** a conquest can end kingship while learned writing, sacred service, textile repeats, and remembered names continue and change at different rates.
- **A traceable lore handoff:** send an evidence table, competing interpretations, naming/register constraints, and design options tagged **ATTESTED / INFERRED / DESIGN / TO-VERIFY**. These tag the packet's provenance; lore-shokunin alone selects canon.

## 11. Provenance cautions and recheck targets

- **Chronicle scarcity:** a continuous contemporary indigenous Sasanian narrative is not available here; inscriptions are interested public claims, later chronicles have transmission histories, and outsider reports have audiences. Do not fill the gaps with a seamless invented history.
- **Calibration:** older “state church,” ethnic essence, inevitable decline, and national-fatalism models require argument, not repetition. Boyce and Shaked disagree on significant reconstruction questions; cite the disagreement rather than declaring all older work either authoritative or worthless.
- **Colonial-era editions:** preserve their philological usefulness while checking assumptions, translations, recension differences, and racialized terminology. Parsi scholars and manuscript custodians were active producers and preservers of this knowledge, not merely suppliers for European interpretation; Anklesaria's prefatory history makes that agency explicit.
- **Verification boundary:** bibliographic anchors are not a claim of manuscript collation or full-text inspection of every listed work. The Anklesaria edition (reachable 2026-10-18 only through its archived copy — see the source list) and Payne publisher overview provide direct checkpoints; blocked scholarly pages and bibliographic discovery aids cannot establish a disputed detail by themselves.
- **Remaining thin points:** the assignment's “senith” label (an archaic spelling of *zenith*, not an Iranian term); specific textile workshop attribution and original creature-names; exact relief patron/date; site-specific astronomical symbolism; detailed everyday dress and name distributions. None is silently promoted to fact.
- **Recheck rule:** before reusing a disputed detail, obtain the edition passage or object catalogue, record recension/object identifier and page/section, compare the competing interpretation, and retain the uncertainty if that evidence remains unavailable.
