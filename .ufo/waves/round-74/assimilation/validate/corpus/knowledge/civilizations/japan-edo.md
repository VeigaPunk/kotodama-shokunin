## Scope and calibration
- Base geography: principally shogunal Edo and the Kyoto–Osaka cultural circuit; rural domains are comparisons, not interchangeable copies of the capital.
- **Established baseline:** Japanese language history, commercial print culture, status-specific regulation, temple certification, and philological nativism are well documented. Their local combination must still be dated.
- **Rejected simplifications:** one immutable four-caste wardrobe; a completely sealed country; an eternally pure Shinto pantheon; a single national Edo dialect; every fashion change caused by prohibition.
- **Method:** separate prescription, recorded enforcement, surviving object, commercial representation, and later interpretation. None automatically supplies the other four.

## Era staging: use several clocks, not one costume reset
| Historical bracket | Institutional/cultural emphasis | Coupling to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| 1603–mid-seventeenth century | Establishment/consolidation of shogunal rule; anti-Christian measures and registration networks expand | An early setting cannot inherit every later inspection form, print technology, or Edo slang convention |
| Genroku, 1688–1704 | Kyoto–Osaka urban literary prominence; Saikaku, theatre, kosode display and consumption debates | Wealth, rank, and cultivated taste become visibly competing claims; not every chōnin is affluent |
| Eighteenth century before Kansei | Edo publishing and pleasure-quarter literature develop; nishiki-e emerges in 1765 | New reproducibility connects fashion, celebrities, captions, calendars, and connoisseurship |
| Kansei reforms, 1787–1793 | Retrenchment and publishing discipline; Kyōden's 1791 sharebon punishment is a genre landmark | Censorship changes what can be said or pictured; it is not identical to a clothing prohibition |
| Bunka–Bunsei, 1804–1830 | Expanding Edo entertainment and print markets; changing fiction genres | Do not label all comic fiction sharebon or project its eighteenth-century dialogue conventions forward unchanged |
| Tenpō/Bakumatsu, 1830–1868 | Reform campaigns, crises, and foreign-pressure politics; varied kokugaku networks | Popular religion, nativism, commerce, and political loyalty need not move together |
- These are overlapping analytical brackets, not synchronized national phases (Totman 1993; Shirane 2002; Met 2003). Provincial adoption dates vary and require local evidence; no national timetable is asserted here.
- Keep reign-era names (*nengō*), the lunisolar calendar, political transitions, fashion shifts, and genre history as separate fields; an Edo “first month” is not automatically Gregorian January.

## Language family, writing, and register ecology
- Japanese belongs to **Japonic**, alongside the Ryukyuan languages; Ainu is not Japonic. A demonstrated genealogical membership in an “Altaic” family is not an established premise (Frellesvig 2010).
- Edo language is not simply modern standard Japanese with archaic pronouns. Date regional forms and distinguish Kyoto/Osaka usage, emerging Edo prestige, learned writing, and performed social voices.
| Written/spoken resource | Social work | Reconstruction constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Kanji with kana | Mixed-script vernacular, lexical distinction, visual wordplay | Modern character forms, kana inventories, spelling, and punctuation cannot be assumed |
| *Kanbun* and *kundoku* | Literary Sinitic composition and Japanese reading practices; scholarly/administrative authority | Script competence does not prove a Chinese-speaking population; written and spoken syntax differ |
| Kana-heavy commercial dialogue | Readable enacted speech, interpersonal timing, jokes and status mistakes | Orthography is a mediated representation, not an audio transcript |
| Learned classical Japanese | Poetry, exegesis, antiquarian authority | Antiquity is a cultivated register, not everyone's everyday speech |
| Cursive manuscripts and carved lettering | Bureaucratic or commercial access control through literacy | “Can read kana” is not equivalent to “can read every hand and genre” |
- Build a register matrix by speaker, addressee, task, venue, and written medium. Honorific/humble choices index relationships; a warrior label alone does not determine every utterance.
- Exact Edo sound values, local pitch patterns, and attested pronoun frequencies are thinly covered here: none is reconstructed in this entry. Do not generate pseudo-Edo dialogue from modern media catchphrases.

## The “four classes”: status model versus actual dress jurisdiction
- *Shi–nō–kō–shō* (士農工商: warriors, cultivators, artisans, merchants) is a useful historical ideological ordering, **not a sufficient census or a single statute assigning four uniforms** (Howell 2005).
- *Mibun* means status/standing, not simply income. *Chōnin* groups urban commoners in town institutions; merchant and artisan are not always separate legal populations with separate textile entitlements.
| Conventional category | Dress/material questions | Necessary qualification |
|---|---|---|
| *Shi* 士, warriors | Service rank, occasion, household livery/crest, formal hakama and kamishimo, entitlement to paired swords | Samurai were regulated too; formal display obligations and economy could conflict. Silk was not exclusively a warrior fiber in actual practice |
| *Nō* 農, cultivators | Workwear, bast fibers/cotton, village office, festival/wedding exceptions, local silk restrictions | Producer of silk does not imply permission to wear it; “all peasants wore identical brown cotton” erases region, season, and village elites |
| *Kō* 工, artisans | Occupational tools, work movement, workshop identity, cloth quality and skill | A craft occupation does not supply a universal separate wardrobe; town and domain jurisdiction must be established |
| *Shō* 商, merchants | Shop/house identity, public respectability, household servants, conspicuous silk and embellishment | Money could buy fashionable objects without buying equivalent status; restrictions often named townspeople, women, or servants rather than “merchants” alone |
- Courtiers, clergy, performers, itinerants, and discriminated status groups do not fit neatly inside that diagram. Historical labels such as *eta* and *hinin* are discriminatory categories, not neutral fantasy “races.”
- Ainu communities and the Ryukyu kingdom require their own political and linguistic histories; neither is an exotic fifth rung of this model (Howell 2005).

## Dress-law detail: an ordinance ledger, not a color chart
- **Normative framework:** *ken'yakurei* 倹約令, economy/frugality regulations; bakufu and domain orders also govern food, furnishings, ceremonies, transport, and gifts. Do not place every clothing rule under one supposed *Buke shohatto* dress code.
- For each rule record **issuer → place/date → addressee → prohibited material/technique/object → exemption → sanction → enforcement witness**. A repeated prohibition proves renewed concern, not universal compliance or universal failure.
| Reported example | Regulated sign and recipient | Evidence/calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Osaka courtesan regulations reported for 1617 | Gold/silver embellishment, gold thread, embroidery and certain tie-dye display in clothing supplied by proprietors | Shively pp. 132–133 via glossary only; the ordinance text, textile terms, and clause grouping are not collated against the original |
| Edo, 1648, household servants; separate Osaka notices | Fine-silk restrictions on servants; Osaka notices also target silk/velvet obi and underbelts | Shively p. 128 via glossary only; the original wording, the Osaka date, and the translation of silk grades are not confirmed against the article |
| Edo townspeople, 1719 | Woolen capes; related restrictions extend beyond clothing to furnishing, ceremonies, transport, and sword display | Shively pp. 129–130 via glossary only; the clothing clauses and exceptions are not collated against the original |
| Regulations for warrior households, not merely commoners | Expenditure and display should fit degree; some statements condemn being too frugal as well as too extravagant | Shively pp. 148–153 via glossary only; issuer and date of the reported 1710 formulation are not confirmed against the original |
- These are **retrieval leads with explicit source chains**, not primary-law translations ready to quote. No unverified fine, universal silk ban, compulsory palette, or price ceiling is supplied here.
- Distinguish fiber (*silk/cotton/bast*), weave, dye, embroidery, metal thread, and finished garment. Prohibiting one conspicuous technique does not prohibit every costly garment.
- Paired swords are a strong warrior-status sign, but exceptional grants and rules for commoners' short swords defeat “only samurai ever carried any sword” (Shively pp. 143, 160 via glossary; local permissions are excerpt-level reports, not collated law).
- Hidden or subtly worked luxury is a useful **possible response**: lining, small pattern, weave quality, accessories. Scarcity, taste, occupational requirements, and imitation also matter; do not derive all *iki* from law.

## Costume and material signature
| Item/resource | Useful description | Coupled meaning and limit |
|---|---|---|
| *Kosode* 小袖 | Straight-seamed wrapped robe; term refers to the small sleeve opening, not merely a short sleeve | Historical vocabulary matters; do not substitute a contemporary kimono etiquette manual for Edo evidence |
| *Obi* 帯 | Sash whose width, placement, tying and display change over time | Date the ensemble, not only the robe; modern standardized knots are not a universal Edo baseline |
| *Hakama* 袴 / *kamishimo* 裃 | Lower garment / formal ensemble including the projecting-shoulder *kataginu* and hakama | Office, service and ceremony, not battle armor worn daily by every warrior |
| Cotton and *asa* 麻 | Cotton and bast-fiber textiles support work and seasonal wardrobes | *Asa* can cover hemp/ramie in translation; identify actual fiber before inferring ecology or rank |
| *Yūzen* 友禅 | Resist-dyeing associated with pictorial patterning and late-seventeenth-century fashion | Technique, workshop and patronage are more informative than “luxury = silk” alone; Kyoto, 1680s–90s, traditionally attributed to the fan painter Miyazaki Yūzensai (Fukatsu-Fukuoka 2004) |
| *Uchikake* 打掛 and elaborate hair ornaments | Outer robe and accessories prominent in some elite/bridal/courtesan representations | Representation is role-specific; a courtesan parade is not ordinary women's street dress |
| *Geta*, *zōri*, *waraji* | Wooden clogs, sandals, straw footwear | Task, weather, road and occasion complicate any one-footwear-per-class mapping |
- V&A object examples support wrap construction and pattern-based signaling; family, workshop, era, sex/gender presentation, age, and event must be checked against dated garments or images.
- Surviving spectacular silk and fashionable prints overrepresent durable/collectable prestige objects; repair, reuse, and laboring wardrobes need inventories and material evidence, not imagined uniform poverty.

## Chōnin culture: working vocabulary, not interchangeable “Japanese aesthetics”
| Term | Working gloss | Use in a coupled model |
|---|---|---|
| *Chōnin* 町人 | Town commoners, especially artisan/merchant populations | An urban institutional location; not all urban residents and not synonymous with rich merchant |
| *Machi/chō* 町; *nagaya* 長屋 | Town/neighborhood unit; row-house/tenement | Link address, landlord/neighbors, occupation and documentary identity rather than treating “the city” as one audience |
| *Yagō* 屋号 | House/shop designation | A commercial/social name layer distinct from a personal name or an administrative status |
| *Ukiyo* 浮世 | “Floating world,” enjoying the transient present | Plays against Buddhist “sorrowful world,” *ukiyo* 憂き世; changed valuation of transience, not proof everyone rejects religion |
| *Kuruwa* 廓; *yūkaku* 遊郭 | Enclosed/licensed pleasure district | Bounded access, specialized etiquette and commodified performance; not a synonym for all urban sexuality |
| *Tsū* 通; *sui* 粋 | Knowing connoisseurship; cultivated sophistication | Overlapping, historically situated evaluations; associate *sui* especially with Kamigata, but do not legislate a rigid city-by-city dictionary |
| *Yabo* 野暮 | Unknowing, unsophisticated, socially obtuse | A relational judgment: knowing vocabulary without knowing when to use it can expose pretension |
| *Iki* 粋 | Stylish urban sophistication, restraint and erotic/social knowingness | A situated taste ideal, not an ethnic personality trait or an invariant list of colors |
| *Gesaku* 戯作; *sharebon* 洒落本 | Playful writing; books of wit/fashion/pleasure-quarter manners | Genre and publishing position, not a single spoken dialect |
| *Giri* 義理; *ninjō* 人情 | Social obligation; human feeling | Productive dramatic tension, not an exhaustive two-part Japanese psychology |
- Historical usage shifts across authors and decades (Shirane 2002; Araki 1969). An actor, clerk, courtesan, scholar and village visitor can know different portions of this repertoire.
- Kuki's *Iki no kōzō* (1930) is a later analytical construction. Its philosophical scheme must not be presented as an Edo ordinance, survey, or uncontested national aesthetic.

## Sharebon: performed social competence in dialogue
- Mature eighteenth-century sharebon commonly stage pleasure-quarter encounters, contrasting cultivated knowledge with conspicuous, unsuccessful pretension; *Yūshi hōgen* (1770) is a standard genre landmark (Araki 1969; Shirane 2002).
- Strong scene mechanism: a visitor deploys a fashionable word, clothing claim or etiquette rule; the response reveals whether he understands timing and relationships. Recognition, embarrassment, purchase and appearance reinforce one another.
- Dialogue can encode hesitation, contractions, particles, indirect refusal and asymmetrical deference. Literary selection and comic timing remain authorial constructions; “vernacular-looking” does not mean unmediated speech.
- Early sharebon can use learned/Sinitic modes; do not project mature Edo dialogue conventions onto every work given the label. Later sentimental fiction is not simply the same register with longer plots.
- A courtesan's staged welcome, a customer's boast, a servant's sales pitch, and a magistrate's account of the same visit should not share one vocabulary-and-politeness profile.
- Exact *arinsu*-type forms, pronouns, sex/gender associations and local argot require an identified text and speaker; this entry supplies none, so do not sprinkle them into every Edo character's speech.
- **Calibration:** these are commercially mediated, often male-oriented representations of coercively organized sexual labor. Witty female characters are not sufficient evidence of unrestricted women's agency or first-person testimony.

## Ukiyo-e: visual–verbal register, not a speech dialect
- Distinguish paintings, single-sheet prints, illustrated books, actor likenesses, beauty pictures, famous-place views and other subjects; “ukiyo-e” is not one caption grammar or one social voice.
- Full polychrome *nishiki-e* dates conventionally to 1765; elite calendar commissions helped its development (Met 2003). Do not put an 1830s-looking print shop into early seventeenth-century Edo without a changed technology model.
- Inspect the whole sheet: title/series, names and reading aids, poems, publisher marks, seals, clothing motifs, gestures and depicted setting. A transcription stripped of layout can destroy a joke.
- *Mitate* 見立, comparative/allusive re-presentation, can place a classical or religious referent in contemporary guise; a modern robe in such an image may be a deliberate analogy rather than a historical-costume error.
- Crests, textile patterns, homophones and rebuses can connect celebrity identity to purchasable fashion. Not every indirect name is censorship evasion; identify the edition and applicable rule before assigning that motive to any particular sheet.
- Production couples designer, block cutter, printer and publisher, not a solitary inspired artist (Met 2003). Availability, commission and marketing constrain the public symbolic vocabulary.
- **Transfer method:** record `visible sign → sound/textual cue → alluded person/story → target audience → commodity/occasion`; leave unresolved readings unresolved.

## Mythic and religious core: overlapping institutions, not a closed pantheon
| Layer | Reference anchors | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Courtly ancient narratives | *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon shoki* (720); Izanagi/Izanami, Amaterasu, Susanoo, divine descent | Courtly textual genealogies authorize rule; different textual versions are not a single village creed |
| Shrine/local kami cults | Inari, Hachiman, Tenjin and numerous place- or community-specific powers | Cult titles, patronage and festivals connect territory, profession, genealogy and identity |
| Buddhist devotion and ritual | Amida, Kannon, Jizō; sects, temples, funerary and memorial work | Soteriology and care for the dead coexist with other local obligations; not all temples offer identical practices |
| Combinatory interpretation | *Shinbutsu shūgō*; *honji suijaku*, buddhas/bodhisattvas as original grounds and kami as manifestations | Ritual complexes can sustain multiple names and explanatory layers; the relation is historically argued, not a fixed conversion table |
| Regime sacralization | Ieyasu as Tōshō Daigongen; Nikkō and patronage | A deceased ruler becomes a cult focus through institutions, buildings, ritual and authorized titles |
| Popular good-fortune imagery | Ebisu, Daikokuten and other Seven Lucky Gods groupings | Commerce, imported religious figures and local cults combine; “native versus foreign” is not a clean descriptive split |
- Breen and Teeuwen (2010), Hur (2007), and Fujiwara and Nosco's account of kokugaku supply different analytical windows; their subjects should not be reduced to “nature worship.”
- The 1868 separation of kami and buddhas is a historical rupture to investigate, not permission to project modern institutional boundaries backward into all Edo practice.

| Token/channel | Useful association | Avoid |

## Temple certification: belief becomes documentary belonging
- Separate **danka** 檀家, a temple-affiliated supporting household/relationship, from **terauke** 寺請, temple certification, and from the official compilation of religious/population registers (Hur 2007; Morris 2018:418–420).
| Component | Work performed | Coupled fields |
|---|---|---|
| Parish temple, *danna-dera* | Abbot certifies non-Christian affiliation | Religious status becomes a document; household support and ritual ties matter |
| *Terauke shōmon* / *terauke-jō* | Certificate, in locally variable forms | Name, affiliation, attestation and mobility must agree; it is not itself the entire census |
| *Shūmon ninbetsu aratamechō* 宗門人別改帳 | Local officials/headmen compile religious and population inspection registers | Names, household members, religion, age and demographic changes become administratively linked |
| *Goningumi* 五人組 | Mutual-responsibility household grouping, including religious surveillance duties | Neighbor knowledge can support certification, concealment, accusation or collective liability |
| Temple death/memorial records, including *kakochō* 過去帳 | Record and ritualize the dead | Mortuary names and household continuity need not match the everyday naming register |
- **Staging:** apostate certification precedes wider population coverage; expansion in the 1630s, domain-office requirements in 1664/1665, and register standardization in 1671 are distinct developments (Morris 2018:415, 419).
- Morris explicitly notes differing dates in the scholarship: 1635 versus 1638 for general certificate requirements. Do not turn one into a universally implemented “system founded on this day” claim.
- Registration could be repeated periodically, often annually, with local procedural variation; in at least one domain the submitted register was compiled every year while the village kept a single copy in use for several years (Ogawa 2017). The cycle and document requirements still need the particular village's or town's own records.
- This system policed outward affiliation and population belonging, not transparent interior belief. Hidden Christian communities and selective enforcement invalidate “certificate proves sincere conversion.”
- *Fumie* 踏絵 are images used in image-treading tests; *e-fumi* names the practice. Such tests are not the same procedure as temple certification, nor should identical use everywhere be assumed.
- A purported 1613 *Gojōmoku* certification ordinance circulating around 1735 is identified as a forgery in the scholarship discussed by Morris (2018:420). A backdated document may itself be evidence of institutional power, not of seventeenth-century law.
- **Fictional-method application:** make a ritual specialist an administrative witness; then test what happens when spoken identity, household record, funerary obligation and travel paperwork disagree. Do not invent an Edo-wide costume badge for the registered.

## Kokugaku: reconstructing language, then claiming an ancient world
- Main inspected synthesis: Gideon Fujiwara and Peter Nosco, [“The Kokugaku (Native Japan Studies) School”](https://plato.stanford.edu/archives/fall2021/entries/kokugaku-school/), *Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, Fall 2021 edition, first published 2018. “National learning” is a broad label for differing schools and projects, not one continuous party platform.
| Scholar/project | Linguistic/textual operation | Mythic/political claim to keep distinct from the operation |
|---|---|---|
| Keichū, 1640–1701 | Philological study of the *Man'yōshū*, readings and ancient language | Later treated as a precursor; his Shingon Buddhist training defeats a simple “pure native learning versus all Buddhism” origin story |
| Kada no Azumamaro, 1666–1736 | Antiquarian study, classical commentary, educational advocacy | The recovery of old words is linked to recovering old meanings and a native Way |
| Kamo no Mabuchi, 1697–1769 | Ancient poetry and language as access to an older *kokoro*, heart/mind | An ideal natural antiquity contrasts polemically with allegedly corrupting Chinese moral systems |
| Motoori Norinaga, 1730–1801 | *Kojiki-den* exegesis, ancient usage, literary criticism and *mono no aware* | Philological achievement and claims about kami, native hearts and imperial descent are separable propositions |
| Hirata Atsutane, 1776–1843 | Broad synthesis extending beyond philological exegesis into spirits, afterlife and popular teaching | Rival afterlife models and national superiority claims; not simply Norinaga's conclusions repeated |
- Useful linguistic focus: recover historical readings, compare word uses and grammatical relations, and distinguish classical textual strata from contemporary speech. Exact Norinaga grammatical analyses and examples need the relevant edition; none is cited here.
- **Calibration:** recognizing real philological achievements does not validate “uncontaminated language proves an inherently superior people.” Chinese-derived vocabulary, writing, scholarship and institutions are historical resources, not empirical impurities.
- *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* are ancient authored compilations; reading them as a transparent, unanimous pre-contact religion is an ideological reconstruction, not an uncontested recovery.
- *Kotodama* 言霊, efficacy/spirit of words, can enter such discussions, but it is not a universal explanation of Japanese grammar or a warrant for treating every utterance as magic. A scholar's particular use requires a specific text; this entry cites none.
- **Transfer framework:** `old text → specialist reading method → claimed ancient mentality → ritual/political program → dissenting interpretation`. Let technical agreement coexist with disagreement about gods and government.

## Naming practices: several names for several institutions
| Naming layer | Historical pattern to investigate | Coupled consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Warrior house and person | Family/house names, common-use names and formal adult personal names; changes over a life | A document, intimate conversation and lineage claim may identify one person differently |
| Town or village household | Personal names alongside house/shop/place designations; surnames could exist despite restrictions on their public use | “Commoners had no surnames until Meiji” is too strong: surname use existed under place- and role-specific restrictions, and entitlement varies by locality and status without a single national rule |
| Artistic/literary identity | *Gō* 号, pen/art names; stage names and artistic houses | A shared or inherited name can convey professional succession rather than biological descent |
| Temple/mortuary identity | *Kaimyō* 戒名 and memorial records | Religious naming links death, status, ritual service and household memory rather than merely replacing a civil name |

## What a worldbuilder can take
1. **Choose the intersection:** date, locality, household status, occupation, gender/age, venue and relevant authority before choosing a robe or voice.
2. **Build legal and taste hierarchies separately:** a low-status person can be wealthy or linguistically fashionable; a high-status person can be indebted or socially inept.
3. **Couple one restriction to several responses:** a textile prohibition might move prestige into lining or craftsmanship, change connoisseur vocabulary, and generate a satirical scene; do not assert that it must do all three.
4. **Keep institution-specific truth:** a temple certificate, a shop name, a poem, a crest and a mythic genealogy can disagree without the setting becoming incoherent. Specify who has power to resolve each disagreement.
5. **Make antiquarianism generative:** scholars can reconstruct the same old language competently yet disagree about pantheon, moral order and political legitimacy; a later “restoration” need not reproduce the past it claims.
6. **Handoff only proposals:** give lore-shokunin the mechanism, alternative interpretations, era constraints, source chain and open checks. Names, gods, costume laws and history for a fictional world remain unadopted until that sibling records canon.

## Provenance cautions and unresolved coverage
- No original Japanese ordinance, registry or manuscript was collated for this entry. Shively's dated clothing examples remain secondary-excerpt retrieval leads; verify their inline flags before presenting a quotation, fine, exemption or exact ban as settled law.
- Scholarly books above are real standard editions, but bibliography-only checks are not represented as fresh readings. Open passages, publisher descriptions, and museum summaries have explicitly different evidentiary weight.
- Japan's Edo archives are abundant, not globally “scarce”; coverage here is nevertheless thin for individual domains, women's first-person speech, laboring wardrobes, rural dialects and unrecorded oral ritual. A court chronicle cannot fill those gaps by itself.
- Ainu and Ryukyuan material, minority religious practice and discriminated communities need dedicated sources, including community scholarship; administrative records often speak about them in coercive state categories.
- Do not repeat older “feudal caste,” timeless national-character or total-isolation narratives as neutral fact. Early-modern trade/contact persisted through multiple controlled channels; a museum's art-period summary is not adequate evidence for a sealed society.
- Commercial images and male-authored pleasure literature encode patronage, marketing and gendered power. Modern costume reconstructions, Kuki's retrospective philosophy, and nationalist readings of kokugaku require separate calibration, not silent fusion into an authentic “Edo spirit.”
