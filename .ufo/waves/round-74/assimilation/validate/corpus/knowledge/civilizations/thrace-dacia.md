## Scope and calibration: do not collapse the labels
- “Thrace” is a changing geographic/political designation, not one language community, dynasty, costume, or religious institution across all periods.
- “Getae” and “Daci” are ancient ethnonyms transmitted chiefly through outsiders; their distributions and the authors' classifications must be dated, not projected onto modern national borders.
- Herodotus includes the Getae among Thracians (4.93); Strabo distinguishes eastern Getae and western Daci while reporting the same language (7.3.12–13). Ancient classification is evidence, not a modern linguistic demonstration.
- Use a claim ledger: **object/text → findspot or speaker → date → medium/language → represented group → interpretation → confidence**.

| Calibration label | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Materially attested | A tomb, relief, inscription, vessel, or depicted garment exists in a dated context | Its owner's complete theology or ethnic self-definition |
| Textually attested report | A named ancient author makes a particular statement | That the practice occurred exactly as narrated, everywhere |
| Plausible interpretation | Several contextual observations support an explanation | A deciphered indigenous doctrine |
| Contested reconstruction | Competing models explain the same fragmentary record | Permission to silently select the most romantic version |
| Unknown / `(to-verify)` | The evidence or its checking is insufficient here | A gap to fill with invented ancient words or rites |

## Era staging: asynchronous contact zones, not one evolutionary ladder
| Working stage | Historical anchor | Language/material coupling | Worldbuilding use, not historical law |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Iron Age / before abundant external description | Regionally varied pre-Classical communities | Archaeological sequences exceed recoverable native speech | Let a material horizon lack a recoverable self-name; do not equate pots with peoples |
| Archaic–Classical contact, roughly sixth–fourth centuries BCE | Greek coastal settlements, Persian campaigns, Thracian polities; Hdt | Greek writing transmits names and labels while local speech persists | Separate diplomatic script from household language; contact is not total replacement |
| Early Hellenistic, late fourth–third centuries BCE | Sveshtari/Sboryanovo; Kazanlak; Dromichaetes tradition | Monumental burial, elite display, and shared artistic techniques | Imported style can advertise local legitimacy rather than erased identity |
| Late Dacian kingdoms, first century BCE–early second century CE | Burebista in Str; Orastie fortress/sanctuary complexes | Mountain strongholds couple military resources and ritual architecture | Stage centralized patronage as a contingent political choice |
| Roman conquest and provincial settings | Thrace annexed in 46 CE; Dacian wars of 101–102 and 105–106 CE | Imperial images and inscriptions change whose categories survive | A conqueror's costume taxonomy is not the society's self-description |
- UNESCO dates the Orastie works to the first centuries BCE/CE and stresses a fusion of classical and later Iron Age techniques; this is not proof of an unchanged Bronze Age civilization.
- The stage dates orient comparison, not ethnic birth/death dates. Southern Thrace, the lower Danube, and the Carpathian centers do not enter each stage simultaneously.

## Language family and writing: an attestation ceiling
- Working classification: Thracian and Dacian are conventionally treated as fragmentarily attested Indo-European languages; their exact relationship, internal varieties, and relationship to other Balkan languages remain disputed (Sowa; Str 7.3.10, 13).
- “Daco-Thracian” is a hypothesis/umbrella, not a reconstructed standard language whose grammar is ready for use. Neither “same language” in Strabo nor similar-looking names supplies a sound-law demonstration.
- The record is weighted toward personal names, place names, divine names, reported glosses, and difficult short inscriptions; it cannot support a confident full syntax, case paradigm, or phoneme inventory here.
- Greek and later Latin are major recording media. Alphabet, language, ethnicity, and political allegiance are separate variables: Greek letters need not mean Greek speech, and a Greek inscription need not mean a Greek-identifying patron.
- The Ezerovo ring inscription is written in Greek characters and conventionally identified as Thracian; proposed word divisions and translations are disputed. Do not present a modern fluent translation as settled: Sowa (2020, pp. 798–799) finds no standard approved edition and readings that differ greatly between scholars (retrograde writing, scriptio continua, 61 letters, last line on the rim).
- Kyolmen is another difficult inscription frequently placed in this discussion; its exact reading, linguistic attribution, and dating require a standard epigraphic edition, which does not exist (Sowa 2020, p. 798 n. 12: readings differ; dating given variously as 5th–6th century BCE). It is not an independent decipherment key.

| Evidence type | Productive inference | Forbidden shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Name copied in Greek/Latin | A transmission layer with the recorder's spelling conventions | Treat every letter as an exact native phoneme |
| Recurring place-name ending, e.g. -dava or -bria | A distribution worth comparing by date and source | Derive a complete language boundary or native dictionary from one suffix |
| Inscribed prestige object | Access to a writing practice in that object's context | Assume universal literacy or an exclusively priestly script |
| External “same language” report | A historical perception of relatedness/communication | Claim modern comparative reconstruction has been completed |
- Application: construct a fictional language on its own explicit rules; borrow the *evidence ecology*—unequal literacy, adapted scripts, rival transcriptions—not pseudo-deciphered Thracian grammar.
- In a reference handoff distinguish **attested spelling**, **normalized scholarly form**, **transliteration**, **proposed etymology**, and **new invention** in separate fields.

## Naming practices: preserve transmission, refuse fake etymology
- Dromichaetes/Dromichaites is a Greek-transmitted royal name in the lower-Danube tradition (Diod 21.12; Str 7.3.8); its spelling is not a reusable native morphology lesson.
- Boerebistas/Byrebistas, commonly normalized Burebista, and Decaeneus, commonly Deceneus, illustrate variant transmission and normalization (Str 7.3.5, 11). Keep aliases attached to the same historical referent.
- Salmoxis/Zalmoxis/Zamolxis likewise varies in transmission; Gebeleizis appears in Hdt 4.94. A variant spelling and an asserted alternative divine name are different kinds of evidence.
- Strabo's discussion of Attic slave names Geta and Daüs (7.3.12) is particularly useful: a label can be assigned by outsiders through enslavement rather than inherited in the source community.
- Do not infer hereditary surnames, universal patronymics, gender endings, birth rituals, or clan structure from this handful of elite/external forms. Those systems are not reconstructed here.

| Naming layer to design | Coupled institution | Evidence-inspired constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Local spoken name versus court inscription | Household kinship versus diplomatic literacy | Record who normalizes, shortens, or substitutes the name |
| Ruler's public designation | Kingship, ritual office, prestige patronage | A title, personal name, and divine identification need not coincide |
| Place-name retained after conquest | Administrative continuity and language contact | Date each spelling; retention alone does not prove unchanged population |
| Outsider's ethnonym or slave name | Trade, war, coercion | Never quietly promote an imposed label into an indigenous self-name |

## Mythic and pantheon core: reports, not a recovered catechism
- Herodotus 5.7 labels Thracian gods Ares, Dionysus, and Artemis, and singles out rulers' Hermes worship/ancestry claim. This is **interpretatio graeca**: a Greek classification, not a native pantheon chart.
- Hdt 7.111 reports a mountain oracle called Dionysian, with Bessi serving as interpreters and a priestess delivering responses. The named institutional roles are more usable than an invented universal “shaman” category.
- Hdt 5.3–5 itself distinguishes groups' death customs; do not combine Trausian mourning at birth or the account of wife-killing above the Crestonaeans into a standard Getic rite.
- Symbolic associations may be local, restricted, or transformed. “Dionysian,” “Orphic,” “Getic,” and “Dacian” are not interchangeable labels for a secret primordial religion.

### Zalmoxis immortality: the source stack
| Layer | What the passage actually supplies | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Hdt 4.93–94 | Getae described as believing the dead go to Salmoxis; some call the recipient Gebeleizis | Attested Greek report; identity of the divine names and precise ontology remain contested |
| Hdt 4.94 | A messenger sent by being thrown onto spear points; a five-year interval; arrows shot during storms | Reported practice, not excavated confirmation; avoid generalized “bloodthirsty Thracians” |
| Hdt 4.95 | Pontic/Hellespontine Greeks tell of a former slave of Pythagoras teaching elite banqueters, hiding underground for three years, and returning in the fourth | An explicitly attributed Greek explanatory story, with civilizing-Greek assumptions |
| Hdt 4.96 | Herodotus suspends judgment on the chamber tale and places Salmoxis earlier than Pythagoras | Preserve the author's skepticism; do not quote 4.95 as his unqualified historical verdict |
| Str 7.3.5, 11 | Priest/counsellor, royal partnership, sacred cavern/mountain, celestial expertise, Decaeneus, ascetic obedience | Later synthesis; not independent proof of every Herodotean detail |
- The minimal craft distinction is **continued existence with a god** versus **resurrection**, **reincarnation**, and **ritual initiation**. Related motifs do not establish identical doctrines.
- Neither Hdt's immortality wording nor his suggestion of exclusive devotion establishes a complete modern-style monotheist theology; his own wider Thracian descriptions complicate a uniform reading.
- Archaeological calibration: the cited tombs and Orastie sanctuaries establish mortuary/ritual investments, not Zalmoxis' name, creed, or the underground chamber in Hdt 4.95.
- An inaccessible chamber, circular sanctuary, or sacred mountain is not diagnostic by shape alone. Strabo names the sacred mountain and its river Cogaeonum (7.3.5: “the people took up the notion that the mountain was sacred and they so call it, but its name is Cogaeonum”), but identifying a specific excavated chamber or sanctuary with that name—or with Zalmoxis—requires independent evidence beyond shape or name.
- Strabo's vine-cutting account (7.3.11) concerns Burebista/Decaeneus; it does not cancel older wine vessels or prove that every Dacian in every period abstained.
- Design use: vary access to the sacred specialist, the audience admitted to a feast, and the rhetoric of survival after death; mark every invented rite and name as fictional, not “restored.”

## Getic tomb-feast symbolism: triangulate rather than fuse
| Evidence/context | Observable content | Interpretation boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Hdt 5.8, wealthy Thracian funerals | Display of the dead, sacrifice, lamentation, feast, burial/cremation, mound, contests | Broad elite report; not a Getic service book or an excavation of a particular tomb |
| Diod 21.12.4–6, Dromichaetes hosting Lysimachus | Splendid couches, precious vessels, and lavish foreign meal contrast with straw beds, modest food, and horn/wood cups | Diplomatic/moralizing banquet narrative, not a funeral; staged frugality is not an inventory of all Getic wealth |
| Sveshtari/Sboryanovo, early Hellenistic Getic setting | Stone funerary beds; female caryatids with vegetal/chiton forms; painted horseman approaching a deity offering a wreath | ICOMOS 1985, p. 2 interprets heroization; the deity's exact name and a complete initiatory sequence are not supplied |
| Kazanlak, southern Thracian setting | Painted central couple, attendants, dining display, horses/chariots | Useful funerary-feast comparison, not automatically Getic and not an illustration of Hdt's Zalmoxis feast |
- “Getic tomb-feast” here is a **comparison problem**, not the name of one securely reconstructed cult. Keep textual hospitality, mortuary provisioning, and painted banqueting distinguishable.
- Sveshtari's beds and elite funerary space support comparison with dwelling/banquet arrangements; its crowning scene is not itself a depicted meal (ICOMOS 1985).
- Heroization is the reading the ICOMOS evaluation gives the Sveshtari lunette (1985, p. 2: the heroizing of the deceased receiving a wreath from a deity); farewell, ancestral reception, and afterlife privilege remain candidate readings of funerary imagery; a sacred-marriage interpretation would need its own argued source.
- A wreath, cup, horse, garment, or vegetal figure can operate within status display without being a one-to-one code for a named god.
- The deceased's identity at Sveshtari, including proposals naming Dromichaetes, is not established here; do not turn a proposed royal attribution into the bridge connecting every text and object.
- Costume coupling: distinguish the patron/deceased's idealized dress, attendants' dress, and sculpted divine/supporting figures. A caryatid's palmette-shaped chiton is not a sewing pattern for everyday Getic women.
- Method: compare **host/guest → seating → vessel material → portion/service → gesture → spatial threshold → audience**. Then ask which relationships remain visible after death.
- Fictional application: a public feast can distribute rank, while a tomb selectively monumentalizes that ranking; avoid making every household share an elite's burial resources or afterlife claims.

## Dacian wolf-dragon banner: object, performance, reception
- Trajan's Column (113 CE), representing the Dacian wars, depicts standards conventionally called Dacian dracones; the Column is a Roman victory monument, not indigenous ethnography.
- “Wolf-dragon” is a modern descriptive shorthand for the canine-like/open-mouthed head and elongated serpentine/tubular form. Exact species identification is interpretation, not a native inscription naming the animal.
- Draco/drakōn is a Latin/Greek naming frame, not a recovered Dacian word for the standard. Do not invent a Dacian etymology from it.
- Arrian, Tactica 35, describes “Scythian” standards of sewn colored material which inflate during movement and hiss; his setting is cavalry display/training. This is a comparator, not a report that every Dacian banner used the same construction.
- A hollow-headed windsock model explains visible animation; exact Dacian materials, internal sound mechanism, dimensions, and colors are not recorded, and no ancient source describes a species-specific “wolf howl”: Arrian (Tact. 35) gives sewn cloth that fills with wind and hisses, and Ammianus 16.10.7 has dragons “hissing as if roused by anger.”
- The surviving Niederbieber metal draco head belongs to a later Roman context: it is not an excavated complete banner from Decebalus' army. Check find context before borrowing its engineering.
- Coulston (1991) is the specialist recheck for chronology and distribution; the device's origin and transmission among Dacian, steppe, and Roman settings must not be reduced to an exclusive ethnic invention.

| Coupled function | Evidence/design distinction | Productive design question |
|---|---|---|
| Visual rallying and group recognition | Military depiction supports use as a standard; precise unit organization needs separate evidence | Who may carry, repair, or surrender it? |
| Animated animal and audible motion | Arrian supports inflation/hissing in his described context | Does movement “awaken” the emblem, and is that an invented local interpretation? |
| Prestige craft and textile supply | Headwork/sewn cloth are a material model, not a recovered Dacian workshop manual | Which specialists and resources keep the emblem operational? |
| Protection, ancestry, cult | Possible religious meanings are not established by the form alone | If chosen for fiction, what additional rite or language makes this meaning legible? |
- Do not equate the banner with Zalmoxis, a universal wolf totem, warrior lycanthropy, or direct continuity into Romanian folklore without independent evidence: the draco was not exclusive to Dacians (Ștefan 2021, p. 48 n. 110), pre-Roman Dacian iconography does not give dogs/wolves an established dominant symbolic weight (Ștefan 2021, p. 48), and Dacian–Romanian continuity is analyzed as a modern metahistory (Szabó 2022).
- A flat modern flag, fixed heraldic blazon, and national color scheme are not recoverable from the carved standard alone.

## “Thracian Orphic tablets”: repair the category before borrowing
- Orpheus is located in Thracian settings in Greek myth; that does not make texts attributed to him ethnically Thracian, linguistically Thracian, or Getic/Dacian testimony.
- Gold lamellae conventionally called “Orphic” or “Bacchic” are inscribed in Greek and distributed across different communities. They are not a homogeneous indigenous Thracian scripture corpus (GJ; Edmonds).
- Distinguish **mythic author**, **language**, **findspot**, **deceased**, **ritual association**, and **modern scholarly label**; none automatically determines the other five.

| Comparison group | Location/material/textual character | Useful motif; limit |
|---|---|---|
| Hipponion and Petelia lamellae | Southern Italy; Greek texts on gold; GJ pp. 4–7 | Underworld route, guarded water, Memory, declared cosmic kinship; local formulations differ |
| Pelinna lamellae | Thessaly; Greek gold leaves; GJ pp. 36–37 | Release attributed to Bacchios and address to Persephone; not proof of Zalmoxis equivalence |
| Olbia bone tablets | Greek Black Sea colonial setting; brief inscriptions on bone | Orphic/Dionysian associations discussed in scholarship; different material, context, and uncertain function |
| Derveni papyrus | Macedonia; Greek commentary on a poem attributed to Orpheus | Textual interpretation and ritual thought; a papyrus, not a gold tablet or Dacian catechism |
- Regional Pontic/northern-Aegean finds require item-level checking; this entry does not claim that every lamella lay outside ancient Thrace. Specific “Thracian tablet” claims need findspot, edition, language, date, and burial context `(to-verify)`.
- “Passport for the dead” / Totenpass is a modern analytic metaphor. It highlights authorized identity and access, but does not establish an ancient centralized issuing institution.
- The corpse, inscription, gold substrate, remembered utterance, and guardian-response scenario form a useful **speech-act/material coupling**: words can claim identity or entitlement rather than simply describe beliefs.
- Do not assume that every bearer read the text, that all versions derived from one original, or that every object was merely a practical travel guide to the underworld.
- Mnemosyne/Memory, Persephone, and Bacchios belong to the Greek materials being compared; importing them into a reconstructed Getic pantheon would erase the evidentiary boundary.
- Orphism's unity is contested: Edmonds (2013) treats the label as a historically polemical classification; other scholarship groups texts and practices more positively. State the chosen analytical model.
- A single “Thracian Orphism” joining royal tombs, Zalmoxis, Greek lamellae, and all animal emblems is a contested synthesis, not a recovered native creed.
- Application: borrow the mechanism of portable ritual language and differentiated afterlife access; invent its institutions and wording explicitly rather than copying a tablet as allegedly Dacian speech.

## Costume and material signatures: keep medium and date attached
| Reference surface | Defensible observation | Calibration / design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Hdt 7.75, Asian Thracians/Bithynians in Xerxes' army | Fox-skin headgear, tunics, multicolored mantles, fawnskin footwear/leg covering, small shields and javelins | Specific contingent in an external account; not every Thracian civilian or a late Dacian uniform |
| Hdt 5.6, tattooing | Author associates tattooing with noble status (“to be tattooed is a sign of noble birth; to bear no such marks is for the baser sort”) | Report of status marking only; the passage supplies no placement, design, or color |
| Sveshtari's caryatids | Stylized colored chitons spreading into inverted palmette forms | Architectural/divine-funerary representation, not direct everyday dress documentation |
| Kazanlak's feast imagery | Differential clothing and adornment appear within a staged elite scene | Compare social roles and bodily gestures before deriving construction details |
| Roman representations of Dacians | Tunics, trousers, cloaks, and soft caps form a recognizable visual repertoire | Roman artistic selection; Dio 68.9.1 names “the long-haired men” and “the noblest among the cap-wearers” among Dacian envoys, but matching each depicted headgear type to a fixed social estate still needs its own argument |
| Orastie fortresses/sanctuaries | Monumental military and religious construction within late Dacian centers | Couple patronage and craft resources; do not infer a named god from masonry alone |
- Do not use the cap as an exclusive ethnic diagnostic: similar soft headgear appears in other ancient representational traditions.
- Material bias matters: gold, silver, stone, and elite painting survive/display differently from ordinary cloth, skin, wood, and unmonumentalized households.
- A precious vessel may be imported, gifted, looted, dedicated, or locally made; motif origin alone cannot decide the owner's spoken language or religious allegiance.
- Build reference sheets in the order **occasion → wearer/depicted role → garment layer → fastening → material → manufacture → evidence medium → uncertainty**.
- Greek descriptive terms and modern archaeological labels are not automatically native garment vocabulary; coin invented terms only within an independently designed fictional lexicon.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **A multilingual frontier model:** local speech, imported writing, elite naming, and imperial classification can coexist; language shift need not synchronize with conquest or a new costume fashion.
- **An authority circuit:** restricted access to a sacred specialist can reinforce kingship, while banqueting distributes visible rank; this is a design option drawn from the source stack, not proof of one pan-Thracian constitution.
- **A mortuary status grammar:** seats, cups, service, horses, garments, and thresholds can preserve or renegotiate social relationships after death without all implying the same afterlife theology.
- **A moving emblem system:** animal form becomes politically effective through carrying, wind, sound, maintenance, and display; specify those practices rather than declaring “wolf = warrior” universally.
- **A ritual utterance system:** a small material object can authorize a voice or identity beyond ordinary life; distinguish memorized speech, literate specialists, purchasers, and intended audiences as fictional choices.
- Apply the sequence: choose one era/contact zone → select independently evidenced features → record why their coupling is compatible → name unsupported bridges → offer alternatives to lore-shokunin.
- Handoff fields: `reference/date | source locus | observed feature | proposed fictional transformation | language/myth/symbol/dress consequence | confidence | alternatives | canon-owner decision`.
- Transfer mechanisms and constraints, not an unlabelled collage of Greek salvation texts, Getic tombs, Roman banners, and modern national folklore. This file selects no canon.

## Provenance cautions and recheck queue
- Ancient literary witnesses are uneven and often external; some are downstream of coastal colonial contact, others of imperial conquest. “Simple barbarian,” Greek tutelage, spectacular sacrifice, and royal frugality are rhetorical frames as well as information.
- Herodotus wrote centuries before Trajan's Column. Strabo reworks traditions, and Diodorus survives here through later excerpting; agreement cannot automatically be counted as independent corroboration.
