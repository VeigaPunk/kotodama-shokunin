## Scope and calibration key

- **Mexica** here principally means the people of Mexico-Tenochtitlan, with Tlatelolco distinguished where sources require; **Nahua** is broader, and **Aztec** is a variable modern umbrella, not one universal ancient self-name.
- Focus: Late Postclassic central Mexico and its early colonial documentation; no assumption that one court's usages describe every Nahuatl-speaking polity or century.
- **Attested** means a specified source records it; **corroborated** means more than one evidence class supports it; **contested** means the interpretation is disputed; **craft abstraction** means a reusable design method, not a historical claim.
- A colonial source can preserve Indigenous expertise while serving Christian, royal, or elite-family purposes. Neither “transparent native survival” nor “all invented by Spaniards” is an adequate default.

| Evidence surface | What it can support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| *Codex Mendoza*, c. 1541–1542 | Pictorial conventions, remembered tribute and dress distinctions; read images with Spanish glosses | Neutral observation of every preconquest community; an empire-wide personnel regulation |
| *Florentine Codex*, sixteenth-century compilation | Nahua testimony, formal speech, ritual descriptions, images; compare Nahuatl, Spanish, and pictures | A single unmediated voice; exact equivalence between the two textual columns |
| Durán and Chimalpahin | Migration variants, dynastic interests, named groups and places | Archaeological location of Aztlan; a literal transcript of centuries-old journeys |
| Excavated monuments and buildings | Material contexts, iconographic arrangements, dated or stylistically staged objects | Automatic recovery of every original spoken explanation |
| Colonial Nahuatl administrative documents | Local terminology, household/land claims, changing institutions | Unchanged reproduction of pre-1521 arrangements |

## Language family, writing, and reverential register

- Nahuatl belongs to the **Nahuan branch of Uto-Aztecan**; “Classical Nahuatl” denotes a historical written-language tradition, not the name of all living Nahuatl varieties in all their diversity.
- Grammatical design handles: extensive affixation, person marking on predicates, productive compounding, incorporation, and valency-changing derivation. Do not imitate the language by merely attaching `-tl` to arbitrary roots. (Launey 2011.)
- Vowel length and the **saltillo** matter; colonial spelling often underrepresents them. Conventional `x` represents /ʃ/, `hu/uh` /w/, and `tl` /tɬ/; Spanish-looking spelling does not imply Spanish pronunciation.
- This entry uses familiar conventional spellings without supplying speculative vowel lengths. Choose one documented orthographic convention before generating a name list.
- Indigenous painted records combine histories, calendar signs, quantities, and named-person/place signs, including phonetic/rebus devices; they are not an alphabet or merely decorative pictures. The range of phonetic encoding is a specialist question, not settled by calling everything “pictographic.”
- Roman-alphabet Nahuatl writing expanded after conquest in the hands of Indigenous writers as well as missionaries; alphabetization did not create Nahua historical thought. Compare *Mendoza* place/year signs with Chimalpahin's alphabetic annals.

| Register mechanism | Historical reference | Coupling opportunity / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Nominal reverential `-tzin-` | Respect, but also tenderness, pity, or diminutive force depending on context; Lockhart 2001: 240 | Relational stance, not a mechanically superior “caste suffix” |
| Verbal reverentials | Reflexive morphology combined with causative/applicative derivation; distribution depends on verb and construction; Launey 2011, Lesson 21 | Respect alters grammar, not only vocabulary; honorific meaning is not literal self-causation |
| Reverential morphology in reference | Can concern the person spoken about, not only the hearer | Separate addressee, referent, and narrator's stance in a dialogue design |
| Formal rhetoric | Parallelism, paired expressions, admonition, ceremonial address; *Florentine Codex*, Book 6 | Institutional speech can preserve older imagery while everyday speech changes |
| Authority vocabulary | *tlatoani*, “one who speaks,” is a ruler title; Lockhart 2001: 238–239 | Couple legitimate command to recognized speech; do not translate every bearer as “emperor” |
| Awe/respect vocabulary | *mahuiztli*: fear/awe/respect or someone worthy of it; Karttunen 1992: 133 | A court's respect lexicon may carry danger as well as affection |

- **Application method:** specify speaker → addressee → respected referent → occasion → grammatical strategy → visible insignia. Rank alone does not determine every speech choice.
- Keep pronouns, nominal reverentials, derived verbs, and rhetorical style as separate controls; they need not all rise together on one “politeness level” slider.
- Do not append `-tzin` to a complete sentence or mechanically to every complete dictionary noun. Stem formation, possession, plurality, and vocative constructions require grammatical checks.
- Calibration: usable morphological architecture, not a production-ready dialogue grammar. Reconstructed preconquest conversational etiquette cannot be assumed beyond specific documented paradigms — honorific registers differ quantitatively between varieties and centuries even in the documented corpus (Romero 2014) — so check the paradigm before commissioning Nahuatl dialogue; modern community norms must not be back-projected from one present-day variety.

## Migration myth and pantheon: a charter distributed across places

- Huitzilopochtli is the Mexica patron associated with warfare and solar power; migration traditions make divine direction, community identity, ritual burdens, stops, and eventual settlement mutually explanatory. (Durán 1994; Chimalpahin 1997, vol. 1: 68–79.)
- Aztlan and Chicomoztoc are not interchangeable labels for a securely excavated homeland. Their relations and the departure sequence vary by account; precise geographical identification is **contested**.
- Huitzilopochtli's name contains hummingbird and left-side elements, but its full interpretation is disputed; Karttunen's head-final analysis complicates the familiar “Hummingbird of the South” gloss. Do not build an entire directional cosmology from that English rendering.

| Narrative operation | Reference pattern | Reusable mechanism, not a plot to copy |
|---|---|---|
| Departure authorized by a deity | Divine speech directs movement and identity in migration accounts | A people can define belonging through an obligation rather than common territory alone |
| Deity carried through successive stops | Sacred burdens, god-bearers, and temporary places of worship | A portable institution can precede a permanent capital |
| Named constituent groups travel together | Chimalpahin 1997, vol. 1: 77 associates migrating calpulli with their gods | Federation memory can survive in local cult names after settlement |
| Place receives a sacred explanation | Coatepec and foundation episodes connect landscape and collective history | A route can become a chain of ritual and land claims |
| Settlement sign becomes civic identity | Eagle and nopal imagery at Tenochtitlan; *Mendoza*, fol. 2r | A founding image can recur in names, documents, regalia, and territorial argument |

- **Keep variants distinct:** the birth of Huitzilopochtli at Coatepec, defending Coatlicue against Coyolxauhqui and the Centzon Huitznahua (*Florentine Codex*, Book 3, ch. 1), is not simply the same episode as every migration chronicle's factional conflict at Coatepec.
- The Templo Mayor's Huitzilopochtli shrine and Coyolxauhqui monument materialize the sacred-mountain relationship; built setting, body placement, and ritual action carry meaning together. Solar readings are influential, not a license to reduce every participant to a celestial object.
- *Mendoza* fol. 2r shows the eagle/cactus foundation image **without a snake**. Do not project the later national coat of arms backward into every version of the foundation tradition.

| Pantheon relationship | Minimum usable reference | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Huitzilopochtli / Tlaloc | Paired shrines at the Templo Mayor; war/patronage and rain/agricultural power | Not evidence that all religion divides into two exclusive departments |
| Tezcatlipoca / Quetzalcoatl | Major powers in creation, sovereignty, and ritual traditions | No stable universal “evil god versus good god” opposition |
| Coatlicue / Coyolxauhqui / Huitzilopochtli | Kinship and violence in the Coatepec narrative | Preserve the source's genealogy; other accounts organize origins differently |
| Local patrons / imperial patron | Group-specific cults coexist with central institutions | Imperial prominence does not erase all other gods or local claims |

- Read pantheon rows through Sahagún Books 1–3 and Miller & Taube (1993); they are a relationship map, not an exhaustive god list or universal “archetype” taxonomy.
- Craft abstraction: build a **charter–route–institution–image chain**, not a mandatory hero's journey. Ask who invokes each variant, which settlement benefits, and which rival version the official account sidelines.

## Calpulli: the ward model, and why it needs qualifiers

- Conventional spelling **calpulli**, also **calpolli**: literally “big house”; Molina's 1571 vocabulary includes a large house/hall or barrio. Translation depends on documentary setting, not etymology alone.
- A useful first model is a local corporate/cult group embedded in a larger polity, but “ward,” “clan,” “parish,” and “guild” each capture only part of the evidence. None is an automatic equivalent.
- **Contested:** older reconstructions treated calpulli as uniform kin-clans or standardized territorial wards. Lockhart (1992: 104–109) and the scholarship assembled under the dictionary's *calpolli* entry warn against that uniformity.
- Chimalpahin can use calpolli for migrating groups and **tlaxilacalli** for settled local subdivisions; this is a valuable historical distinction, not a universal replacement rule for every region.
- Calpulco can designate a neighborhood temple in Sahaguntine contexts. Cult building, associated people, and settlement district must not be collapsed just because English uses “ward” for all three.

| Institutional dimension | Questions to extract from a particular source | Fictional coupling test |
|---|---|---|
| Land and membership | Who holds or works which land? Is membership residence, ancestry, dependence, or cult affiliation? | Can a migrant join without claiming the founding ancestry? |
| Shrine and patron | Which deity and ritual office belong to this group? | Does its festival name preserve a migration stop? |
| Labor and tribute | Which service obligations are routed through the group? | Who records arrears, and what is the formal language of demand? |
| Education and mobilization | How do neighborhood institutions connect youth training and war service? | Does military achievement change an individual's privileges without changing the group's obligations? |
| Elders and leadership | Which named officers or elders appear, and in which century? | Can ritual prestige conflict with royal appointment? |

- Household → local subdivision → **altepetl** polity → imperial alliance is a design scaffold, not a claim that every town had identical nested administrative boxes.
- Do not assume every calpulli owned the same land regime, contained one occupation, or constituted an equal-sized military company. Preconquest membership and office rules are documented only per district and period, and the scholarship warns against uniform readings (Lockhart 1992: 104–109; Kellogg 1986, cited in the dictionary's *calpolli* entry); supply a local source before generalizing.
- Craft abstraction: a district can remember itself as an ancestral traveling community while functioning as a landed, taxed neighborhood; names, shrine dress, and legal register need not change at the same speed.

## Symbolic system and xiuhmolpilli: binding time into an institution

| System | Reference structure | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| *tonalpohualli* | 260-day count: 20 day signs cycling with coefficients 1–13 | Repeating signs organize divination and calendrical naming, not twelve European zodiac months |
| *xiuhpohualli* | 365-day year: eighteen 20-day periods plus five nemontemi | Festival obligations and the year's named sequence are separate from the 260-day count |
| Year-name sequence | Coefficients 1–13 combined with four year signs: Rabbit, Reed, Flint, House | A year label recurs; it is not an unambiguous absolute date without context |
| *xiuhmolpilli* | Conventional year-bundle / 52-year-cycle terminology | A finite span becomes a bound, represented, and ritually handled object |
| Successive Suns | Cosmogonic ages; the present Sun is associated with 4 Movement | Cosmic age, 52-year cycle, and royal reign are different temporal scales |

- The ideal calendar recurrence is 18,980 days: `lcm(260, 365) = 52 × 365 = 73 × 260`. Arithmetic establishes a recurrence, not the historical date of every actual ceremony or a leap-day policy.
- Distinguish **xiuhmolpilli** as bundle/cycle terminology from forms such as **xiuhmolpilia / toxiuhmolpilia**, used for binding the years / our years. Check actual text forms instead of treating the conventional English label “New Fire” as a literal translation of every term.
- Terminology is still discussed: the dictionary reports Gordon Whittaker's preference for *xiuhtlalpilli* in a 2023 personal communication. That note is not a settled replacement for the attested forms or for Helmke et al.'s archaeological terminology.
- For the well-documented **1507, 2 Reed** observance, Sahagún's Book 7 describes extinguished fires, an anxious celestial vigil, fire-drilling associated with a sacrificed captive, and redistribution of fire from ceremonial centers to households; Cerro de la Estrella near Iztapalapa is central to the account.
- Bundle imagery and stone bundle effigies make elapsed years materially present. Fire transmission connects cosmic renewal to a network of temples, neighborhoods, and hearths. (Helmke, Nielsen & Rivera Guzmán 2023: 1–3.)
- **Contested schedule:** do not state “every New Fire everywhere, without exception, occurred in 2 Reed at an immutable 52-year interval.” Helmke et al. (2023: 14–17) contrast uniform colonial schedules with varied monument dates and chronicle reports of adjustments.
- **Evidence gap:** earlier rites, calendar correlations, the continuity of practices across different central-Mexican societies, and the identification of individual astronomical observations require separate argument; conversion into an exact European day and date depends on a correlation constant that remains disputed across Mesoamerican calendrics (Barrera Atuesta 2022, with its bibliography).
- Separate the observed rite, reported cosmological danger, and political interpretation. A ceremony can renew obligations and authority without resetting all historical counting to year zero.
- Other couplings: **atl-tlachinolli**, paired water/burning imagery, signifies war; eagle/jaguar pairings evoke martial distinction; precious feathers, greenstone, and turquoise connect sacred display to material procurement. None supplies a universal animal/color dictionary independent of object and occasion. (Miller & Taube 1993.)
- Do not import Maya Long Count calculations, a modern “2012 apocalypse,” or an inevitable end-of-the-world prediction into this Mexica cycle.

## Costume and material signature: rank is more than an animal helmet

- Base garment vocabulary includes **tilmatli** mantle and **maxtlatl** loincloth; **huipilli** and **cueitl** designate major women's garment categories. Read cut, fiber, pattern, fastening, and occasion, not just a silhouette. (*Mendoza*, daily-life section; Sahagún, Book 8.)
- Cotton and maguey-fiber cloth occupy different material/status positions; fine cotton, featherwork, ornaments, and specified garment patterns mark privilege. Do not turn this into “no commoner ever wore cotton”: awarded distinctions and context matter.
- **Ichcahuipilli** is padded cotton armor; **tlahuiztli** names martial costume/insignia in relevant usage; **chimalli** is a shield. Protective layer, outer suit, back device, hairstyle, and civil mantle can carry different information.
- Featherwork is skilled manufacture supported by trade and tribute, not an unlimited supply of wild-animal trophies. An animal-patterned image alone cannot prove a suit was made from that animal's skin.
- Reference locus for the following capture-linked sequence: *Mendoza* **fol. 64r**, interpreted with Berdan & Anawalt (1992/1997), not a modern game's combined “Aztec tech tree.” [Bodleian image and descriptive record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bodl_Arch.Selden.A.1_roll113D_frame66.jpg).

| Depicted attainment / identity | Visible distinction in this source base | Calibration / craft use |
|---|---|---|
| One captive, fol. 64r | Flowered mantle privilege | Achievement changes civilian appearance as well as battlefield equipment |
| Two captives, fol. 64r | Cuextecatl-style suit with conical cap | Named costume styles can encode inherited regional associations |
| Three captives, fol. 64r | Butterfly back device and differentiated mantle | Silhouette and textile pattern are parallel status channels |
| Four captives, fol. 64r | Jaguar costume and animal-head helmet | A precise source-bound capture/dress association, not every jaguar image's meaning |
| Eagle and jaguar warriors | Prestigious martial identities conventionally described as warrior orders/societies; animal-form regalia | Not a proven universal sequence “jaguar, then eagle”; not exhaustive military organization |
| Otomi and shorn-warrior distinctions | Additional elite categories occur beyond the familiar animal pair | Do not equate a costume/rank label automatically with the wearer's ethnicity |
| Command and noble status | Some high officers appear in distinguished mantles rather than more elaborate beast suits | Office, ancestry, achievement, and costume entitlement are intersecting axes |

- Eagle-form figures from the House of the Eagles corroborate the importance of eagle imagery, but do not by themselves recover admission procedures. Eagle/jaguar corporate membership rules, exclusivity, and any universal capture threshold are not established by the sources used here; treat a single rank ladder as an inference from colonial testimony and pictography rather than a recovered constitution.
- Captive-taking mattered, but birth status, office, quality of an achievement, and ceremonial context complicate a simple numerical ladder. Fol. 65r's priest-warrior material must not be spliced into fol. 64r as though it were the same career.
- **Craft abstraction:** record entitlement as `achievement → recognized authority → permitted garment/device → permitted occasion → penalty for false display`; keep everyday dress, battle dress, and deity embodiment distinct.
- Couple this to language by asking who publicly announces the honor, what title can now be used, and whether respectful address recognizes office, age, deed, or ritual role; do not invent an attested Nahuatl rank-to-honorific correspondence.

## Era staging and naming practices

| Stage / clock | Historical anchor | What to vary in a fictional system |
|---|---|---|
| Migration memory | Divergent Aztlan/Chicomoztoc journeys and sacred stops | Portable cult, constituent-group names, landscape charters; not a uniform “primitive era” |
| Capital foundation | 1325 is the conventional commemorative date for Tenochtitlan | Stabilized place-name and emblem; keep conventional chronology separate from archaeological precision |
| Imperial expansion | Triple Alliance formation in 1428 after defeat of Azcapotzalco | Tribute supply, elite display, and competing local versus central titles |
| Ritual renewal within the state | 1507 year-binding | A calendar threshold can renew an existing order rather than found a new dynasty |
| Conquest and colonial transformation | 1521 fall of Tenochtitlan; continuing Nahua communities and writing | Baptismal names, new offices, alphabetic documents, changed display rules; not cultural disappearance |

- Chronology anchors above follow the *Mendoza* historical section and its scholarly commentary; staging directions are **craft abstractions**, not reconstructions of one inevitable cultural sequence.
- Personal names can draw on animals, plants, actions, circumstances, and calendrical associations; distinguish an individual's **name**, **title**, **honorific form**, and **name glyph**. Do not impose a European hereditary surname model on every preconquest record.
- Calendar-sign vocabulary does not imply that every Mexica personal name followed the same day-name formula; Mixtec and Maya naming evidence is not interchangeable with Mexica usage.
- **Cuauhtemoc**, conventionally “Descending Eagle,” illustrates action plus animal imagery; **Nezahualcoyotl**, associated with Texcoco, is not evidence that its bearer was a Tenochtitlan ruler. A Nahuatl name alone does not identify political allegiance. (Karttunen 1992; *Mendoza* commentary.)
- Place-name signs can encode words through depicted objects and rebus devices; locatives such as `-co` and `-tlan` require grammatical analysis, not a random “Mesoamerican-sounding” ending. (Launey 2011; *Mendoza* place-name commentary.)
- Colonial records combine Indigenous names, Christian baptismal names, titles, and local affiliation. Do not assume every additional name is a surname or every Christian name marks abandonment of Indigenous identity. (Lockhart 1992.)

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Respect as grammar:** design several mechanisms for reverence, endearment, and awe; let speaker/referent relationships determine their combinations rather than a single caste chart.
- **Migration as institutional memory:** connect a mobile patron cult to later districts, land claims, shrine dress, and names; retain disagreements between local and royal origin narratives.
- **Dress as a licensed record of deeds:** distinguish achieved honor, inherited privilege, and appointed command. Decide who certifies each entitlement before choosing an animal emblem.
- **Era-binding as logistics:** couple celestial timing, embodied ritual, a tangible year-bundle, and the distribution of renewed fire or another scarce ritual resource through institutions to households.
- **Asynchronous change:** a new ruler can centralize costume manufacture while local cult names and formal speech remain older; conquest can change writing media without erasing all named institutions.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: offer a relation map, variant chronologies, a register-role matrix, a dress-entitlement table, and unresolved questions. Label each as sourced analogy, deliberate transformation, or open option; no fictional god, polity, genealogy, or canon date is established here.

## Provenance cautions and known thin areas

- Checked on 2026-10-17: linked dictionary attestations, scholarly edition reviews, INAH viewer availability, Getty's project description, the fol. 64r image record, and Helmke et al.'s (2023: 14–17) calendar discussion. This is not a claim to have newly read every cited book or article in full.
- Grammar bibliography and Launey's Lesson 21 coverage were checked through its scholarly review; full paradigm verification is explicitly outstanding. Bibliographic certainty must not be represented as sentence-level linguistic verification.
- Chronicle authors worked after conquest; Indigenous contributors were knowledgeable people with divergent positions, and Christian categories, Spanish glosses, patronage, and elite retrospection shape surviving accounts.
- Migration chronology and earliest local institutions are thinner than late imperial imagery. No literal Aztlan location, immutable calpulli constitution, or universal eagle-order admission rule is supplied here.
- Calendar scholarship is not unanimous: preserve the distinction between ideal recurrence, attested observance, debated monumental reading, and modern interpretation. A dictionary's personal-communication note is weaker evidence than a published argument with inspectable data.
- Thin areas: regional women's dress beyond codex conventions, non-elite spoken register, living community-specific honorific norms, and local membership enforcement. Any detailed claim extending this entry into those areas is `(to-verify)`.
- Avoid colonial caricature and tourist essentialism: sacrifice and war were real institutions, not the whole of Nahua life; animal dress is not automatic shapeshifting, all gods are not fixed “archetypes,” and living Nahua peoples are not an extinct aesthetic resource.
