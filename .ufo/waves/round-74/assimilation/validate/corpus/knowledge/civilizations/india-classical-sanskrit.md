## Scope and calibration

- “Classical India” is a working shelf label, not a homogeneous people, religion, language, or dress code. Select a region, century, institution, and social position before using any row.
- Core comparison: first-millennium CE Sanskrit literary and political cultures; earlier precedents and developments through approximately the thirteenth century are explicitly staged below.
- Evidence labels: **textual prescription** describes an author's desired order; **attestation** records a particular inscription or representation; **interpretation** is a scholar's explanatory model; **design transfer** is this entry's fictional method.
- Sanskritization, Pollock's Sanskrit cosmopolis, and the spread of Hindu worship are not interchangeable processes. Sanskrit also served Buddhist and Jain intellectual and literary production. (Pollock 2006; Salomon 1998.)
- This entry is strongest on textual coupling, weaker on regional textile reconstruction and non-elite lived practice. A court poem, law text, and temple image cannot jointly become an unqualified ethnography.

## Language family, writing, and the cosmopolitan mechanism

| Layer | Reference knowledge | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy | Sanskrit is Indo-Aryan within Indo-Iranian within Indo-European; Vedic and Classical Sanskrit are historical/register distinctions, not separate script families | Do not equate a prestigious language with the ancestry of everyone using it. (S98; U08.) |
| Sound and word shape | Vowel length, aspiration, dental/retroflex distinctions, and ś/ṣ/s matter; inflection, compounds, and sandhi affect visible name forms | Keep stems, dictionary forms, spoken forms, and inflected inscriptional forms separate; IAST is a modern scholarly transliteration, not an ancient alphabet. |
| Multilingual ecology | Sanskrit coexisted with Middle Indo-Aryan languages and diverse regional languages, including Dravidian languages | Give courts, merchants, households, monasteries, and ritual specialists overlapping rather than identical repertoires. (P06; S98.) |
| Scripts | Sanskrit has been written in multiple scripts; Brāhmī-derived regional scripts are central to its epigraphic history | Devanāgarī is not “the original Sanskrit script.” Language choice and script choice need separate dates. (S98.) |
| Earlier comparison | Aśokan inscriptions, third century BCE, chiefly use Middle Indo-Aryan; Brāhmī and northwestern Kharoṣṭhī are distinct writing traditions | A Mauryan-looking monument with later Sanskrit conventions requires an explicit fictional history, not a default mixture. (S98; U08.) |
| Political attestation | Rudradāman's Junagadh inscription, approximately 150 CE, is an important early extensive Sanskrit royal inscription | Treat it as a named attestation, not proof that Sanskrit suddenly began, or that his subjects spoke it at home. (S98; P06.) |
| Literary speech | Sanskrit drama conventionally distributes Sanskrit and literary Prakrits among roles, often by status and gender, with exceptions | Stage speech is a representational code, not a census of actual women's language or competence. (P06.) |

- **Pollock's Sanskrit cosmopolis:** a transregional arena of courtly literary competence and political expression, extending beyond South Asia; shared prestige need not require one empire or one vernacular. (P06, part 1.)
- **Institutional chain:** patronage → trained grammarians/poets → recognizable kāvya style and praśasti, or royal eulogy → participation in a wider political-cultural arena. This is a design extraction, not Pollock reduced to “propaganda causes empire.”
- **Vernacularization:** regional languages acquire literary and political functions, often adapting existing cosmopolitan forms; Sanskrit does not simply disappear at a universal cutoff. (P06, part 2.)
- **Calibration: influential, interpretive, debated.** Pollock's emphasis on courtly aesthetic power and his large-scale chronology are a model to test against local evidence, not a unanimous account of every transmission. Avoid either “all Sanskrit was sacred” or “cosmopolitan Sanskrit was wholly secular.”
- Fictional use: define who can commission a polished inscription, who can recite it, who can interpret it, and who only recognizes its script or royal placement. Those are different powers.

## Era model: stage the coupling, not a civilizational ladder

| Working horizon | Language / institution | Myth / symbol | Costume / material caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mauryan comparison, third century BCE | Aśokan edicts, regional language/script choices | Do not back-project a complete later Purāṇic temple system | Contemporary sculpture is a dated comparator, not an all-India wardrobe. (S98; U08.) |
| Early centuries CE, approximately first–third | Expanding Sanskrit royal expression; Kushan and other overlapping regional polities | Buddhist, Jain, and Brahmanical image-making coexist | Northwestern coats/boots and Gandhāran drapery do not describe the whole subcontinent. (U08.) |
| Gupta and contemporaries, approximately fourth–sixth | Courtly Sanskrit, inscriptions, learned and religious patronage | Important Purāṇic developments; Devī-Māhātmya commonly situated around fifth–sixth centuries | Ajanta paintings are elite/religious representations with their own patronage, not photographs of everyone. (P06; C91; U08.) |
| Regional formations, approximately sixth–tenth | Sanskrit alongside expanding regional literary and inscriptional practices | Temple institutions, localized sacred geography, sectarian developments | Specify production region, climate, ritual role, and access to ornaments before selecting silhouettes. (P06; U08.) |
| Later comparator, approximately tenth–thirteenth | Further vernacular literary expansion; continued Sanskrit scholarship | Developed aesthetic and theological commentaries must be dated | Do not import a later court, temple, or festival costume unchanged into an earlier setting. (P06; P16.) |

- These overlapping horizons are not “tribal → Vedic → Hindu → medieval” evolutionary stages. “Gupta golden age” is an evaluative label, not evidence that every region or social group prospered equally.
- Keep **composition**, **redaction**, **surviving manuscript**, **inscription date**, and **depicted story time** in separate fields. A late manuscript can preserve older material without making every passage equally old. (R86; S98.)
- Yuga, manvantara, and kalpa organize theological time; they are not ready-made archaeological periods. Dynastic genealogy may bridge cosmic time and political legitimacy without being an independently verified chronicle. (R86.)

## Purāṇic myth cycles and pantheon grammar

- The Purāṇas are layered, transmitted, and repeatedly reworked textual traditions, not a single authored mythology with one fixed release date. Conventional lists of eighteen major Purāṇas do not remove variation in contents or classification. (R86.)
- **Pañcalakṣaṇa**, the five-characteristic genre schema: sarga, creation; pratisarga, re-creation; vaṃśa, genealogy; manvantara, periods of the Manus; vaṃśānucarita, dynastic histories. Use as one organizing grammar, not an exhaustive description of every Purāṇa. (R86.)

| Mythic focus | Generative relation | Do not flatten into |
|---|---|---|
| Viṣṇu and avatāra narratives | Divine intervention across changing threats and ages; royal and devotional exemplarity | One invariant list of ten incarnations accepted identically everywhere. (R86.) |
| Śiva | Ascetic power and household relations; destruction, renewal, and place-specific worship | A simple “god of destruction” job slot detached from paradox and worship. (R86; K86.) |
| Devī / Mahādevī | Multiplicity of goddesses can be articulated as manifestations of a supreme Goddess | Every goddess merely the wife, or subordinate energy supply, of a male god. (C91; K86.) |
| Brahmā and creation accounts | Creation can be narrated through different sectarian and cosmological perspectives | A universal Trimūrti bureaucracy with three equally central cults. (R86.) |
| Rivers, nāgas, local deities, sacred places | Landscape and community acquire genealogies, episodes, ritual obligations | A leftover “folk layer” outside history or an interchangeable nature-spirit list. (K86; U08.) |

- A narrative can subordinate another deity, identify multiple deities, or make one supreme while preserving others. Record **which text, speaker, community, and occasion** asserts the hierarchy.
- **Devī-Māhātmya worked cycle:** the first episode concerns cosmic sleep and Viṣṇu's awakening; chapters 2–4 narrate the Goddess's battle with Mahiṣa; the later cycle concerns Śumbha and Niśumbha, with a framing story about human petitioners and restoration. Cite the thirteen-chapter work through C91 rather than inventing one invariant Mārkaṇḍeya Purāṇa chapter numbering.
- **Recurrence grammar for fiction:** disruption → concentration or disclosure of power → embodied contest → restoration → boon/praise/ritual remembrance. This is a design transfer from a particular narrative architecture, not a universal Hindu “hero's journey.”
- Keep epic, Purāṇic, liturgical, regional oral, and modern screen versions distinct. A familiar modern composite is not automatically the oldest recoverable version.

## Śakti and the symbolic system: relations before a color chart

- **Śakti** denotes power/capacity; in particular theological settings it is personified or identified with Goddess. It is not a scientific energy substance or one uniform doctrine shared by all Indian traditions. (C91; K86.)
- In the Devī-Māhātmya, divine powers, embodied manifestation, fierce combat, praise, and protection belong together. The analytical unit is an enacted relationship, not “female = fertility.”
- Do not assume worship of powerful goddesses proves equality for historical women; divine agency and social institutions require different evidence. (K86; U08.)

| Symbol complex | Attested interpretive context | Coupled design question |
|---|---|---|
| Goddess, lion, buffalo opponent, donated divine weapons | Durgā/Mahiṣa narrative and iconographic traditions; particular images vary (C91; K86) | Which victory licenses which weapon, epithet, procession, or costume element? |
| Śrī/Lakṣmī, lotus, prosperity | Auspicious abundance and royal associations; not all lotus-bearing figures are Lakṣmī (K86) | Who can display abundance, who produces it, and who disputes its legitimacy? |
| Śiva's ascetic / household contrast | Renunciation and domestic divine relations coexist (K86) | Can the same garment or ornament change value when worn in court, wilderness, or ritual? |
| Mantra and voiced efficacy | Sacred words can act through authorized recitation, not merely denote a deity; reception changes historically (C91) | Does authority lie in semantic comprehension, lineage, performance, or institutional recognition? |
| Oṃ in Manu 2.74 | A specific prescription frames Vedic lessons with the syllable (O05; B86) | Couple permission to speak with teaching and initiation; do not infer one formula for every ritual. |

- Color meanings need deity, rite, object, region, and date. “Red = śakti,” “white = Brahmin,” and “black = evil” are not sufficient historical rules; the varṇa colours that do appear are prescriptive and bounded (white cloth for the Brāhmaṇa, mañjiṣṭhā-dyed for the Kṣatriya, haridrā-dyed for the Vaiśya — Vaśiṣṭha 11.49 and Āpastamba 1.3.1–9, in J20's comparative notes), and colour sets differ by text (Nardi 2006).
- Later tantric diagrams, mantra systems, and modern rainbow-cakra charts are not a default Classical Indian symbolic grid. The spectral colouring is a Western assembly — Blavatsky's transmission from 1879, Leadbeater's *The Chakras* of 1927, and a colour list joined to the familiar chakra qualities only in 1977 (Leland 2016) — so date any local tantric scheme to its own text before era placement.
- For a fictional symbol sheet, record **term → narrative warrant → permitted user → material carrier → occasion → rival interpretation**. Transfer that relation structure; do not reproduce a living sacred formula as decorative gibberish.

## Varṇa dress law: a bounded primary-text case

- **Critical scope:** Manu 2.41–42 regulates brahmacārins, initiated students, in a Brahmanical normative scheme. It is not a decree assigning everyday clothes to every man, woman, occupation, or community in India. (O05; J20; B86.)
- Varṇa is a ranked classificatory schema; jāti denotes historically situated social communities and cannot simply be substituted for the fourfold schema. Neither is a reliable costume-identification algorithm. (O05; U08.)

| Student category in this prescription | Skin, Manu 2.41 | Cloth, Manu 2.41 | Girdle, Manu 2.42 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Brāhmaṇa | Black antelope | Hemp | Muñja grass, described as threefold, even and smooth |
| Kṣatriya | Ruru deer | Flax | Bowstring made of murvā fibre |
| Vaiśya | Male goat | Wool | Hemp-fibre cord |
| Śūdra and other persons | No corresponding assignment in this passage | No corresponding assignment in this passage | Do not invent a fourth set to complete the chart |

- The upper-skin/lower-cloth allocation is explicit in Medhātithi's interpretation and Bühler's rendering; keep that interpretive layer distinct from the compact Sanskrit verse. Plant/fibre equivalents here follow the cited translations, not a new botanical identification.
- The **mekhalā**, waist girdle, is not the same item as the sacrificial thread discussed separately in 2.44. “Sacred cord” is too imprecise for costume design.
- Manu 2.43 permits substitute girdle materials when specified ones are unavailable. Even the normative passage contains an availability rule; a rigid color-coded caste uniform discards its context. (B86; O05.)
- Comparative notes accompanying J20 show disagreement among related normative traditions. That is evidence of textual plurality, not proof of how frequently any rule was obeyed.
- **Calibration: strong textual attestation, unproven general enforcement.** To assert local enforcement, add region-specific institutional evidence. Do not call Manu a single ancient Indian state law code or endorse its exclusionary hierarchy.
- Fictional transfer: specify the legislating/teaching institution, eligible life stage, full ritual kit, substitutes, penalties, dissent, and actual supply. Ritual equipment can classify people without determining their complete wardrobes.

## Costume and material signature: build an ensemble, not “the Indian outfit”

| Component / term | Usable reference distinction | Evidence discipline |
|---|---|---|
| Vāsas/vastra, cloth or garment; uttarīya, upper covering | Broad cloth vocabulary is not a unique cutting pattern | Preserve lexical breadth; derive fastening and drape from dated evidence. (U08; DK02.) |
| Lower wrapping, often labeled antarīya in reconstructions | Wrapped forms vary in length, pleating, tucking, and coverage | `antarīya`/`uttarīya` are costume-history reconstruction labels for lower and upper cloth (Alkazi 1983); drape styles differ by region, and not every ancient wrap is a modern sari or dhoti (Boulanger 1997; Banerjee and Miller 2003). |
| Sewn and unsewn clothing | Wrapping and tailoring can coexist, especially across regions and contacts | “Ancient Indians never sewed” is not an acceptable civilizational rule. (U08.) |
| Fibres and finish | Cotton, wool, plant fibres, silk, skin, and their values depend on place, trade, and use | A sculpted fold cannot identify fibre, weave, dye, or thread count by itself: those determinations come from preserved remains under laboratory analysis (the Mohenjo-daro cotton adhering to a silver vessel, Marshall 1931, 1:32–33). Name the object evidence or leave the recipe open. (U08; J20.) |
| Jewellery, girdles, hair, garlands, scent | Adornment can produce rank, cultivated urbanity, ritual state, or divine recognizability | Do not automatically read every ornament as caste, marriage, or gender law. (DK02; U08.) |
| Renunciant, student, householder, performer, sovereign | Different life roles can use visibly different ensembles within one locality | A deity's costume, a stage costume, and an ordinary person's dress are separate evidence classes. (O05; DK02; U08.) |

- **Inference check:** depicted bare skin may reflect idealization, transparency conventions, a religious body, or chosen costume. Do not infer climate, universal toplessness, or sexual availability from an isolated image.
- **Production check:** specify fibre access, spinning/weaving labour, dye access, laundering, repair, storage, and patronage. The social meaning of luxury requires a production chain, not just gold-colored concept art.
- **Thin area:** this entry supplies no securely reconstructed Ajanta textile recipe or region-wide garment chronology. Such claims need a dated object/image catalogue, excavation context where available, and specialist textile work; mural and sculptural patterns alone cannot settle fibre, dye, or weave.

## Kāma, alaṅkāra, and aesthetic vocabulary

- **Kāma** includes desire and cultivated pleasure; DK02 situates it in relation to dharma and artha. The fourfold life-aim schema also includes mokṣa, liberation, but do not force every text into an identical four-part program.
- The *Kamasutra* includes education, social skills, household life, adornment, and urban cultivated conduct, not merely sexual positions. Its privileged urban perspective is not a neutral survey of all classes. (DK02, book 1.)

| Term / framework | Working distinction | Fictional coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Kāma | Desire, pleasure, cultivated enjoyment | A court can value perfume, poetry, conversation, and garment care together rather than separating “fashion” from “learning.” (DK02.) |
| Alaṅkāra | Ornament/adornment; in poetics, figures and devices of expression | Material ornament and verbal ornament offer a useful analogy, not proof that every jewel corresponds to a particular trope. (P06; P16.) |
| Upamā / rūpaka | Simile / metaphor, within differentiated traditions of poetic analysis | Decide whether rank is spoken through comparison, identification, or a conventional epithet; do not make decorative compounds semantically random. (P06.) |
| Rasa / bhāva | Aesthetic savor or experience / affective states; their relation is theorized, not a one-word translation of “emotion” | Design the intended audience response separately from the character's feelings and costume. (P16.) |
| Śṛṅgāra / vīra / karuṇa | Erotic or amorous, heroic, and pathetic/compassionate aesthetic modes | One deity, person, or garment can support different modes in different scenes; these are not permanent personality types. (P16.) |
| Dhvani | Suggestion, especially developed in Ānandavardhana's ninth-century poetics | A later literary institution may prize implications earlier formalists describe differently; date the theory rather than giving every era the same criticism. (P16.) |

- The *Nāṭyaśāstra* tradition's familiar eight rasas and the later debate over śānta, tranquility, should not become an undated universal “nine emotions” chart. Textual layering, commentators, and applications differ. (P16.)
- Keep nāṭya, drama/performance; kāvya, literary art; devotional experience; and courtly comportment related but distinct. A theatrical costume code cannot by itself prove daily dress practice.
- Applied aesthetic brief: **occasion → patron/audience → desired rasa → register and poetic device → body/adornment choices → material feasibility → dissenting reading**. This is designer tooling, not an ancient indigenous checklist.

## Naming practices: role, grammar, and documentary context

| Naming layer | Reference anchor | Application / guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Personal and dynastic names | Sanskrit compounds and recurring elements appear in royal records; Candragupta and Samudragupta are historical examples (S98; U08) | Build a small licensed inventory and real compound rules; a repeated element need not be a modern hereditary surname. |
| Patronymic, lineage, gotra, affiliation | Documentary identities can specify descent and institutional belonging in different ways (S98) | Decide which document needs which field; gotra is not a universal surname shared by all inhabitants. |
| Titles and praise | Royal titles and epithets can be more elaborate than everyday reference (P06; S98) | Separate personal name, throne style, donor identification, and praise formula; no title alone proves ethnicity. |
| Normative social naming | Manu 2.31–33 ranks naming semantics by varṇa and prescribes pleasing, auspicious feminine names (O05; B86) | Describe this discriminatory prescription as prescription; do not implement it as a universal historical name generator. |
| Ritual / spoken address | Manu 2.49 varies placement of bhavati in students' begging formulas; 2.122–28 regulates salutation and address (B86) | Couple grammar and position with ritual status; personal names need not be the default respectful address. |

- Retain a lemma plus any inflected quotation; do not harvest a genitive from an inscription and silently present it as the citation-form name. Standardize IAST, simplified display spelling, and pronunciation policy independently.
- A Sanskrit name, regional vernacular name, and divine epithet may coexist. A Sanskrit-looking name does not prove the person's mother tongue, caste, or theology. (S98; P06.)

## What a worldbuilder can take

1. Choose a bounded comparison packet: region, overlapping century range, institution, social position, and evidence classes; never “India” alone.
2. Transfer **prestige infrastructure**: who teaches the high language, funds inscription and performance, controls access, and translates it into local speech.
3. Transfer **narrative warrants**: an episode authorizes an epithet, object, ritual action, garment, or claim to power; retain rival versions and rival interpreters.
4. Transfer **embodied classifications**: distinguish initiation kit from daily clothing, deity image from performer, and textual rules from compliance; include substitutes and supply constraints.
5. Transfer **historical drift**: a regional language enters public patronage; an old symbol gains a new sectarian reading; a luxury material changes access. Change language, myth, and costume together, not all at once by fiat.
6. Hand lore-shokunin a proposal with source/locator, evidence label, era/region, uncertainty, proposed coupling, and rejected alternatives. These are reference-backed options, not canon declarations.

## Provenance cautions and research gaps

- **Chronicles and dates:** royal praśastis are interested eulogies, not neutral annals. Many Purāṇic layers lack securely dated authors or composition events; exact local chronology can be scarce even where texts survive abundantly. Do not manufacture reign-by-reign narrative continuity. (S98; R86.)
- **Colonial mediation:** B86 and other early translations use dated categories and sometimes flatten dharma into “law” or social groups into rigid taxonomies. J20 is a scholarly Indian translation, not exempt from historical translation choices; modern mirrors may contain transcription errors. Use O05 for disputed Sanskrit readings.
- **Contestation:** Sanskrit cosmopolitanism is an explanatory framework, not proof of an apolitical elite or a single coercive language policy. Textual varṇa is not a population census; deity taxonomy is not religious consensus.
- **Discredited shortcuts:** do not turn “Aryan” into a biological race (the term is linguistic; Bryant 2001), assume uninterrupted Indus-to-Purāṇic deity identities (the “Proto-Śiva” seal reading is an iconological assessment, not a consensus identification — Srinivasan 1975–76, 1984), or use Jungian god-job archetypes as evidence of historical transmission (the archetypal reading is a twentieth-century psychological project — Jung 1932/1996). A proposed continuity needs its own sources.
- **Coverage gaps:** regional weaving and dye histories, everyday women's naming and dress, non-elite practice, local oral versions, and the practical enforcement of dress prescriptions require specialist/local evidence. Later ethnography and contemporary religious practice are valuable but cannot silently fill an ancient gap.
- **Stop rule:** when a claim needs a precise manuscript reading, textile reconstruction, local ritual, or date not established here, mark it `(to-verify)` and name the needed evidence. Do not turn a plausible coupled design into a historical assertion or a world-bible fact.
