## Evidence key and scope

- **A — material/linguistic attestation:** an object, analytical result, or cited manuscript form; attestation does not automatically establish its social interpretation.
- **C — colonial testimony:** a report mediated by conquest, evangelization, translation, informants, and the writer's genre; not an unfiltered Indigenous account.
- **R — reconstruction/debate:** a modern explanatory model, including internally coherent models that remain underdetermined historically.
- **F — fictional transfer:** a design operation proposed here, never evidence that the Muisca themselves articulated that operation.
- These are evidence types, not a numerical ranking: archaeology can establish an alloy but not recover a vanished prayer; a missionary dictionary can preserve a word while distorting its category.
- Geographical base: the eastern Colombian Andean highlands, especially present-day Cundinamarca and Boyacá; do not generalize this entry to every Chibchan-speaking population.
- Temporal base: chiefly late pre-contact archaeology and colonial documents. Myth narratives, language documentation, and excavated objects often belong to different centuries.
- Thin coverage: original ritual utterances, everyday personal-name formation, regional speech variation, precise garment arrangements, and contemporary community-specific practice; unsupported particulars remain `(to-verify)`.

## Language family, documentation, and writing

| System | Reference knowledge | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy | Muisca/Chibcha belongs to the Chibchan family [A04] | Chibchan is a linguistic family, not a synonym for a single religion or archaeological style |
| Names of language | Muisca, Mosca, and Chibcha occur in documentary/scholarly usage; Muysc cubun is used in current language scholarship | Preserve the source's spelling and distinguish language label, people, and modern project name |
| Grammatical base | Affixal morphology and substantially head-final organization [A04; L19] | Build a grammar, not just a Spanish word order with substituted nouns |
| Written record | Colonial grammars, vocabularies, catechetical and confession texts use adapted Latin orthographies [L19; G87] | Record which manuscript and editorial normalization a form comes from |
| Pre-contact notation | A deciphered pre-contact glottographic Muisca script is not established by these sources; numerical/iconographic proposals remain debated [I09, §§3.3.3, 5.3] | An image, tally, or textile pattern is not automatically phonetic writing |

- Missionary “general language” documentation is not proof that every chiefdom used identical speech; regional differences and documentary selection must remain visible.
- Do not supply a confident reconstructed pronunciation from Spanish spelling alone; competing reconstructions and grapheme conventions need their own apparatus [A04; DG].
- The cessation of historical language transmission is not the disappearance of Muisca people. Current reclamation and community identity require present-day sources, not extrapolation from chronicles.
- **F:** maintain parallel fields for `source_form`, `normalized_form`, `gloss`, `genre`, `date`, and `certainty`; do not silently normalize historical variation into fictional “ancient spelling.”

## Mythic core — narrative, place, and authority

| Figure or domain | Received narrative / association | Calibration and usable coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Bachué | Emerges from Lake Iguaque with a boy; their descendants populate the world; the pair eventually return as serpents [I09, §2.4] | **C:** origin, generation, water, and transformation are linked; not evidence for every lake having the same cult |
| Bochica | Civilizing figure associated with instruction in skills, including weaving; opens the Tequendama outlet in the flood narrative [I09, §2.4] | **C:** craft instruction and landscape formation can authorize an order of life; do not equate him automatically with a Christian saint |
| Sun and Moon | Solar and lunar beings/phenomena occur in the colonial religious and calendrical accounts [I09, §§2.4, 3.3] | **C/R:** Sun–Moon importance does not prove a uniform solar/lunar political constitution |
| Ancestors and ritual places | Accounts link distinguished dead, ceremonial houses, caves, and custodial specialists [I09, §§2.2, 2.4] | **C:** authority can be maintained through ancestral bodies and places, not just a roster of named gods |
| Lakes, caves, and offerings | Votive deposition is supported by objects and accounts; the Pasca assemblage belongs to a cave context [M21] | **A/C:** destination matters; do not reduce all sacred geography to Lake Guatavita |

- These are transmitted narrative complexes, not a recovered canonical scripture. Do not flatten variant figures into an “Olympian” list with fixed departmental portfolios.
- A flood story recorded by a Christian chronicler needs transmission analysis; resemblance to Genesis alone proves neither direct borrowing nor complete independence.
- **F:** distinguish an origin narrative, its local place claim, its officiants, and later retellings. Let weaving instruction, sacred geography, and authority share a history without assuming identical myths in every town.

## El Dorado — a ritual statement, not a city of bullion

- **C:** Rodríguez Freyle's version, reported through [M21], describes an heir undergoing preparation, a naked body coated with a sticky substance and gold dust, a raft carrying companions and offerings, deposition into the lake, and return to shore as recognized chief.
- Important correction to the shorthand **“zipa gilding”**: the particular informant/story is associated with the cacique of **Guatavita**; identifying every participant with the Bogotá zipa is not warranted [M21, opening discussion].
- Earlier chroniclers' versions are not identical: [M21] notes that some describe offering rather than an explicit coronation. Do not merge all details into one supposedly eyewitness script.
- Treat an obligatory dive, a fixed annual repetition, exact dust quantities, and precise ritual words as separate claims requiring their own passages; none appear in the account summarized here ([M21], opening discussion, re-inspected 2026-10-17).
- The Spanish expression *El Dorado* points to a gilded person in this narrative complex; the shift toward a wealthy location belongs to the legend's expansion [M21]. It is not an attested Muisca city-name.
- **A:** the famous raft was found near Pasca in a cave offering assemblage, not recovered from Lake Guatavita. A charcoal sample associated with its mold yielded 1295–1410 CE; that range is not a date for Freyle's narrated ceremony [M21].
- [M21] explicitly critiques the automatic raft = Guatavita coronation equation. Possible political circumstances around the offering are hypotheses, not the identification of a particular historical event.

| Analytical step | What the reference supports | Fictional design question, not a historical translation |
|---|---|---|
| Material acquisition | Goldworking relied on exchanges connecting highland products and other regions [I09, §2.3; M21] | Who can obtain the substance, and whose labor makes the ritual possible? |
| Authorized body | A differentiated ritual participant bears gold in a colonial narrative [M21] | What changes when a body temporarily becomes the medium of an office? |
| Transfer | Objects are offered rather than simply retained for display [M21] | Who gives, who receives, and what obligation is acknowledged? |
| Place | Water and cave deposition are not interchangeable contexts [M21] | Which destination makes the act appropriate? |
| Reception | Freyle's version connects the act with recognition of a successor [M21] | What constitutes success: witnesses, specialist ratification, or a subsequent sign? |

- **F — ritual speech-act analogy:** treat the sequence as a socially consequential statement made through bodies, materials, and placement. This is a craft lens, not a claim that its original spoken formula or “grammar of gold” has been deciphered.
- Gold can be valuable wealth and a ritual medium simultaneously; “not merely bullion” does not mean “without economic value.”

## Tunjo vocabulary — separate lexeme, artifact, and imposed category

| Form / term | Attestation or analytical meaning | Do not silently infer |
|---|---|---|
| `nyia`, with documented spelling variants | Gold: Ms. II/2923, fol. 31v; money: Ms. 158 vocabulary, fol. 59r [DG, “nyia”] | The colonial money gloss does not prove a timeless pre-contact coinage system |
| `chunso` | Colonial gloss “ídolo”: Ms. 158 vocabulary, fol. 124r; a gold-to-image example occurs at fol. 80v [DG, “chunso”] | The Christian category “idol” is not a neutral native definition of every object |
| *tunjo* | Spanish/archaeological category for figures discussed as offerings; [M21] distinguishes votive figures from ornaments | Every metal figure was wearable; every figure represented a named god; every “idol” was metal |
| *ofrenda* / votive offering | An offering/action and its material vehicle; archaeological interpretation uses context [M21] | A universal object function recoverable from shape alone |
| *ofrendatario* / offering vessel | Spanish archaeological vocabulary for a container in an offering context [M21, Pasca assemblage] | An independently verified Muisca word for that container |
| *tumbaga* | Modern comparative metalworking term for gold–copper alloys, which can also contain silver [M12; M21] | A native Muisca alloy-name, uniform composition, or “fake gold” |
| `chyquy`; Spanish *jeque/xeque* in accounts | “Sacerdote”: Ms. 158 vocabulary, fol. 112r; [DG] notes application to both Indigenous and Catholic priests | One unchanged office or theology across the conquest |
| `psihipqua`, with variants | Lord/chief and related colonial glosses; [DG, “psihipqua”] | An exact equivalent of European hereditary king or a personal surname |

- The dictionary's manuscript evidence is the anchor; suggested etymologies, cognates, and modern phonetic reconstructions are separate propositions.
- **A/R:** offering-specific alloy choices are supported by [M12]; an exact metal color → deity → rank code remains `(to-verify)` unless a particular context supplies it.
- **Object biography / chaîne opératoire:** distinguish commission → material acquisition → manufacture → possible presentation → deposition → later recovery. [M21] warns that public display was not necessarily integral to offering.
- **F:** build a relational offering lexicon: maker, commissioner, officiant, represented person, material, requested favor, recipient/place, and permitted handling. These are design slots, not invented Muisca vocabulary.

## Political dualism — zipa and zaque without an invented binary state

| Conventional pole | Reference shorthand | Necessary correction |
|---|---|---|
| Zipa | Southern power associated with Bogotá and the Funza area in received accounts [I09, §2.2] | Historical Bogotá is not simply the modern capital projected backward |
| Zaque | Northern power associated with Hunza/Tunja in the conventional model [I09, §2.2] | A paired label does not establish equal offices within one constitution |
| Other chiefs and centers | Guatavita, Guasca, Pasca, Ubaque, and others appear in competing accounts and alliances [M21] | Their dependencies, independence, and relative power cannot be fixed from one tidy map |
| Specialists / ritual authority | Jeques and chiefs both participate in accounts of offering and succession [M21] | Do not impose a modern church/state separation or assume all power was one office |

- “Muisca Confederation” is convenient modern shorthand, not proof of a formally chartered, consistently unified federation.
- [M21] stresses contradictory chronicles and competition for prestige. Preserve contested relationships instead of treating a chronicle's king/subject vocabulary as transparent political measurement.
- The **degree and trajectory of centralization** need regional archaeological and documentary evidence; neither “empire” nor “egalitarian villages” is an adequate default.
- Matrilineal succession/descent is discussed in [I09, §2.2]; it does not establish universal female sovereignty, identical rules in every chiefdom, or an absence of hierarchy.
- **F:** use a relationship graph with distinct edges for tribute, military alliance, ritual precedence, marriage, and contested claims. A two-pole narrative can coexist with a multipolar political landscape.

## Era staging — retain change without turning it into disappearance

| Stage | Working chronology and evidence | Language–myth–costume staging |
|---|---|---|
| Herrera background | Approximately 800 BCE–800 CE in [M21]'s contextual discussion; regional chronologies vary | Do not back-project late chief titles or a fully developed tunjo system into every earlier deposit |
| Early Muisca | Approximately 800–1200 CE [M21] | Stage emerging/increasing integration through settlement and production evidence, not a single legendary founding year |
| Late Muisca | Approximately 1200–1600 CE as an archaeological period [M21] | Elaborate regional hierarchy and ritual competition; do not make every prestige object royal regalia |
| Conquest / early colonial | Spanish incursions from 1537; political and religious restructuring [I09; M21] | Indigenous offices, missionary spellings, translated categories, and coercion overlap rather than replacing one another instantly |
| Later colonial | Votive practices continue into colonial contexts, with meanings potentially changing [M21] | A late object is not proof of unchanged ritual; a late dictionary gloss may include colonial money or Christian offices |
| Modern interpretation and reclamation | Museum display, national symbolism, scholarship, and living claims are distinct evidentiary settings [M21; DG] | Do not treat museum presentation as pre-contact staging or language reclamation as a population “returning from extinction” |

- The Late Muisca archaeological boundary near 1600 and the conquest beginning in 1537 answer different questions; they need not coincide.
- [M21] discusses differing radiocarbon ranges for the raft and associated litter and a possible old-wood effect: an assemblage is not automatically one precisely dated manufacturing event.
- **F:** assign each word, garment, office, and myth version an attestation horizon. A colonial-era label can appear in a retrospective account of an older practice without being that practice's original name.

## Costume and material signature — textiles first, not gold armor

| Layer | Evidence base | Coupled design use / caution |
|---|---|---|
| Cotton *mantas* | Blanket-like cotton textiles served as clothing; painted textiles were valued, but surviving fabrics are scarce [I09, §2.3] | Track cloth production, exchange value, and visible differentiation; *manta* is Spanish description, not a recovered native cut-name |
| Exchange ecology | Salt, emeralds, textiles, cotton supplies, and gold belong to interregional production/exchange networks [I09, §2.3; M21] | High-altitude clothing need not imply local sheep or pre-contact woolen tailoring |
| Adornment | The raft depicts differentiated headdresses, a conspicuous nose ornament, ear ornaments, and other dress signals [M21, iconography] | An image offers evidence for represented distinctions, not a complete everyday wardrobe |
| Ritual body | The gilded body is described as naked before coating in Freyle's narrative as summarized by [M21] | Ordinary clothing and exceptional ceremonial exposure can have different social meanings |
| Votive representation | The raft's principal figure is larger and more elaborately adorned than surrounding figures [M21] | Scale, posture, and arrangement can signal rank; metal representation is not necessarily a literal garment material |
| Making and depositing | [M21] treats manufacture and material selection as meaningful components of offering | Do not turn sacred workshop operations into merely decorative costume production |

- Avoid the “everyone in gold” shortcut: a gold image may depict fabric, feathers, bodies, ornaments, and offices rather than a person dressed entirely in metal.
- Exact wrapping, fastening, gender distribution, motif placement, and sumptuary restrictions must be checked against period-specific evidence `(to-verify)`; the current entry cannot supply a universal costume pattern.
- **F:** separate ordinary dress, exchange cloth, office insignia, funerary treatment, and one-time ritual body transformation; let shared materials acquire different meanings in each setting.

## Calendar — the acrotom debate is part of the reference

| Proposition | Attribution / status | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| Number words and counting | Colonial grammatical/lexical evidence, including Lugo; analyzed in [I09, §3.3.2] | Numerical vocabulary is stronger evidence than a complete festival schedule inferred from it |
| Lunar and seasonal reckoning | Accounts and reconstruction in [I09, §§3.3.4–3.3.10] | Keep observable cycles separate from disputed historical units and algorithms |
| 20-month *zocam* cycle | Duquesne's model as analyzed in [I09, §3.3.8] | Attribute to this model; a gloss “year” is not automatically a Gregorian-sized year |
| 37-lunar-month *acrotom* cycle | Duquesne, reconstructed by [I09, §3.3.9], as 12 + 12 + 13 months | **R:** do not present as a securely recovered nationwide liturgical timetable |
| “Acrotom century” | Twenty 37-month cycles, approximately sixty solar years in the model [I09, §3.3.12] | Arithmetic consistency is not independent evidence of historical adoption |
| Written signs / Choachí stone | Numerical/calendrical readings proposed in [I09, §§3.3.3, 5.2–5.3] | A proposed calculator is not a deciphered script or a securely identified calendar machine |
| Jupiter–Saturn correlations | [I09, ch. 4] argues links with recorded ceremonies | **R:** astronomical coincidence and a selected historical event need independent cultural corroboration |

- **Source ladder:** Duquesne's late-eighteenth-century account → Humboldt/other dissemination → Restrepo's 1892 rejection → Izquierdo Peña's reconsideration. Repetition down this chain is not multiple independent witnesses.
- Restrepo's objection cannot be adopted wholesale: [I09, §3.2] shows assumptions that colonial communities could retain no meaningful ancestral knowledge. Rejecting that prejudice does not, by itself, prove Duquesne's complete model.
- Izquierdo Peña explicitly cautions that his reconstruction of **Duquesne's interpretation** is not a definitive calendar of the pre-contact Muisca [I09, Part II introduction]. Preserve that qualification even when borrowing the arithmetic.
- *Acrotom/acrótomo* is Duquesne's own designation: [I09, §3.3.6] states that the Muiscas left the 37-moon year unnamed and that Duquesne gave it this name. Do not manufacture a Muysca etymology for it.
- **F:** choose independently whether a fictional culture has agricultural, market, observational, and specialist ritual cycles. If they differ, specify who reconciles them, how intercalation is authorized, and what dissenting towns do.
- Test a fictional calendar over many cycles for seasonal drift; a functioning simulation validates the invented arithmetic, not the historical Muisca reconstruction.

## Naming practices — keep person, place, title, and gloss apart

- `psihipqua` belongs to a documented authority vocabulary [DG]; zipa/zaque belong to the political terminology transmitted in accounts. None is automatically a hereditary family surname.
- Tisquesusa and Nemequene occur as ruler designations in the chronicle tradition discussed by [I09, ch. 4]; their segmented meanings and suitability as productive name templates remain `(to-verify)`.
- [I09, §2.2] notes the association of a ruler's designation and the name applied to his realm. A Spanish source's “Bogotá” may therefore require identifying whether it means a person, place, or polity in that passage.
- Guatavita and Iguaque can be recorded as places linked to particular narratives; their literal etymologies must not be invented from superficial modern word resemblance `(to-verify)`.
- [M21] identifies Freyle's informant as **don Juan, cacique of Guatavita**: Christian name, Spanish honorific, Indigenous office, and territorial designation coexist in a colonial naming record.
- Do not infer a complete native birth-name system, clan inventory, or gendered suffix paradigm from a short roster of prominent men.
- **F:** store `person_identifier`, `recorded_designation`, `office`, `territory`, `language_of_record`, `date`, and `translation_status` separately. A generated conlang name needs that conlang's rules, not decorative borrowing of a real sacred name.

## What a worldbuilder can take

1. **An offering system, not a treasure aesthetic:** connect procurement, skilled making, authorized handling, destination, and desired response; allow offerings whose public display is uncertain or unnecessary.
2. **A political ecology of words and insignia:** distinguish rival centers and overlapping jurisdictions; let an office's recognition, body treatment, and vocabulary reinforce one another without a single empire.
3. **Material polysemy:** one substance can support wealth, ritual relations, and translated colonial meanings; one word such as “priest” can span institutions without proving theological identity.
4. **Periodized dress and language:** stage cotton cloth, exceptional gilding, documented spellings, borrowed categories, and changing ritual practice independently before coupling them.
5. **Competing calendars:** borrow the design problem of reconciling cycles, not an unqualified claim that a debated 37-month scheme has been recovered in full.
6. **An auditable handoff:** send lore-shokunin alternatives with `reference`, `era`, `evidence_type`, `interpretation`, `fictional_transformation`, and `open_questions`; lore owns adoption, naming approval, and canon consistency.

## Provenance cautions — stop before filling the gaps

- Colonial “idol,” “king,” “priest,” “coronation,” and “drunkenness” are acts of classification. Quote or paraphrase them with a speaker/source, not as transparent native categories.
- A chronicle's report is not automatically eyewitness testimony; distinguish date of narrated event, date of composition, publication, and modern edition.
- The Pasca raft is strong evidence for an elaborate offering assemblage; its exact commissioner, historical occasion, and equation with Guatavita remain interpretation [M21].
- Surviving textiles, ritual speech, and ordinary naming are too thinly covered here for confident reconstruction; false precision would be worse than an explicitly marked gap.
- Related Chibchan traditions may support comparison, never automatic backfilling of Muisca prayer, mythology, costume, or phonology.
- Living communities are not bound by the scholarly reconstruction in this entry. Community-specific contemporary claims require their own voices and sources.
- No pantheon roster, calendar, costume rule, or invented culture in this file is canon; unresolved claims stay `(to-verify)` rather than becoming facts through repetition.
