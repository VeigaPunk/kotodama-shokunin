## 1. Evidence model: separate the layers before coupling them

- **Minoan** and **Mycenaean** are modern archaeological categories, not interchangeable ethnic self-names or a single successive national history.
- Minoan material culture is centered on Crete; Mycenaean material culture develops on the mainland and also occurs on Crete and elsewhere. Interaction, appropriation, migration, and local continuity complicate a neat island/mainland binary (Companion; Judson 2024).
- **Attested:** excavated objects and plans; readable Linear B wording, subject to damage and lexical uncertainty.
- **Inferred:** function, social organization, a depicted figure’s divine status, or the relation between a motif and a ritual; confidence depends on context and independent evidence.
- **Later reception:** Homeric epic, Classical cult, and Greek/Roman stories about Minos, Ariadne, Theseus, and Dionysos. These are evidence for their own transmission periods, not Bronze Age chronicles.
- **Design proposal:** a worldbuilding transformation of the evidence. Mark it as an option rather than claiming that the historical culture practiced it.
- Chronicle evidence is especially scarce: surviving Linear B archives chiefly concern administration, not continuous histories or myth narratives; Linear A cannot presently supply a readable Minoan royal or religious literature.
- Thin knowledge is real here: Minoan divine names, everyday spoken registers, ordinary dress distributions, and indigenous explanations of symbols remain poorly recoverable. Do not fill these gaps with confident mythology.

## 2. Era staging: overlapping regional sequences, not one civilization clock

Dates below are approximate BCE scaffolding from the Companion; relative ceramic phases are often safer than exact calendar boundaries.

| Stage / region | Working date range | Linguistic and institutional texture | Material/dress staging lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prepalatial Crete | roughly 3000–1900 | Regional communities precede the mature palace systems; do not project later archives backward | Regional burial, seal, and craft traditions; avoid giving every community the later Knossian image kit |
| Protopalatial Crete | roughly 1900–1700, much of MM I–II | First palatial centers; Cretan Hieroglyphic and Linear A participate in developing administration | Storage, seals, specialized production, and polychrome Kamares pottery can distinguish this stage |
| Neopalatial Crete | roughly 1700–1450, MM III–LM I | Expanded court-centered complexes and Linear A administration; interregional exchange | Elaborate fresco programs, patterned elite dress imagery, LM IB Marine Style pottery |
| Early Mycenaean mainland | roughly 1700–1450, LH I–II | Mainland elite consolidation overlaps Neopalatial Crete rather than simply following it | Shaft-grave wealth, weapons, goldwork, and selective appropriation of Cretan visual forms |
| Later Bronze Age Crete | after roughly 1450; LM II onward | Greek-language Linear B administration at Knossos and later other contexts; political transition is not a fully documented invasion narrative | Continuities and changes coexist; date individual buildings, graves, and paintings |
| Mature mainland palatial systems | roughly 1400–1200, LH IIIA–IIIB | Regional palace administrations, Linear B accounts, differentiated offices and production | Megaron complexes, elite frescoes, textile production, fortifications at some centers |
| Postpalatial Aegean | roughly 1200–1050, notably LH IIIC | Many palaces and their archive practices disappear; populations and craft traditions do not all disappear | Reused settlements, regional styles, changed exchange networks; no compulsory universal “dark costume” |

- **EM/MM/LM** = Early/Middle/Late Minoan; **EH/MH/LH** = Early/Middle/Late Helladic; Cycladic sequences require their own synchronization.
- The Thera eruption belongs within LM IA; its absolute date and cross-regional synchronization are debated. Do not equate it with every later Cretan destruction or the mainland collapse around 1200.
- Knossos has difficult destruction/archive contexts; avoid a single exact “date of all Knossos tablets.” Older teaching pages are not sufficient to settle this.
- Palace collapse has multiple proposed causes and regionally different trajectories. A single “Dorian invasion” or “Sea Peoples did everything” explanation is not an adequate default (Companion).
- **Apply:** stage script use, office meanings, motif placement, and garment access separately; an imported style can arrive before a political institution or survive its destruction.

## 3. Language family, writing, and the decipherment contrast

| Dimension | Linear A / Minoan contexts | Linear B / Mycenaean contexts |
|---|---|---|
| Language affiliation | Underlying language or languages not securely assigned to a family; “Minoan” is a conventional label | Mycenaean Greek: early Greek, within Indo-European |
| Reading status | Undeciphered as a language, despite interpretable numbers, accounting structures, and some commodity signs | Deciphered; individual words, names, restorations, and interpretations can still be uncertain |
| Script relationship | Shares many signs with Linear B; conventional B-based transliterations are useful analytical notation | Adaptation within the Aegean linear-script tradition, not the Greek alphabet |
| Typical evidence | Administrative clay documents and inscriptions on objects, including ritual stone vessels | Administrative tablets and painted inscriptions on containers, especially transport stirrup jars |
| Safe inference | Accounts can expose quantities and repeated structures without yielding a complete translation | Accounts reveal Greek morphology, names, commodities, offices, and cult recipients |
| Unsafe inference | Shared signs prove Greek, Semitic, Anatolian, or any other proposed language identification | Every CV spelling transparently reproduces the spoken word, or all vocabulary has been understood |

- **Salgarella’s palaeographic approach:** compare sign variants, scribal practices, document structures, and contexts; do not reduce the relationship to a tidy alphabet-like replacement chart.
- **Decipherment framework:** Alice Kober’s structural analysis of recurring forms and Michael Ventris’s sign-grid work preceded the Greek identification announced in 1952; Ventris and Chadwick’s 1953 publication supplied linguistic argument. This was cumulative work, not a lucky universal cipher key.
- **Orthographic mismatch:** Linear B mainly writes vowel and consonant-vowel syllables. Greek clusters and final consonants require conventions; many distinctions in speech are not separately represented (VC, pp. 42–48).
- Examples: `ko-no-so` corresponds to Knossos; `pa-i-to` to Phaistos; `wa-na-ka` to *wanax*. Transliteration hyphens divide signs, not necessarily spoken syllables or morphemes.
- Linear B’s main series does not distinguish /r/ from /l/; stop distinctions are also unevenly represented. Do not infer that speakers lacked the contrasts the script fails to distinguish.
- Conversely, applying a Linear B value to a similar Linear A sign is not proof of its exact Minoan pronunciation, much less the word’s meaning.
- The Greek alphabet is a first-millennium adaptation of alphabetic writing, not Linear B’s next evolutionary stage.
- **Apply:** let a language borrow a prestige syllabary that fits it imperfectly; retain conservative spellings in accounts while speech changes. Keep the designer’s underlying pronunciation distinct from the displayed inscription.

## 4. Palace economies: couple the ledger to cloth, offerings, and rank

“Palace economy” is an institutional lens, not a claim that the palace owned every field or controlled every transaction (Companion; VC; updated reference: Killen 2024, chapters VIII–IX).

| Evidence / term | What it supports | Coupled design question | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Magazines, pithoi, sealings | Organized storage and controlled access | Who measures, seals, carries, and authorizes release? | Storage capacity alone does not identify the ruler or a monopoly |
| Sheep, wool, textile, and personnel accounts | Substantial administrative involvement in textile production | Who receives fiber and rations; who supplies elite or ritual cloth? | The archive records selected obligations, not the whole economy |
| Perfumed oil and vessel accounts | Managed manufacture, allocation, and ceremonial consumption | How do craft vocabulary, scent, gift-giving, and cult intersect? | Not every luxury is a temple monopoly |
| `wa-na-ka` / *wanax* | Highest palatial authority in standard interpretation | What title links landholding, personnel, and ceremonial centrality? | Do not automatically make the office a divine priest-king |
| `ra-wa-ke-ta` / *lawagetas* | Another exceptionally important office | Can several forms of authority share one palace? | Etymological “leader of the people” does not settle exact duties |
| `qa-si-re-u` / *basileus* | A local/group leadership designation, not simply the later paramount king | Can a title gain importance after the palace network fails? | Later Greek meaning cannot be read backward unchanged |
| `da-mo` / *damos* | A corporate/community entity in landholding records | What resources remain outside the ruler’s immediate household? | Not Classical democracy |
| Priests, priestesses, dependents, and ration groups | Institutions and people with differentiated economic relationships | Who has land rights, who receives subsistence, who performs skilled work? | Do not translate every dependency as the same legal status |

- **Archive survival:** clay records often survive because destructive fires hardened them; preservation selects an administrative moment, not a balanced description of society.
- **Gender calibration:** women’s textile labor and priestesses’ economic roles are evidence for particular positions, not proof either of universal female subordination or of a matriarchal state.
- **Apply:** model a chain such as flock assessment → wool allocation → patterned cloth commission → official issue → festival display. Then add households, local exchange, and unofficial practice outside that chain.
- Do not make a decorative octopus automatically the palace’s official seal, textile classification sign, and supreme deity. Each identification needs its own evidence or an explicit fictional design decision.

## 5. Mythic and pantheon core: names are not complete myth cycles

The most secure textual foothold is Mycenaean offerings to named recipients; Minoan cult is primarily reconstructed through objects, images, and places (Palaima; VC; Rutter 15, 26, with dated interpretations filtered).

| Strand | Secure or reasonably grounded base | Interpretation requiring restraint |
|---|---|---|
| Zeus, Poseidon, Hera, and other later Greek names | Several recognizable divine names occur in Linear B; Poseidon is prominent in Pylos’s religious records | An unchanged Classical Olympian family, hierarchy, and portfolio |
| `po-ti-ni-ja` / *Potnia*, “Mistress” | A divine title occurs with different qualifications | Every Potnia is one universal Mother/Earth Goddess |
| `e-re-u-ti-ja` / Eileithyia | A recognizable childbirth-goddess name in Knossian records | Full continuity of a particular later narrative or ritual at every site |
| Dionysos-name forms, including `di-wo-nu-so` | The name is attested in Linear B; identification of any particular fragment depends on context | An exclusively late foreign arrival, or a fully documented Bronze Age Bacchic religion |
| Female figures, snakes, trees, mountains, and offerings | Recurrent visual/cult associations; some figures plausibly represent deities | Secure Minoan divine names, a single pantheon chart, or a universal fertility theology |
| `ma-te-re te-i-ja`, conventionally “Divine Mother” | A Mycenaean cult designation discussed in the Pylos evidence | Automatic identity with Gaia, Demeter, Rhea, or every Cretan female image |

- **Dionysiac strand:** distinguish the attested name from the later complex of wine, masks, maenads, ecstatic processions, death/rebirth interpretations, and Ariadne traditions. These later associations are not all demonstrated by Bronze Age accounts.
- Wine production, feasting, and a Dionysos-name attestation do not by themselves prove a particular Dionysiac festival, costume, or initiation system.
- **Earth/vegetation strand:** caves, snakes, plant imagery, female figures, and seasonal production permit hypotheses about earth, regeneration, or cultivated fertility; they do not translate themselves into “Earth Mother.”
- Identifying a particular Minoan figurine as Demeter or Gaia is **not established** and is not a recovered Linear A name: the script remains undeciphered and Minoan divine names are not securely readable (the *da-ma-te* = Déméter proposal, [Duhoux 1997], is a contested etymological argument, not a decipherment). Proposed Bronze Age readings of later divine identities need item-level linguistic argument. [Salgarella 2022]
- **Discredited as an evidential shortcut:** prominent female divinities → female political rule → peaceful matriarchy. Each arrow is a separate, unsupported inference; “fertility goddess” is not an explanation for every female image.
- **Apply:** separate recipient-title, local sanctuary, offering obligation, represented attributes, and narrative. A fictional culture may link them, but the linkage must be designed, not falsely borrowed as established history.

## 6. Symbolic system: use motif distributions, not a universal dictionary

| Motif / object | Attested visual or material setting | Craft use | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Double axe | Objects, votives, images, and architectural marks | Link a portable emblem to specific rites or authorized spaces | Precise meanings vary or are uncertain; *labrys* is not a deciphered Linear A gloss |
| “Horns of consecration” | Modeled objects and architectural/image settings | Mark boundaries, display points, or ritual stages | Modern interpretive label; exact ancient semantics remain uncertain |
| Bull imagery and bull-leaping | Multiple media, including frescoes and seals | Couple risk, performance, bodily training, costume, and spectatorship | Neither a literal Minotaur chronicle nor proof of the same ritual at all sites |
| Trees, pillars, and gestures | Seal/ring compositions and cult settings | Design approach, touch, enclosure, and restricted visibility | “Epiphany” is an interpretive framework, not a translated caption |
| Griffin and antithetic animals | Elite and cult-associated compositions | Frame a throne, threshold, or central figure | Not automatically a national coat of arms or named god’s exclusive attribute |
| Octopus and other marine life | Especially LM IB Marine Style pottery; also painted marine environments | Let motif scale and tentacle movement respond to the decorated surface | Maritime association does not establish a decoded theology |
| Spirals, rosettes, bands, and repeating fields | Multiple media, including depictions of patterned cloth | Build a repeat grammar adaptable to borders, fields, and architectural panels | Similar shapes can have different technical and social histories |

- **Contextual sequence:** record site → phase → object/surface → associated activity → motif arrangement → inference. “Minoan octopus means X” skips the decisive steps.
- Do not explain the double axe through the modern etymological slogan “labyrinth = house of the double axe” as settled fact; both linguistic and historical bridges require argument.
- **Apply:** a motif can move from mobile luxury vessel to palace surface to garment border without retaining identical meaning. Track the agent of transfer: workshop, marriage, exchange, patronage, or imitation.

## 7. Costume and material signature: untangle octopus, fresco, and textile

Primary distinction: pottery decoration, wall/floor painting, painted clothing, and actual textile remains are different evidence classes (Woven Threads; Rutter 14).

| Evidence class | Useful reference | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Marine Style vessel painting | Octopus tentacles exploit vessel curvature; marine fillers organize remaining space | That an octopus garment was worn, or that its pattern was woven |
| Marine painted floors | Ayia Triada’s marine scene includes fish and an octopus; dating is debated in the teaching literature | The floor is a copied carpet, or all marine painting belongs to walls |
| Fresco depictions of dress | Patterned borders, repeated fields, fitted upper garments, skirts, and other differentiated costumes | Exact weave structure, fiber, dye recipe, or everyday prevalence |
| Figurines and modeled costume | Three-dimensional relationships among belt, bodice, skirt, and body | A complete tailoring pattern or unambiguous distinction between deity and wearer |
| Linear B textile accounts | Economic categories of wool, cloth, labor, and allocation | A secure one-to-one matching of every recorded cloth term to a painted outfit |
| Surviving fragments, impressions, and tools | Direct technical constraints, supplemented by reconstruction | A complete wardrobe of the population |

- **Elite female image kit:** some Cretan and Aegean images show fitted/open-front upper garments, pronounced waist belts, and long layered or flounced skirts. Do not treat every female figure, region, or era as identically dressed.
- **Male and occupational variation:** loincloth/kilt-like forms, robes, and other garments occur in different contexts; military display and ceremonial performance require separate reference boards.
- Exposed breasts and narrow waists in images are not reliable demographic measurements or proof of sexual emancipation. Restoration, artistic convention, ritual role, and garment reconstruction matter.
- Wool and flax/linen belong in the production model; do not import cotton, silk, or later Classical draped-dress terminology as the default Bronze Age wardrobe (Companion; Woven Threads).
- A painted pattern might represent weaving, embroidery, applied ornament, or another treatment. Select a technique only after checking repeat geometry, available tools, labor, and comparable technical evidence.
- Painted blue/red/white is evidence of representation; an exact dyestuff or rigid color-rank law needs independent support.
- **Octopus-to-textile exercise, explicitly a design proposal:** turn free tentacles into a border repeat, simplify fine suckers to a stitch/weave scale, and choose where seams interrupt the image. The exercise does not claim an excavated octopus robe.
- **Era change exercise:** contrast a costly pictorial field with a later simplified repeated motif, but do not label abstraction “cultural decline” or date it without a specified corpus.

## 8. Megaron and palace architecture terms

The following are analytical vocabulary, often Greek/Latin or modern archaeological labels, not a recovered Minoan architectural glossary (Rutter 12, 20; Companion).

| Term | Working definition | Coupling lever / caution |
|---|---|---|
| **Megaron** | Long hall or hall unit; major mainland palatial examples have an axial porch–vestibule–main-room sequence | Access funnels an audience toward the main chamber; the label also covers less elaborate examples |
| **Porch / aithousa** | Open-front entrance space in conventional descriptions | Transition between court and controlled interior; ancient terminology is not thereby attested on a tablet |
| **Vestibule / prodomos** | Intermediate room before the main chamber | Waiting, screening, and changes in visibility can structure ceremony |
| **Domos / main hall** | Principal enclosed chamber in the analytical sequence | Avoid automatically identifying any hall as a temple |
| **Anta; antae** | Projecting wall ends flanking a porch; columns may stand between them | Describe the masonry/column arrangement, not a social office |
| **Central hearth** | Prominent hearth in major megaron halls; four surrounding columns characterize familiar examples | Heat, smoke, gathering, and prestige; not every “megaron” has the full package |
| **Propylon; propyla** | Monumental gateway unit | Gates and courts create successive access thresholds |
| **Central court** | Major open organizing space in Cretan palatial complexes | Connected wings and circulation differ from a closed axial hall; public use must be argued |
| **Polythyron / pier-and-door partition** | Repeated pier-and-door openings enabling flexible separation | Light, air, and visibility become adjustable resources |
| **Lightwell** | Open vertical space admitting light/air | Couple internal costume and display to real illumination, not generic torchlight |
| **Magazine; pithos, pithoi** | Storage room; large ceramic storage jar(s) | Connect carrying routes, sealings, quantities, and ceremonial redistribution |
| **“Lustral basin”** | Conventionally named sunken stepped space in Minoan architecture | Ritual/purification interpretations are debated; not automatically a bath or drain |

- Pylos, Tiryns, and Mycenae supply important megaron comparisons; variation prevents treating one reconstruction as an Aegean universal.
- Distinguish **palace** as an administrative/elite complex from **citadel** as a fortified site: Late Palatial Pylos need not resemble the fortified stronghold of Tiryns.
- A court-centered Cretan complex versus a mainland megaron is a spatial contrast, not proof of peaceful egalitarians versus inherently warlike Greeks.
- **Apply:** derive room-names, approach formulas, procession clothing, and eligibility to cross thresholds from the circulation plan. Do not copy a Greek technical label and pretend it is a Minoan endonym.

## 9. Naming practices: distinguish person, office, place, and recipient

- Mycenaean archives preserve personal names, office titles, place-names, and divine recipients; a transliterated sign group needs its documentary context before being assigned a category (VC glossary; Killen 2024 reference).
- Greek-understandable names coexist with opaque or probably non-Greek forms. Opacity is not permission to invent a translation or force every name into Greek etymology.
- `a-ki-re-u` is conventionally interpreted as *Akhilleus*; an epic-compatible name does not identify Homer’s Achilles or authenticate the Trojan War narrative.
- Name + occupation, location, or patronymic can distinguish people in particular contexts; do not impose a universal modern given-name/surname system.
- `wa-na-ka` is an office/title, not a safely recovered monarch’s personal name; calling a site the “Palace of Nestor” is not a contemporary identification of its occupant.
- Place continuity such as `ko-no-so` / Knossos and `pa-i-to` / Phaistos can anchor analysis, but does not recover the full etymology or original language of every toponym.
- Ariadne, Minos, and Theseus belong to later Greek narrative evidence; do not present them as a deciphered Minoan royal name list.
- **Apply:** maintain separate layers for local speech, scribal spelling, foreign rendering, office, and later legendary form. Invent an island language’s names as fictional constructions, never as “authentic translations” of Linear A.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Borrow an interface, not an identity:** choose court-based allocation, a megaron audience route, or a conservative syllabary as the starting mechanism; do not transplant “the Minoans” wholesale.
2. **Build the production substrate:** specify sheep/flax, spinning and weaving, storage, dyes or pigments, skilled labor, and exchange. Let these constrain the available prestige clothing.
3. **Specify the archive’s blind spot:** decide which obligations get written, which medium survives, and which worship or household exchanges remain invisible.
4. **Separate deity from institution:** a title like “Mistress” can have local qualifiers and different offering administrators without requiring a single universal goddess.
5. **Give motifs careers:** document a motif’s first medium, technical translation, restricted users, and changed interpretation across eras. Marine pottery → fictional textile is a designed transfer, not historical testimony.
6. **Stage semantic change:** a lesser office may acquire sovereign force after larger institutions collapse; a script may die while speech and names persist.
7. **Test the coupling:** can a scribe record the cloth, can a maker produce it, can a wearer move through the room, and can an observer identify the claimed rank at that distance and light level?
8. **Hand off options:** give lore-shokunin the mechanism, historical reference, evidence level, alternatives, and unresolved questions. Lore chooses whether any fictional proposition becomes canon.

## 11. Provenance cautions and recheck queue

- **Colonial-era framing:** “Europe’s first civilization,” peaceful island paradise, Oriental despotism, and martial mainland race are value-laden narratives, not neutral explanations. Early excavators’ categories and restoration decisions require scrutiny.
- **Restoration bias:** museum-visible frescoes and figurines can combine ancient fragments with extensive modern completion. Check an excavation drawing or conservation record before copying an exact pose, garment cut, or room arrangement.
- **Source age:** Rutter’s pages are valuable descriptive teaching aids, but preserve disputed or obsolete statements, including on Knossian chronology and cult interpretation. Bibliography-update dates do not certify every narrative sentence.
- **Current-reference caution:** Judson (2024) notes that parts of Killen’s new collection were completed much earlier and that invasion/origin statements need calibration. A recent cover date does not eliminate inherited assumptions.
- **Text-level recheck:** before using a divine-name spelling, occupational label, or cloth term as a design keystone, consult the tablet identifier, current edition, context, and damage/restoration marks. This entry is not a fresh epigraphic edition.
- **Unsettled questions:** Minoan language affiliation, exact Minoan pantheon, a specific earth-goddess identification, and a named Bronze Age Dionysiac costume/festival remain unestablished; any stronger assertion outruns the present evidence state [Salgarella 2022], not merely this entry’s coverage.
- **Deliberate gap:** no specific surviving octopus-patterned textile or octopus garment is claimed. A proposed archaeological exemplar must be checked against a catalogued object before being cited as fact.
- **Boundary:** this is a methodological reference packet. It neither writes a fictional world’s history nor licenses canon changes in lore-shokunin’s world bible.
