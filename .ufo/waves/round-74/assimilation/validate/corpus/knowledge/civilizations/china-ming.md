## Scope and evidence discipline
- This is a reference base and generative method, not a fictional polity, pantheon, wardrobe, or approved world-bible entry.
- Unit of comparison: a dated locality, institution, household status, and communicative event; not an undifferentiated “Chinese civilization.”
- Keep four evidence classes separate: prescriptive statute, observed/surviving object, literary representation, and modern reconstruction.
- **Calibration:** administrative terms and the broad rank list are well attested; actual compliance, regional pronunciation, and particular loan itineraries require narrower evidence.
- Modern pinyin below is an identification aid, not a reconstruction of Ming pronunciation; English animal labels are conventional translations, not secure zoological identifications.
- An inline `(to-verify)` is an unresolved historical detail, not permission to silently promote it into a fact.

## Era model: overlapping systems, not a costume slideshow
| Working stage | Institutional/material anchors | Linguistic-cultural coupling to model |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation, 1368–1398 | Hongwu legislation; household registration; 1391 rank-dress prescriptions | Central authority tries to make occupational inheritance, visible status, and record vocabulary agree |
| Early fifteenth century | Yongle court; Beijing becomes principal capital in 1421; Zheng He's voyages, 1405–1433 | A court can sponsor far-reaching multilingual expeditions while regulating private maritime activity |
| Fifteenth–mid-sixteenth centuries | Regional markets and accumulated administrative adaptations; coastwise exchange and prohibitions | Written categories persist while local practice changes; port speech and official classification diverge |
| Later sixteenth–early seventeenth centuries | Silver-linked commerce; Macao and Manila networks; expanding commercial print | Merchant literacy, expensive display, devotional patronage, and mixed literary registers reinforce one another |
| Crisis and succession, 1640s onward | Beijing falls in 1644; Southern Ming resistance continues beyond that date | A dynastic boundary does not instantly replace local speech, cloth stocks, ritual habits, or allegiances |
- Use Brook (1998) for social-economic staging, Huang (1974) for fiscal mechanisms, and Mills (1970) for voyage observations; their temporal scales differ.
- **Do not infer isolation from haijin 海禁.** Maritime prohibitions varied by reign, place, actor, and kind of voyage; tribute, licensed activity, and illicit exchange were not one category.
- **Periodization rule for fiction:** stage changes separately in taxation, shipping rights, cloth supply, printing, and court symbolism; record which population experiences each change first.

## Language family, writing, and speech ecology
- Sinitic languages belong to Sino-Tibetan; Mandarin, Wu, Min, Yue, and other regional forms cannot be collapsed into one mutually intelligible spoken language (Norman 1988).
- The state was multilingual beyond Sinitic: frontier, diplomatic, religious, and trading situations involved additional languages; do not model all non-court speech as defective Mandarin.
- Guanhua 官話 is an official/interregional speech practice with historical variation, not modern Putonghua projected backward; exact late-Ming sound values need Coblin's evidence, not pinyin.
- Written Chinese is morphosyllabic, not a collection of pictures for universal ideas. Shared characters can bridge readers with different pronunciations without eliminating language differences.
- Literary Chinese, administrative documentary formulas, written vernacular, and local oral performance coexist; “diglossia” is a starting comparison, not an exhaustive two-box account.
| Communicative layer | Resource and audience | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Classical quotation/examination prose | Educated readers; authority through inherited texts | Allusion and parallel structure can claim cultivated standing without a rank badge |
| Official document | Named offices, liabilities, dates, register categories | A clerk may command documentary power without holding the authority of a high civil official |
| Guanhua interaction | Officials, travelers, interpreters; uneven proficiency | Accommodation, translation, and accent can disclose training or travel, not moral character |
| Regional household/market speech | Local kin, clients, craft workers | Kinship address, occupational vocabulary, and local idiom make a different social map |
| Port mediation | Brokers, merchants, missionaries, ship crews | One person can shift between trade jargon, household speech, and a written petition |
- **Application:** give a character separate competencies for speaking, reading, composing documents, recognizing allusions, and reading costume; do not make “literate” a universal proficiency switch.

## Mythic and pantheon core: jurisdiction before a master family tree
| Religious field | Attested base or useful distinction | Coupling to language and material display |
|---|---|---|
| Heaven, imperial ancestors, state sacrifice | Political legitimacy and calendrical ritual are not identical to popular stories about a celestial court | Who may address a power, with which title, offering, and garment, matters as much as a deity list |
| Household/lineage ancestors | Genealogical belonging, memorial practice, and property relationships | Ancestral names, generation markers, tablets, and ritual roles organize social memory |
| Buddhist institutions and Guanyin devotion | Buddhist terminology and salvation practices form an inherited layer, not a Ming novelty | Monastic names, translated scriptures, images, and lay patronage can cross class boundaries |
| Daoist institutions and local ritual specialists | Institutional affiliation and local divine efficacy need not coincide | Different ritual vocabularies and garment permissions; no single “wizard uniform” |
| City/territorial protectors | Jurisdiction offers a useful comparison with human offices | A deity's title and shrine locality can constrain where a petition is thought effective |
| Maritime protectors, including Mazu/Tianfei | Seafarer devotion and imperial recognition overlap; neither erases the other | Shipboard petitions and official honorifics can name the same protector differently |
| Wealth and dangerous efficacy | Von Glahn's Wutong study exposes morally ambivalent powers | Merchant offerings need not prove a god is virtuous; prosperity stories can also threaten households |
- These are intersecting fields, not seven exclusive denominations; “Three Teachings” is a historical classificatory idiom, not proof that every practitioner harmonized everything.
- Mazu's title history has dates to keep: **Tianfei 天妃** was an official title by 1281 (longer forms granted later, including 1409), so a Ming reference may use it; **Tianhou 天后** belongs to the Qing-era conferral (1683/84; sources differ by a year) and must not be silently backdated. For the title-history distinction and its bibliography, see [Mazu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mazu); actual local usage of either title in a given locality remains `(to-verify)`.
- Monastic dress is internally differentiated: *Ming shi* 67 gives separate prescriptions for Chan, teaching, and ritual Buddhist clergy, and distinguishes Daoist personnel. Do not derive every religious robe from secular official dress.
- **Thin coverage:** this entry does not reconstruct a specific village's liturgy, women's ritual speech, minority traditions, or the precise local iconography of each god; obtain dated local evidence before specifying these.

## Symbolic grammar: authority is an interpreted relation
- Buzi 補子 links a recognized image to an authorized rank and institution; it is not merely a personal “spirit animal.”
- Wen 文 / wu 武 offers an explicit civil/military contrast: Cammann (1953) reports Qiu Jun's association of birds with literary elegance and beasts with martial courage; treat this as a historical explanation, not biological destiny.
- The xiezhi 獬豸 of censorial/legal authority couples a fabulous creature's discrimination of right and wrong to institutional judgment; it is not simply another military beast.
- A crane can signify official grade in one garment context and longevity elsewhere; image identity does not fix meaning independently of carrier, occasion, date, and wearer.
- Auspicious rebuses depend on actual linguistic resemblance: do not import a modern Mandarin homophone into a different regional language without checking its pronunciation.
- Colors are rule-bound but historically variable: *Ming shi* 67 records several changing restrictions, permissions, and violations; “red always means X” cannot substitute for a dated dress regulation.
- Imperial gifts create exceptions; imitation creates disputes; an image may be authorized, inherited, borrowed, theatrical, or illicit. Encode those alternatives before declaring its meaning.

## Buzi dress code: read the ensemble and the date
- The 1391 prescriptions are a major anchor, not the invention of all bird/beast ornament; earlier precedents do not equal an unchanged Ming rank code.
- For officials' ordinary official attire, *Ming shi* 67 names the black gauze hat, round-collared garment, and belt; distinguish **changfu 常服** from **chaofu 朝服**, **gongfu 公服**, and sacrificial dress.
- A badge cannot by itself identify the whole ceremony: hats, belt materials, garment cut, and the occasion also encode permissions.
- The following is the **Ming list as transmitted in *Ming shi* 67**, not a claim that each object followed it or that it is the later Qing list.
| Grade | Civil image | Military image | Reading warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Crane, 仙鶴 | Lion, 獅子 | Military 1 and 2 share an image; badge alone is insufficient |
| 2 | Golden pheasant, 錦雞 | Lion | Do not give Ming grade 1 a Qing-style qilin by default |
| 3 | Peacock, 孔雀 | Tiger/leopard, 虎豹, grouped with grade 4 | A modern nine-animal chart can conceal grouped privileges |
| 4 | Wild goose, 雲雁 | Tiger/leopard, grouped with grade 3 | Later summaries differentiate these; specify the source/date |
| 5 | Silver pheasant, 白鷳 | Bear, 熊羆 | White bird does not automatically mean crane |
| 6 | Egret, 鷺鷥 | Biao, 彪, conventionally “panther” | Historical beast categories need not match modern taxonomy |
| 7 | Xichi, 鸂鶒, conventionally “mandarin duck” | Biao, as grade 6 | Preserve the Chinese label if precise species matters |
| 8 | Oriole, 黃鸝 | Rhinoceros, 犀牛 | Qing civil grade 8 quail is an anachronistic substitution |
| 9 | Quail, 鵪鶉 | “Sea horse,” 海馬 | Do not automatically draw the modern seahorse fish |
| Unranked miscellaneous offices | Lianque, 練鵲, conventionally “paradise flycatcher” | Not supplied by this civil category | Unranked is not another numbered civil grade |
- Nobles and imperial sons-in-law have separate privileges involving **qilin 麒麟** and **baize 白澤**; censorial personnel use **xiezhi**, not the bird list mechanically.
- **Chronological calibration:** Cammann's article (inspected) reports rank-doubling in the early Ming and a 1527 law that fixed a single bird per civil rank (eighth rank rhinoceros only, ninth rank Sea horse) after repeated usurpation; *Ming shi* places a fixed civil list under 1391. Preserve this difference in presentation: a collector's synthesis and a retrospective history are not an enforcement log; an exact enforcement chronology still requires contemporary statute/edition comparison.
- **Construction:** Ming examples can have large chest/back designs, paired birds, tapestry weaving, brocade, or embroidery; “square” is a collecting term, not a universal measurement or construction method (Cammann 1953).
- Do not automatically import a Qing center-opening surcoat, necessarily split front badge, standardized sun-disc composition, hat-button hierarchy, court beads, or queue into a Ming ensemble.
- **Gender/status caution:** portraits show women with rank imagery, but *Ming shi* 67 separately prescribes titled women's **xiapei 霞帔**, headgear, and rank-grouped bird decoration. “Every wife wore an identical male uniform” erases garment and occasion differences.
- *Ming shi* records special gifts and repeated bans, including restrictions on makers of prohibited designs: legal display is a production-and-patronage system, not just a wearer checklist.
- **Application:** store `[date, office, grade, personal grant, garment category, household title, maker, source type]`; only then choose imagery. A mismatch is a research question before it becomes a plot clue.

## Costume and material signature beyond the badge
| Surface | Ming reference anchor | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|
| Official ensemble | Round collar, hat, belt, grade decoration; separate audience/sacrificial categories | One red robe serves all ranks and ceremonies |
| Titled women's attire | Large-sleeved garments, xiapei, differentiated ornaments in prescriptive sources | All women, or all officials' female relatives, had identical entitlements |
| Textile workmanship | Silk tapestry, figured weaving, embroidery, metallic thread | A woven image is an embroidered patch; every shiny element is solid gold |
| Non-elite clothing | Status-specific cloth and ornament restrictions recorded alongside repeated prohibitions | A law proves perfect obedience or a surviving elite silk robe represents farm work |
| Military/work clothing | *Ming shi* includes padded narrow-sleeved military garments and riding-specific provisions | A civil official's ceremonial silhouette is universal everyday clothing |
- Couple supply to display: transport, workshops, household resources, and repair determine who can obtain a legally correct image; Brook (1998) helps frame status competition, not a nationwide wardrobe census.
- **Application:** give prestigious textiles a labor chain and an audience. A copied elite motif may change social meaning without changing the raw material.

## Maritime trade loan layers: route, domain, and chronology
- A foreign cargo, a foreign name in a travel account, and an integrated loanword are three different observations; do not count them as equivalent evidence of borrowing.
| Layer | Evidence/example | Coupling use and confidence limit |
|---|---|---|
| Inherited transregional religious vocabulary | Buddhist terms long predate Ming seafaring; Norman (1988) supplies linguistic context | Do not date an old sacred loan to the newest trade route; a particular donor/intermediary still needs etymological evidence |
| Early-Ming voyage transcriptions | Ma Huan records ports and peoples across Southeast Asia and the Indian Ocean | A written exonym establishes documentary encounter, not adoption throughout ordinary speech |
| Regional merchant speech | Southern Min/Hokkien is directly documented in Manila missionary sources analyzed by Klöter (2011) | Model a port vernacular distinct from Guanhua; one seventeenth-century community does not stand for all Fujian speakers |
| East/Southeast Asian trade mediation | Different brokers, diasporas, and receiving languages carry different commodities and terms | Modern Hokkien loans in Malay/Tagalog are not automatically dated Ming; each proposed example needs an early witness `(to-verify)` |
| European-contact layer | Portuguese **chá** and Dutch **thee** belong to different transmission chains for tea | Chinese pronunciation differences leave outward lexical traces; exact intermediary and first attestation must be checked per word |
| Later crystallization | Later pidgins, dictionaries, and colonial spellings preserve only later witnesses | Do not backdate nineteenth-century Chinese Pidgin English to Zheng He's fleets |
- Tea is a useful counterexample to “cha by land / te by sea”: Portuguese **chá** also traveled through maritime contact; Dutch **thee** reflects a Min-associated route, possibly with Malay mediation (Mair and Hoh 2009; [etymological overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tea#Etymology)).
- Do not equate Portuguese **chá** directly with Guanhua: the Macau/Cantonese setting matters. Likewise “Hokkien” identifies a family of contact evidence, not one unchanging pronunciation.
- Concrete documentary layer: [Ma Huan's Chinese text](https://zh.wikisource.org/wiki/瀛涯勝覽), “Malacca,” records **打麻兒香** (a tree resin burned for light and used to coat ship hulls) and **沙孤米** (starch grains made from the 沙孤 tree); “Sumatra” records **底那兒**, a gold-currency designation — all three senses verified against the transcription (accessed 2026-10-17). Modern identifications compare *damar*, *sago*, and *dinar*, respectively; exact donor/intermediary paths and vernacular integration are `(to-verify)` here.
- Working administrative/contact lexicon: **haijin 海禁**, maritime prohibitions; **shibo 市舶**, maritime-trade administration; **chaogong 朝貢**, tribute presentation; **tongshi 通事**, interpreter/linguistic mediator. Institutions and permissions vary; a generic English “customs officer” can erase those distinctions.
- **Wokou 倭寇** is an official/adversarial label in particular conflicts, not a reliable census of Japanese ethnicity; check the actors rather than deriving their speech from the label.
- **Fólángjī 佛郎機** is an incoming, mediated ethnonym: its Persian/Arabic *farangī* “Frank, Latin Christian” origin is documented, and in Ming usage it designated the Portuguese and their guns; the Malay intermediary is reported but not certain, and the exact first local attestations are `(to-verify)` here. Do not use it as evidence of a Portuguese-wide vocabulary layer without lexical witnesses.
- Loan worksheet: `[recipient variety, donor candidate, intermediary, earliest dated form, sense, users, medium, sound adaptation, competing etymology]`.
- **Application:** first select brokers and commodities, then borrow a restricted semantic field; let prestige, religion, taxation, and household multilingualism determine which words spread beyond the quay.

## Novel vernacular registers: crafted heteroglossia, not recorded conversation
- **Baihua 白話** in Ming fiction is a literary resource with earlier histories; it is neither modern standard Chinese nor unedited peasant speech (Hanan 1981; Norman 1988).
- **Zhanghui 章回**, chaptered narrative, and **huaben 話本**, vernacular-story traditions/texts, are form/history labels; neither proves that an extant book was dictated by a performer.
| Register/resource | Where to look | Fictional-culture application |
|---|---|---|
| Storyteller-style address and transitions | Hanan's narrative model; narrator–audience framing | Create a performed intimacy that may conceal an editor's sophisticated design |
| Classical quotation, poems, parallel headings | Vernacular narrative embeds prestigious written forms | A street-oriented story can still demand elite literary recognition |
| Oath, heroic nickname, brotherhood address | *Shuihu zhuan* / *Water Margin* traditions discussed by Plaks | Distinguish self-made fraternity from registered kinship and state legitimacy |
| Celestial offices, scripture, comic petitioning | *Xiyou ji* / *Journey to the West* traditions | Bureaucratic language can cross human/divine worlds without making satire a doctrinal manual |
| Household negotiation, money, documents, songs | *Jin Ping Mei* traditions | Track authority through spouses, servants, brokers, and objects rather than court titles alone |
| Historical narration and speeches | *Sanguo yanyi* / *Three Kingdoms* traditions | Elevated narration can coexist with vernacular episodes; the “four masterworks” are not stylistically uniform |
- Plaks's emphasis on literati recensions and irony usefully challenges “novels are simply folk speech”; do not replace that error with “all popular performance is really elite authorship.”
- Authorship, dating, and textual layers are contested for several major novels. Name the edition before extracting dialect forms or treating a scene as a historical observation.
- **Era trap:** a Ming-produced work may set its story in the Han, Three Kingdoms, Tang, or Song; separate production date, narrated era, and later editorial intervention.
- **Application:** define each speaker's address forms, quotation access, taboo terms, and occupational lexicon; vary register by audience and stakes, not just by social class.

## Lijia vocabulary: administration creates a social lexicon
- *Ming shi* 77 locates the empire-wide Yellow Register order in **Hongwu 14 (1381)**: ideally **110 households** per li, with **10 leading households** and **100 others divided into ten jia**; this is a normative design, not a universal settlement size.
- Leading-household and jia service rotate over a **ten-year** cycle; the chronicle calls the ordering **páinián 排年**. Ten leading households are a rotating pool, not ten simultaneously serving lizhang. Who actually served, paid, delegated, or remained registered requires local evidence (Huang 1974).
| Term | Working meaning | Coupling / mistranslation warning |
|---|---|---|
| **hù 戶** | Registered household | Fiscal/legal unit; do not assume one roof, one nuclear family, or one biological lineage |
| **hùjí 戶籍** | Household registration/status record | Documentary identity can persist when residence or livelihood changes |
| **lǐjiǎ 里甲** | Organization through li and jia | Not identical to a natural village, lineage, or every later mutual-surveillance institution |
| **lǐ 里** | Higher grouping in this scheme | Contextual administrative sense, not the distance measure merely because the character is identical |
| **jiǎ 甲** | Constituent grouping | Membership and service category, not a clan name |
| **lǐzhǎng 里長** | Li head/service role | Not automatically a salaried county magistrate or permanent village chief |
| **jiǎshǒu 甲首** | Jia head/service role | Household obligation and mediation; do not translate all local headmen as one office |
| **dīng 丁; liáng 糧** | Assessed adult-male/labor and grain dimensions | Ranking households by ding/liang is not a neutral census of bodies and food |
| **fùyì 賦役; yáoyì 徭役** | Tax/service obligations; labor service | Preserve the distinction between a levy, compulsory service, and later payment substitutions |
| **huángcè 黃冊** | Yellow Registers, household-based fiscal/service records | Neither a land map nor a complete, continuously accurate population count |
| **yúlín túcè 魚鱗圖冊** | Fish-scale registers, parcel-based land records | Land and household records relate but are not interchangeable; “fish-scale” is not a tax rate |
| **jīlíng 畸零** | Residual category, including persons unable to bear service in the cited scheme | Not simply “criminal outsiders”; normative inclusion/exemption and practical marginalization need separate study |
| **mín / jūn / jiàng 民 / 軍 / 匠** | Civilian, military, artisan registration categories | Registered obligation does not transparently describe an individual's current daily work |
| **táohù 逃戶; liúmín 流民** | Households evading obligations; mobile/displaced people in the chronicle's categories | Administrative accusations and migration experience are not identical descriptions |
- The chronicle distinguishes urban **fang 坊**, suburban **xiang 廂**, and rural **li 里** terminology, and later notes discrepancies between formal Yellow Registers and working registers; do not narrate the founding grid as timeless reality.
- **Single Whip, yitiao bian 一條鞭:** later-Ming consolidation/commutation of fiscal and service liabilities belongs to a reform history, not a new name for lijia; exact locality, implementation date, and assessment mix remain essential (Huang 1974).
- **Application:** make a tax notice, household petition, market account, and oath name the same person differently; an institutional category can survive after its economic basis changes.

## Naming practices: identities by audience and document
- Distinguish family surname, personal name **ming 名**, courtesy name **zi 字**, sobriquet **hao 號**, office title, kinship address, and nickname; do not concatenate every form into a universal full name.
- Elite naming conventions are unevenly documented and socially selective; avoid giving every farmer, servant, woman, or child a literatus's complete naming repertoire.
- Ma Huan's personal name, courtesy name **Zongdao 宗道**, and literary sobriquet (*Kuaiji shanqiao* 會稽山樵, “Mountain-woodcutter of Kuaiji”) appear in the text's own prefaces and colophons and illustrate multiple referential identities; [biographical pointer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ma_Huan), accessed 2026-10-17. This entry does not collate which forms appear in each edition.
- **Hongwu 洪武** and **Yongle 永樂** are reign-era names, not personal surnames; rulers also have personal, temple, and posthumous names. Date formulas and forms of reference need their own audience rules.
- Lineage generation characters and naming taboos are useful design mechanisms, but a particular family's sequence, prohibited graphs, enforcement, and exceptions require dated genealogical or legal evidence `(to-verify)`.
- A woman's appearance under a surname plus **shi 氏**, kinship relation, or title may reflect a record's conventions rather than an absence of personal identity; never fill an archival silence with invented biography.
- **Application:** maintain `[identity, registry label, intimate address, respectful address, literary signature, hostile nickname]`; let rank imagery and spoken address agree only when the situation warrants it.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **A coupled authority stack:** registration class → service obligation → office/grade or household title → permitted garment → recognized image → appropriate address; each arrow can fail or be contested.
- **A contact ecology:** court lingua franca + local household language + broker repertoire + selective written transcription; borrowed words spread unevenly with people, goods, and institutions.
- **A plural sacred order:** state recognition, local efficacy, lineage memory, and institutional religion can intersect without reducing to one fixed divine bureaucracy.
- **An era engine:** changes in trade permissions, silver obligations, workshop access, and print markets alter both vocabulary and visible status; do not merely swap decorative motifs between periods.
- **A handoff, not canon:** give lore-shokunin alternative rules, dated reference anchors, uncertain cells, and consequences; the sibling chooses and records fictional facts.

## Provenance cautions and research stop rules
- Statutes tell us what authorities prescribed; portraits stage identities; excavated/surviving textiles are selective; novels construct voices. Agreement among these is evidence to investigate, not something to assume.
- Cammann's 1953 collection study contains colonial-era collecting assumptions and overt political/cultural bias; use its object observations critically, not its account of an allegedly vanished or uniform “Old China.”
- Missionary grammars and travelers translate through their own categories; Ma Huan's court-linked observations also select and classify foreign societies. Neither a European nor a Chinese elite witness supplies an unmediated local voice.
- This base is thinnest on non-elite speech, women as speakers rather than depicted subjects, minority-language traditions, and locality-specific ritual/costume practice. Chronicle silence is not evidence of cultural absence.
- Stop before specifying an exact historical pronunciation, universal badge construction, single loan itinerary, local reform date, or undocumented ritual detail without a dated source; retain `(to-verify)` rather than inventing precision.
