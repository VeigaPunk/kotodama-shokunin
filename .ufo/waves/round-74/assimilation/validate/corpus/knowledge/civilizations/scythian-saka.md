## Scope: related reference frames, not a single civilization package

- **Scythian, narrow use:** principally the northern Black Sea populations discussed by Greek authors; qualify region and century whenever attaching a custom to this label.
- **Scythian, broad archaeological use:** a family of steppe material traditions, sometimes called Scytho-Siberian; shared weapons, horse equipment, and animal art are not proof of one language or political identity.
- **Saka:** an Iranian designation used in Persian contexts for steppe populations; its scope does not map exactly onto every Greek use of “Scythian.”
- Herodotus 7.64 explicitly says Persians call all Scythians Sacae; this is his account of Persian classification, not a modern census or proof that all groups shared an autonym.
- **Pazyryk:** an Altai archaeological culture and its type-site cemetery; a particularly rich archive of preserved organic materials, not a transcript of Pontic Scythian life.
- Pazyryk and the Ukok Plateau's Ak-Alakha burials belong to a related archaeological discussion but are not the same excavation site; do not attach a later Ukok discovery to Rudenko's earlier excavations.
- This entry offers reference knowledge and design procedures only. Any fictional synthesis, name, period boundary, or religious rule remains a proposal for lore-shokunin.

## Calibration ladder: what each source can actually support

| Evidence class | Strongest usable claim | Limit / unsafe inference |
|---|---|---|
| Excavated object with documented context | Material, construction, decoration, deposition; approximate dating | Object meaning, owner's spoken language, or everyday use do not follow automatically |
| Preserved clothing or tattooed skin | A particular cut, ornament, or body placement existed | One burial cannot establish a population-wide uniform or an initiation rule |
| Herodotus's text | A Greek author reports a particular custom, name, or explanation | Reported practice is not automatically independently corroborated history |
| Iranian comparison and transmitted names | Iranian affiliation; possible sound correspondences and etymologies | A complete Scythian grammar, one common dialect, or a native spelling system |
| Later Khotanese/Tumshuqese manuscripts | Actual Middle Iranian language and script evidence | Direct recovery of an earlier Altai or Pontic community's speech |
| Modern symbolic interpretation | A testable reading to compare against object distribution | A decoded, universal “steppe symbol dictionary” |

- Use **attested object**, **reported by author**, **comparative reconstruction**, and **fictional design** as separate record fields; confidence in a report's wording is not confidence in its historical universality.
- Named checks: **taphonomic filtering** asks what survived; **equifinality** asks which different practices could leave the same trace; **object biography** separates manufacture, circulation, use, repair, and burial.
- The major thin areas here are ordinary speech, non-elite naming, indigenous explanations of motifs, and the relationship between burial presentation and living dress.

## Language family and writing: high family confidence, low recoverable detail

- The defensible broad affiliation is **Indo-European → Indo-Iranian → Iranian**; Scythian varieties are conventionally placed with Eastern Iranian, but their internal relationships are much less secure than this broad affiliation (Lubotsky 2002).
- “Iranian” here is a linguistic classification, not modern citizenship, a biological type, or proof of adherence to Zoroastrian doctrine.
- Evidence is heavily mediated: personal and tribal names, deity names, scattered glosses, and forms transmitted through other languages. Greek spelling is evidence about a transmission channel as well as about its source language.
- Lubotsky (2002, p. 190) stresses the meagreness of Old Iranian-period Scythian evidence and the uncertainty whether it represented a single language; do not present a speculative dialect tree as settled.
- Distinguish early Scythian material from later Sarmatian/Alanic names; comparison with Ossetic is useful, but modern Ossetic is not a ready-made dictionary for every ancient steppe population.
- Treat **dialect continuum** and **language contact** as working models to test, not as permission to erase distinct communities or chronological breaks.

| Surface | Writing / transmission | Practical calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Pontic Scythian names in Greek accounts | Greek alphabet, Greek inflection, manuscript transmission | Keep the transmitted spelling beside any proposed Iranian reconstruction |
| Saka labels in imperial records | The recording empire's language and script | An imperial label does not establish the labelled community's own literacy |
| Pazyryk material culture | Objects, images, bodies, deposition | An animal motif is not thereby a letter, a clan name, or a readable inscription |
| Khotanese and Tumshuqese | Modified Brāhmī in a much later Tarim Basin manuscript setting | Genuine textual evidence, but not a contemporaneous Pazyryk script (Emmerick 2009) |
| Greek-speaking cultural intermediaries | Herodotus 4.78 reports Scyles learning Greek speech and letters from his mother | A reported individual literacy/contact pathway, not evidence for universal Scythian literacy |

- The sources used here do not provide a continuous native Scythian prose corpus from which to reconstruct complete syntax, case paradigms, or an oath formula.
- Do not label ambiguous marks an established “Saka alphabet.” A specific proposed decipherment requires an inscription edition, competing readings, and an acceptance-status check before use (see the Issyk inscription anchor below).
- For a fictional language: choose phonology and morphology explicitly; identify which features are comparative inspiration and which are inventions. Do not market the result as recovered Scythian.
- Couple multilingualism to actual routes: interpreters, intermarriage, port residence, mercenary service, or elite education; Herodotus 4.24 reports trade across multiple languages, not one frictionless steppe lingua franca.

## Era model: parallel regional clocks

Approximate staging below is a reference scaffold, not a universal succession of peoples or a precise dating tool (Rolle 1989; Rudenko 1970; Simpson and Pankova 2017).

| Region / horizon | Broad staging | Coupling opportunity | Chronological brake |
|---|---|---|---|
| Northern Black Sea, earlier Scythian horizons | Principally seventh–sixth centuries BCE for this scaffold | Emergence of elite display and changing external contacts | Do not date every animal-style object to Herodotus's lifetime |
| Northern Black Sea, Classical-period interaction | Fifth–fourth centuries BCE | Greek-mediated names, trade, commissioned imagery, contested elite identities | A Greek-made object need not depict a Greek religious interpretation accepted by its owner |
| Altai, Pazyryk frozen-tomb archive | Especially fifth–third centuries BCE; cemetery/object dates vary | Preserved clothing, horse furnishings, tattoos, imported textiles | Not a later “stage” through which all Pontic groups passed |
| Regional transformations | Third century BCE onward, with different local trajectories | Change in settlement, elite networks, dress, and labels | Avoid a single extinction date or simple Scythian → Sarmatian replacement story |
| Tarim Basin manuscript comparison | First millennium CE, substantially later than the tomb archive | Written Middle Iranian registers and Buddhist textual culture | Not an unchanged linguistic or religious survival from Pazyryk |

- For production, lock **region + date range + evidence type** before selecting an outfit or divine name. Exact cemetery chronologies require their current excavation/dating publications before use.
- Track language change, burial change, motif change, and political change separately; they need not occur together.
- Do not equate pastoralism with total absence of farming: even Herodotus distinguishes farming and nomadic Scythians (4.17–20). His own categories should not become timeless ethnic castes.

## Mythic and pantheon core: the Greek interface is part of the evidence

**Interpretatio graeca** is the equation of another community's gods with Greek gods; it reveals the narrator's translation strategy, not an exact identity of cults.
The following spellings follow Godley's Herodotus 4.59 rather than pretending to reconstruct native pronunciation.

| Reported name | Herodotus's Greek equation | Secure textual point | Do not infer automatically |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tabiti | Hestia | Given special priority in worship | Every Greek Hestia story or institution belonged to her cult |
| Papaeus | Zeus | Paired with Earth as her husband | A recovered Iranian divine genealogy or the whole Olympian Zeus profile |
| Apia | Earth | Named within this reported divine relationship | Every female figure in steppe art depicts her |
| Goetosyrus | Apollo | A reported divine equation and name | A securely demonstrated solar cult with Greek Apollo's attributes |
| Artimpasa | Heavenly Aphrodite | A reported divine equation and name | A single pan-steppe “fertility goddess” with a fixed costume |
| Thagimasadas | Poseidon | Worship attributed specifically to Royal Scythians | Universal coastal/sea symbolism among inland Saka |

- The same passage names Heracles and Ares without supplying equivalent Scythian names there. Leave that gap open rather than inventing a native deity name.
- Herodotus 4.62 describes a sword serving as the image of Ares at a brushwood structure; cite this as his report, not as archaeological proof of every steppe community's weapon cult.
- **Origin traditions must remain separate:** 4.5–7 attributes a Targitaus-and-sons account to Scythians; 4.8–10 attributes the Heracles/serpent-woman account to Pontic Greeks.
- In the first account, burning heavenly gold selects the youngest brother for kingship; agricultural, martial, and drinking implements connect sovereignty to more than warfare alone.
- Godley's 4.5 renders some implements “sword” and “flask”; Rawlinson's translation instead has “battle-axe” and “drinking-cup” (both texts checked). Check the Greek and chosen edition before building a precise regalia inventory.
- In the Greek-origin story, bow, girdle, vessel, and a test of competence connect descent, bodily equipment, and legitimate rule. This is a narrated charter, not a chronological report of a founder's life.
- **Dumézilian trifunctionalism** can organize a comparative hypothesis about sovereignty, warfare, and production; it cannot independently prove three hereditary classes or explain every triad.
- “Shamanic” is not an automatic identification for an unusual hat, tattoo, hybrid creature, or burial. A pan-steppe, timeless shamanic religion is unsupported by these sources.
- Iranian linguistic affiliation does not license filling gaps with the entire Avesta, later Persian mythology, or modern Ossetian narratives without a separate historical argument.

## Oaths: distinguish compact-making from royal-hearth perjury

**Herodotus 4.70, reported sequence:**

1. Parties to an agreement contribute blood from a small cut or puncture; it is mixed with wine in a large earthenware vessel.
2. A sword, arrows, an axe, and a javelin are dipped into the mixture. Godley's “scimitar” is a translation label, not a modern curved-sabre costume specification.
3. Solemn imprecations are made; the contracting parties and their most honourable followers drink.

- The source supports a report of **embodied pledge + shared medium + weapons + malediction + elite participation**. It does not preserve the words of a Scythian-language oath.
- “Blood brotherhood” may be a modern shorthand, but 4.70 itself does not establish adoption, lifelong fictive kinship, a universal legal institution, or use in every commercial exchange.
- **Different passage, different mechanism:** 4.68 connects an oath by the king's hearth, alleged perjury, royal illness, and diviners' accusations; 4.68–69 describes dangerous adjudication and punishment.
- Do not merge the royal-hearth accusation episode with the compact-making rite into one standardized liturgy.
- **Greek-source bias:** Herodotus writes through Greek categories, Black Sea contact networks, mediated information, and a literary interest in contrasting customs. He does not identify 4.70 as a personally witnessed rite.
- Bias is not a verdict that all detail is false. Preserve the specific report while withholding claims of universality or independent confirmation; an image of shared drinking alone cannot verify blood in the cup.
- The account concerns the Scythian world of his narrative, not automatically Pazyryk or every Persian-labelled Saka group.
- Fictional application: define who can pledge, whose presence validates it, which objects witness it, what speech performs it, and how breach is established. These are design questions, not recovered ancient rules.

## Kurgans and frozen tombs: costume evidence through a burial filter

- A **kurgan** is a burial mound in archaeological terminology; the term is not a recovered Scythian word for the grave or its ritual.
- At Pazyryk, frozen burial conditions preserved bodies and organic equipment exceptionally well. Water ingress and freezing are part of the preservation history; “all Siberian tombs preserve everything” is false (Rudenko 1970).
- Burial assemblages are selected presentations of the dead, often elite and sometimes disturbed or robbed. Separate grave costume, horse costume, chamber furnishing, and evidence for clothing used in life.
- Use **chaîne opératoire**: fibre/skin acquisition → preparation → felt-making or weaving → cutting/sewing → ornament → wear/repair → funerary selection. Do not reduce textiles to decorative patterns.

| Material / costume signature | Evidential use | Language–symbol coupling | Restriction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wool textiles and felt | Distinguish woven structure from matted fibre; record actual construction | Separate vocabulary for fibre, fabric, garment, and technique | Felt is not a weave; a felt hanging is not automatically a cloak |
| Leather, fur, coats, trousers, stockings, and footwear in the burial archive | Assemble outfits only from documented objects and their associations | Movement, insulation, fastenings, bodily coverage | The archive's garment range is not one uniform worn by everyone |
| Appliqué, embroidery, and attached ornaments | Identify substrate, attachment, and layering | A motif can cross textile and leather while changing execution | Surface ornament does not establish the garment's cut |
| Horse masks, harness, saddle coverings, and carved/gilded fittings | Extend dressed display beyond the human body | Shared visual repertoire on rider, horse, and equipment | Funeral horse masking is not ordinary riding dress by default |
| Imported or externally connected textiles, including silk | Investigate manufacture and circulation separately | Loanwords and prestige may follow exchange, but need not | Silk possession does not prove local sericulture or direct travel to its producer |
| Preserved animal tattoos | Record figure, position, and relationship to other marks | Body as a durable display surface beside detachable costume | No automatic reading as writing, clan membership, or shamanic office |
| Tall pointed Saka caps in Herodotus 7.64 | A Greek description of a particular military contingent | Outsider classification can foreground a distinctive silhouette | Not proof that every Pazyryk person wore the same cap |

- Rudenko's “Clothing and Personal Adornment” chapter is a starting point; use Simpson and Pankova's later catalogue for comparison, not as a substitute for an object's excavation record.
- Record whether “gold” means solid metal, sheet, foil over another material, or merely a modern colour description. Gold-covered wood can create costly-looking display without a solid-gold object.
- Garment cut, biological sex assessment, gender presentation, social rank, and ritual office are different claims; do not derive all from one hat or weapon.
- “Ice princess” and similar modern labels are not excavated titles. Exact headdress reconstructions, stitch patterns, dyes, and office identifications require item-level verification (see the Ukok anchor below).

## Animal-style symbolism: a formal repertoire, not a decoded dictionary

Rudenko (1970), Rolle (1989), and Simpson and Pankova (2017) support comparison of objects and motifs; the meaning column below deliberately offers hypotheses, not translations.

| Formal feature | What to record first | Possible design move, explicitly fictional | Historical caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Deer/cervids with emphasized antlers and folded limbs | Species identification, pose, antler treatment, support | Make branching forms recur in insignia and ritual vocabulary | “Deer = sun/soul” is not a universal decipherment |
| Felines, raptors, and predator–prey compositions | Relation between figures, orientation, distortion, repetition | Express competition, capture, protection, or renewal through contrasting compositions | Violence in a picture does not identify the represented myth |
| Griffins and other composites | Which anatomical parts are combined and where | Distinguish ordinary animals from named ritual beings | Modern “griffin” is an analytic label, not its recovered local name |
| Animal transformations or exaggerated anatomy | Transitions, repeated heads, contour, negative space | Let one motif change across bodily and material surfaces | Technical/stylistic choices can resemble cosmological statements |
| Related motifs on skin, textiles, wood, and metal | Date and distribution before proposed equivalence | Build a visual grammar that survives translation between materials | Shared imagery need not have identical meaning in every medium |

- Operational sequence: **describe form → locate object → date/contextualize → compare distribution → propose competing meanings → label confidence**.
- Keep **iconographic identification** (“an antlered animal”) distinct from **iconological explanation** (“an ancestor carrying souls”). The latter needs more evidence.
- Decorative portability is a useful coupling mechanism: a motif can fit an appliqué panel, harness fitting, plaque, or tattoo without requiring a common writing system.
- Greek, Near Eastern, and steppe workshops and patrons participated in changing networks; separate maker, patron, user, and burial location before claiming an “ethnic style.”
- Do not use “Aryan animal totem” or a universal colour/animal code as scholarship. Iranian language affiliation and visual similarity do not establish racial identity or a single religion.

## Naming practices: preserve the transmission chain

| Name layer | Evidence / example | Use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Personal names | Godley's Ariapithes, Scyles, Idanthyrsus; Herodotus 4.76, 4.78–80 | Attested in a Greek narrative; an elite-weighted sample, not a complete naming system |
| Collective labels | Scythians/Sacae; Herodotus's Skoloti in 4.6 | Keep exonym, reported self-designation, imperial category, and archaeological label separate |
| Divine names | Tabiti and the other names of 4.59 | Keep transliteration and editorial variants visible; do not invent missing equivalents |
| Toponyms and glosses | Herodotus 4.52 pairs Exampaeus with a Greek explanation | A reported gloss is not an independently verified morpheme-by-morpheme analysis |
| Equipment terms | “Sagaris” in 7.64 | A term in a Greek account is not an entire recovered indigenous garment/weapon lexicon |

- A name ledger should carry **transmitted form → source/locus → spelling variants → proposed analysis → confidence**. Mark reconstruction with `*`; mark invention as invention, not as a cognate.
- Do not simply strip Greek endings and declare the remainder native. Check both source-language phonology and the receiving language's spelling/inflection.
- Productive name templates, stress patterns, female/commoner name inventories, and exact royal-name etymologies remain thin in the sources consulted here; no fabricated ancient meanings are supplied.
- For fiction, build a declared naming grammar, then specify how neighbouring scribes alter it. Give attire and symbols their own words rather than naming everything after a real-world ethnonym.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Coupled authority:** make a claim to rule legible through a narrated selection, named regalia, a bodily competence test, and public dress; do not import a whole historical pantheon unchanged.
- **Coupled oath:** design a relationship among spoken commitment, witnesses, meaningful equipment, and a breach procedure; leave the real Scythian wording unknown.
- **Coupled material grammar:** let a recognizable motif migrate among tattoo, garment, harness, and prestige object while the terms and techniques differ by craft.
- **Coupled regional history:** preserve distinctions between port-facing elites, mobile pastoral groups, and mountain burial traditions; a shared motif need not require a shared language.
- **Coupled contact:** distinguish an imported fabric, a copied design, an adopted word, and a translated deity; none automatically entails the others.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: **reference region/date → source and evidence class → proposed transformation → language rule → myth/ritual rule → motif placement → outfit construction → alternatives and confidence → canon decision left open**.
