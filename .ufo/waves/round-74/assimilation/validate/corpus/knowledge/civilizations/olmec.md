## Scope and calibration ladder
- Geographic anchor: the southern Gulf lowlands of present-day Veracruz and Tabasco; distinguish this heartland from the much wider distribution of Olmec-style objects (Pool 2007; Diehl 2004).
- “Olmec” can name an archaeological tradition, an art style, or a proposed population; those categories do not automatically coincide with one language, ethnicity, or state.
- Translated contemporary chronicles are not available as a secure check on religious narratives or royal identities; material evidence is abundant relative to recoverable indigenous explanations.
- Labels used here: **O** = observed archaeological feature; **I** = supported interpretation; **D** = disputed explanation; **U** = unknown or not securely recoverable.

| Evidence class | What it can support | Inference ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| Excavated context, stratigraphy, material sourcing | Association, relative sequence, movement of particular materials | Does not identify the language or religious meaning of an object |
| Repeated visual attributes and their combinations | Distinct iconographic categories; patterns of presentation | A motif is not a translated divine name or complete myth |
| Individualized faces, regalia, monument investment | An argument for elite/ruler commemoration | Not a securely identified king, reign, or dynasty |
| Calendrical notation in later inscriptions | Numerical sequences and some chronological anchors | Calendar recognition does not decipher surrounding language |
| Later Mesoamerican texts and living traditions | Explicitly bounded comparative hypotheses | Not substitute Olmec chronicles or proof of unchanged belief |

## Era model: stage sites and media, not a single eternal culture
Dates are approximate working brackets, not simultaneous boundaries across all regions (Pool 2007; Diehl 2004).

| Stage | Archaeological anchor | Coupled change to track | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early formation, second millennium BCE | El Manatí ritual deposits and emerging Gulf settlements | Wetland setting, offerings, specialist craft, exchange | Formation is gradual; do not assign a universal founding year |
| San Lorenzo prominence, roughly 1400/1200–900 BCE | Large center, monumental sculpture, landscape modification | Concentrated patronage, stone logistics, elite display | Starting dates vary with definition; political reach needs separate demonstration |
| La Venta prominence, roughly 900–400 BCE | Monumental precincts, buried offerings, greenstone and serpentine deposits | Monumental space and deposition as political/religious media | Not proof that a royal capital or population simply migrated intact |
| Later regional/Epi-Olmec traditions, after about 400 BCE into the first millennium CE | Tres Zapotes, La Mojarra, Cerro de las Mesas | Inscribed monuments, calendrical display, changing regalia | Not merely “the same Olmec kingdom, later”; local sequences and affiliations differ |

- San Lorenzo and La Venta histories overlap with other communities; prominence is not a complete succession list.
- Monument reuse, movement, recutting, and burial complicate dates assigned from findspots; distinguish carving date, reuse date, and deposition date.
- Do not turn the ending of an archaeological style into extinction of its people or language; a single catastrophic collapse story is not established.
- Fictional-era method: vary patronage, access to stone, prestige vocabulary, and ritual staging together; do not require phonological change to coincide exactly with every art-historical boundary.

## Language family and writing: keep separate hypotheses separate
- **D:** Campbell and Kaufman (1976) support a Mixe–Zoquean affiliation through geography, reconstructed vocabulary, and loans into other Mesoamerican languages; their argument is not direct transcription of Olmec speech.
- **U:** A single securely identified language for every Olmec-associated site is not available; regional multilingualism and language contact must remain possible.
- A proposed ancestral affiliation supplies neither an authenticated royal-name generator nor a recoverable complete phonology, grammar, or everyday lexicon for San Lorenzo.
- **Method:** distinguish a language family, an archaeological population, a writing system, and a visual style in the working record; evidence for one does not automatically identify the other three.

| Sign-bearing evidence | Observed/proposed status | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|
| Recurring symbols on sculpture and portable objects | **O:** structured visual repertoire | Every repeated motif is a phonetic sign |
| San Andrés seal and greenstone fragments, approximately 650 BCE | **O:** signs and imagery; **D:** Pohl et al. 2002 interpret speech, calendrical names, and early writing | An agreed syllabary, translated text, or exclusive invention center |
| Cascajal Block, proposed early first millennium BCE, often approximately 900 BCE | **O:** incised sign sequence; **D:** writing status, dating, and implications require separate assessment | Secure excavation-layer dating or an accepted translation |
| Later Isthmian/Epi-Olmec inscriptions | **O:** recurrent signs and recognizable calendrical notation; **D:** linguistic readings | A demonstrated continuous script lineage from Cascajal, or securely translated Early Formative chronicles |

- Cascajal was recovered from disturbed material, not a controlled primary excavation context; chronology rests on association and comparative arguments (Rodríguez Martínez et al. 2006; Englehardt et al. 2020).
- Englehardt et al. (2020) provide positive imaging, composition, patina, and manufacturing evidence; calling the object simply “a proven fake” ignores that work.
- Conversely, authentic ancient manufacture does not establish an exact engraving date, prove every sign encodes speech, or decipher its language.
- Numerical/calendrical recognition and phonetic decipherment are different achievements; do not publish a readable date as proof that a proposed king's name is secure.

## Epi-Olmec case file: not all surviving texts are mere fragments
Pérez de Lara and Justeson (2006), especially “Known epi-Olmec texts,” distinguish lengthy texts, short passages, eroded signs, and monuments whose script attribution is uncertain.

| Object/group | Evidential contribution | Limitation to carry forward |
|---|---|---|
| La Mojarra Stela 1 | Substantial main inscription, figure, calendrical passages; additional side column | Side column is heavily eroded; linguistic reading remains disputed |
| Tuxtla Statuette | Clearly incised portable inscription; comparative sign forms | Shorter context cannot by itself validate an entire decipherment |
| Tres Zapotes Stela C | Long Count notation, with date conventionally correlated to 32 BCE; additional signs | Secure numerical interpretation does not make every non-calendrical sign readable |
| Chiapa de Corzo sherd | Early short text assigned to the Francesa phase, 450–300 BCE | Recovered in intrusive fill; preserve its contextual qualifications |
| Cerro de las Mesas stelae | Evidence extending the tradition into Classic-period settings | Uneven weathering; affiliation and legibility vary monument by monument |
| Teotihuacan-style mask inscription | A substantial additional comparison text used by Houston and Coe | Unknown find context and private-collection history weaken independent chronological control |

- Justeson and Kaufman (1993) propose a mixed word/syllable system and a language ancestral to Zoquean; their 1997 paper presents the recovered side column as a successful further test.
- Houston and Coe (2003) dispute the decipherment's performance on another text; this is a real scholarly challenge, not grounds to pretend that no linguistic proposal exists.
- The mask's uncertain archaeological provenance also limits the force of a negative test; neither team should be silently treated as an independent neutral authority on its own reading.
- Operational status: **disputed, not a secure translation base**. Exact ruler names, narrative translations, and sign pronunciations require independent verification before reuse (Robinson 2008; Macri 2017).
- For fictional scripts, borrow the research problem: readable dates can coexist with opaque names and political rhetoric. Do not copy proposed Isthmian syllabograms as established ancient speech.

## Mythic/pantheon core: an iconographic repertoire, not a recovered scripture
Joralemon (1996) and Taube (2004) distinguish recurrent supernatural attributes; classifications and functional identifications are scholarly models, not an emic divine census.

| Analytical category | Visual/relational anchor | Reading ceiling |
|---|---|---|
| “Were-jaguar” or composite infant | Cleft heads, downturned mouths, infant-like bodies, human/feline combinations in some depictions | Not every cleft head identifies one being; “jaguar” itself can be interpretive |
| Rain/water supernatural | Particular combinations of facial attributes, headgear, and water-related comparisons | Functional attribution is not a recovered divine name or complete rain myth |
| Maize supernatural | Vegetal/maize attributes and sprouting or cleft-head imagery in iconographic analyses | A plausible fertility association is not proof of a universal creation story |
| Earth/cave monster imagery | Monstrous openings, niches, and settings compared with earth or emergence imagery | Do not automatically supply a later named underworld and its narrative geography |
| Avian, serpent, and aquatic composites | Repeated animal attributes combined with human or supernatural forms | Not a one-to-one dictionary linking each animal to one abstract meaning |
| Humans in elaborate regalia | Associations between supernatural attributes, presentation, and elite performance | Deity, impersonator, ruler, and ancestor may not be separable from appearance alone |

- Las Limas Monument 1 combines a seated bearer, an infant-like composite, and several incised supernatural profiles: useful evidence against reducing the entire repertoire to a single jaguar god.
- “Were-jaguar” is a modern comparative label, not a known Olmec word; it imports no werewolf-like full-moon cycle, contagion, or fixed human/animal dualism.
- The hypothesis that a human woman and a jaguar produced divine offspring is a reconstruction from ambiguous imagery, not a preserved Olmec narrative; label it **D**, never “the Olmec creation myth.”
- Interpretations invoking transformation or shamanic mediation are hypotheses; visual hybridity alone does not prove a uniform shaman caste, trance technology, or particular intoxicant.
- Use attribute bundles and co-occurrence before functional labels: record head cleft, mouth, ear treatment, headdress, posture, bearer, medium, and context independently.
- A defensible working inventory can say “composite A, water association proposed”; it should not manufacture sacred names to make an incomplete pantheon look complete.

## Symbolic system: relations, media, and controlled ambiguity
These are analytic handles from the iconographic literature, not a translated Olmec symbol dictionary (Joralemon 1996; Taube 2004; Pool 2007).

| Coupling mechanism | Evidence-sensitive use | Common category error |
|---|---|---|
| Part standing for a larger supernatural configuration | Compare a diagnostic attribute across full figures and abbreviated depictions | A lone cleft or crossed-band motif always names the same god |
| Greenstone, growth, maize, and valued offerings | Test agricultural/life associations against the object's context and imagery | “Green means fertility” everywhere, regardless of object or period |
| Celt forms and vegetal imagery | Compare polished stone forms, offering arrangements, and maize interpretations | Every celt was an agricultural implement or represented the same ritual |
| Niche/throne and earth/cave associations | Investigate how elite bodies are framed by charged places | Every niche depicts the same literal episode of emergence |
| Headdress and supernatural attributes | Track how office or ritual display may appropriate a visual vocabulary | A head ornament is already a readable personal name |

- Material movement, stylistic borrowing, shared religious reference, and shared spoken vocabulary are distinct processes; document the bridge between them instead of assuming it.
- Productive ambiguity is usable craft knowledge: the same emblem can acquire different meanings in court ceremony, household practice, or a later reinterpretation, without all meanings being archaeologically recoverable.

## Colossal heads: ruler portraiture is persuasive, identities are not recovered
Pool (2007) and Diehl (2004) support ruler-portrait interpretations while acknowledging inferential limits.

| Interpretation | Why it is considered | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Portraits of particular rulers | Individualized faces, distinctive headgear, costly monumental production | **I:** leading interpretation; stylization and intended likeness cannot be independently checked against named sitters |
| Rulers as ballplayers | Helmet-like headgear and comparison with the ritual ballgame | **D:** possible role/display, not proof that every sitter was a professional player |
| Warrior or protective-headgear readings | Some headgear looks protective | **D:** resemblance does not identify exact function, material, or military institution |
| Ancestral or commemorative rulers | Monument investment and subsequent reuse can fit memorial practice | **D:** compatible with portraiture, not evidence for a specific funerary rite |

- Alternatives are not all mutually exclusive: a ruler could be portrayed in ceremonial equipment or remembered after death.
- Some heads were recarved from earlier monumental forms, including thrones; a stone's biography can outlast one display program (Pool 2007; Diehl 2004).
- Resetting, mutilation, and burial need local context; ritual retirement, political alteration, and conflict are alternatives, not interchangeable proven events.
- Do not infer a dynasty's sequence, headgear-to-name key, or reign dates from head numbering; archaeological catalog numbers are not ancient regnal ordinals.
- Facial resemblance is not evidence for African or Egyptian founders. Hyperdiffusionist origin claims are unsupported alternatives to Indigenous Mesoamerican development, not equally established archaeological models (Taube 2004, introductory discussion).
- Fictional application: tie commissioned likeness, office emblem, stone supply, and later recutting to different institutions; keep portrait identity distinct from how later viewers reinterpret it.

## Costume and material signature: elite images are not a wardrobe census
Read represented form before assigning fiber, construction, daily use, or rank rules (Pool 2007; Diehl 2004; Taube 2004).

| Surface | Recoverable anchor | Reconstruction limit / coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Helmet-like headgear | Strongly differentiated forms on colossal heads | Function and original construction are not settled; useful for individual/office distinction, not an authenticated heraldic alphabet |
| Waist garments and belts | Sculptural depictions conventionally described as loincloths and belts | Silhouette does not establish weave, fiber, fastening method, or universal gender rules |
| Ear ornaments, pendants, and other adornments | Depicted ornaments and surviving worked objects | Wearing position and social value need context; an isolated ornament does not identify a priestly caste |
| Supernatural or ceremonial dress | Headgear and bodily attributes couple human display to composite beings | Ritual presentation should not become everyone's daily costume |
| Greenstone and serpentine carving | Durable portable objects and deposits | “Jade” is not a mineral identification for every green object; distinguish material, color, and value |
| Basalt monuments, ceramics, and perishable media | Different preservation, visibility, and investment patterns | Surviving elite stone is a biased sample of material culture, not the whole society |

- Warm, wet environments and survival bias make cloth, featherwork, and other organics unevenly visible; absence of preserved garments is not evidence of an unclothed population.
- El Manatí's exceptional organic preservation warns against treating the surviving stone repertoire as a complete material inventory (Diehl 2004).
- Specific Olmec garment names, textile recipes, dye semantics, sumptuary codes, and headgear construction remain thinly evidenced here; preservation is the limiting factor, with only a few fragments of cordage and woven mats recovered so far, and textile practice argued mainly from tools, depictions, and greenstone implements (Follensbee 2008). Do not import later Nahua or Maya terms as authentic substitutes.
- Fictional terminology method: name anatomical placement, fastening, material, role, and era separately; only then decide which terms become hereditary office titles, ritual epithets, or ordinary garment nouns.

## Naming practices: preserve the empty cells
- “Olmec” derives from a later Nahuatl designation conventionally glossed “rubber people”; its modern archaeological use is not a recovered Early Formative self-name (Diehl 2004).
- San Lorenzo, La Venta, and archaeological monument labels are modern location/catalog conventions, not translated royal or divine names.
- A secure general inventory of Olmec personal names, name morphology, endonyms, and spoken titles cannot be supplied from this evidence; later disputed Epi-Olmec readings do not repair that gap.

| Naming layer | Safe record | Unsafe substitution |
|---|---|---|
| Archaeological reference | Site + monument identifier + provenance | Treating “Head 4” as a fourth ruler or “La Venta” as an ancient capital-name |
| Proposed inscription reading | Object, sign sequence, proposer, publication year, disputed status | Copying a proposed ruler-name translation without its uncertainty |
| Fictional construction | Explicitly invented naming grammar, social registers, and historical changes | Calling fabricated Mixe–Zoquean-looking words authentic Olmec |

- Do not mix Maya royal titles, Nahua honorifics, or Mixtec calendrical naming into one supposedly attested Olmec system.
- For fiction, decide independently whether birth names, offices, ceremonial epithets, and later historian labels differ; coupling them is a design choice, not recovered Olmec practice.

## Mother culture, sister cultures, and the missing inferential bridge
The unrestricted claim that one Olmec source supplied every major Mesoamerican institution is not a safe baseline; neither is the opposite claim that Gulf centers had no exceptional influence (Pool 2007; Flannery and Marcus 2000).

| Model | What it foregrounds | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Mother-culture/diffusion model | Early Gulf monumental centers, prestigious imagery, far-reaching connections | Exclusive invention, one-way dependence, shared language, or political rule over all recipients |
| Sister-cultures model | Multiple contemporary regional trajectories and reciprocal interaction | Equal power, identical contributions, or absence of Gulf influence |
| Selective interaction/network model | Different goods, motifs, technologies, and institutions moving on different routes | A particular transmission history without chronological and contextual evidence |

- Blomster, Neff, and Glascock (2005) use elemental analysis to argue for Gulf production and export of Olmec-style pottery; this is evidence for a specific production/exchange model, not proof of total civilizational parenthood.
- Stoltman et al. (2005) present petrographic evidence for reciprocal exchange and multiple production areas; Flannery et al. (2005) challenge sampling, assignment methods, and social conclusions.
- Do not convert that dispute into “chemical sourcing never works.” Compare sampling, reference groups, alteration, mineralogy, and the precise objects each study can source.
- Two-way movement of vessels does not prove equal political status; Gulf asymmetry would not prove every borrowed institution originated there.
- Require separate evidence for **raw-material source → object manufacture → image distribution → local interpretation → institutional adoption**. A pot with a motif does not transport an entire pantheon, state, or language automatically.
- “Earliest surviving example” is not “place of invention”; preservation and excavation bias matter. Avoid assigning writing, calendrics, or every later rain deity to one ancestral people as a package.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **Coupled prestige without linguistic uniformity:** let a fictional interregional elite borrow imagery and rare materials while translating ritual language locally; decide which terms are loans and which are local calques.
- **Era-sensitive authority:** link a shift in patronage or monument medium to changed titles, ceremonial dress, and commemorative practice; do not assume every system changes simultaneously.
- **Plural readings of composite beings:** generate recurrent attribute bundles and competing interpretations before writing any definitive myth; distinguish creators, ritual specialists, later antiquarians, and outsiders.
- **Political biographies of objects:** a costly portrait or throne can be recut, moved, buried, or revived; track the material act separately from the story later institutions tell about it.
- **Partial legibility to later readers:** preserve a gap between understood dates, uncertain name-signs, and unknown narrative clauses; incomplete modern decipherment is not evidence of limited ancient literacy.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: separate sourced observations, scholarly interpretations, explicitly invented mechanisms, and unresolved alternatives; lore decides whether any proposed fictional system enters the world bible.

## Provenance cautions
- Knowledge is thinnest here on emic divine names, oral narratives, individual rulers, spoken naming grammar, ordinary clothing, and household religious variation; chronicle evidence is scarce, not silently recoverable from later analogies.
- Later Maya, Nahua, Mixe, and Zoque traditions have their own histories. Colonial-period recordings also carry translation, political, and Christian mediation; neither those records nor present-day communities are transparent windows onto the Early Formative.
- Looted, traded, or poorly provenienced objects lose contextual controls; attractive stylistic attribution and museum display do not restore an excavation context.
- This entry checks the FAMSI report and PNAS arguments against full texts, and key journal claims against publication metadata/abstracts; the standard monographs and catalog essays are reference editions, not claims of fresh page-by-page inspection.
- Citation level: the monographs in the source key are cited at book/essay-theme level from bibliographic records, publisher pages, and abstracts; no page-specific quotations or object-level attributions from them are made here, and no invented quotations, indigenous words, or translated Olmec royal biographies are supplied. A future page-level quotation or attribution requires a fresh check against the edition.
- The cited decipherment debate establishes a documented disagreement, not an exhaustive survey of every subsequent proposal; later structural work (Macri 2017, Glyph Dwellers Reports 51–53) should be rechecked before presenting any new reading as accepted.
- Reuse the coupling mechanisms and uncertainty discipline, not a generic “jaguar civilization” aesthetic, a universal pantheon chart, or an unsupported foreign-founder story. This file is reference/method knowledge and records no fictional canon.
