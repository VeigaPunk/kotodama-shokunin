## Scope and confidence contract

- **Living Indigenous people, not an extinct civilization:** this directory’s label is organizational; do not impose a city-state, empire, or vanished-people model on the Ainu. [F26; UP]
- The reference area includes Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and the Kuril Islands, with historical Ainu presence also relevant to northeastern Honshu; distributions changed through contact, displacement, and state borders. [F26; UP]
- **Coverage bias:** the examples here lean toward Hokkaido. Horobetsu narratives, Saru recordings, Chitose recordings, Sakhalin ritual objects, and Kuril clothing evidence are not interchangeable samples.
- **Established** below means supported by the cited documentation, not uniform across every community or period; **interpretive** means a scholarly or museum explanation; **contested** means competing reconstructions; **design** means a proposed fictional mechanism.
- Keep source date, narrator/maker, locality, recording/collection circumstances, and translation separate. A recorded twentieth-century practice is neither automatically prehistoric nor automatically a recent invention.

## Language family, structure, and writing

| Dimension | Reference baseline | Coupling consequence / guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| External genealogy | Ainu is conventionally treated as a language isolate; Hokkaido, Sakhalin, and Kuril varieties also motivate speaking of a small Ainu language family. [L22] | “Isolate” concerns unestablished external relatives, not lack of internal diversity or contact. |
| Japanese relationship | Ainu is not classified as a Japonic language. Shared word-order features or similar-looking words do not establish common descent. [L22; UP] | Separate genealogy, areal convergence, borrowing, and writing-system adoption. |
| Grammatical profile | Verb-centered, head-marking description; generally subject–object–verb order, with postpositions and rich verbal morphology. [L22] | Build an independent grammar rather than Japanese sentences with substituted nature nouns. |
| Geographic variation | Hokkaido and Sakhalin differ; a grammatical statement about one documented dialect needs a locality tag. [L22] | Do not splice a Sakhalin form into a Hokkaido paradigm without a contact history. |
| Transmission and script | Traditional transmission was predominantly oral; later transcription uses Latin letters and adapted katakana with additional signs. [UP; N21] | A rich verbal tradition does not require an ancient native alphabet; script can have a later political biography. |
| Present language | Severe disruption of transmission coexists with learning, recording, and revitalization initiatives. [F26, pp. 12–13; UP] | Avoid both “everyone still speaks it” and “no living community remains”; speaker totals need dated definitions. |

- Editorial convention: use `kamuy`, `mosir`, `cise`, and `attus` as convenient Romanized forms; sources also use `kamui`, `moshiri`, `chise`, and `attush`. These spelling pairs are not evidence of four historical sound changes.
- Modified katakana must be able to represent Ainu consonant endings; simply applying ordinary Japanese syllable spelling can obscure distinctions. [UP; L22]
- **Design:** distinguish everyday speech, performed narrative, ritual address, archival transcription, and administrative naming as different registers or media, not different “evolutionary levels.”
- Fine-grained accent rules, dialect-specific sound inventories, and person-marking paradigms are outside this entry’s checked coverage: consult the specialist descriptions first — the phonetics/phonology and morphosyntax chapters collected in [L22], and [DC24] for Sakhalin Ainu. This entry neither reproduces nor adjudicates them.

## Yukar: classify the performance before borrowing a plot

| Category / evidence | What matters | Fictional adaptation, not an Ainu reconstruction |
|---|---|---|
| `yukar` and heroic narrative | A diverse performed narrative repertoire, not a synonym for every Ainu story; terminology and classification vary regionally. [O22] | Specify the local genre, narrator role, audience, and performance occasion before assigning a meter. |
| `kamuy yukar` in Chiri’s collection | Spiritual beings speak in the first person; each narrative has a distinctive refrain. [S11; C23] | Let the apparent “I” be nonhuman; design how listeners recognize the speaker and what knowledge that speaker lacks. |
| `uwepeker` / `uepeker` | Prose tales are distinguished from kamuy yukar in the documented corpus. [N21] | Give prose narration its own authority and social uses rather than treating it as degraded epic. |
| Recorded versus printed performance | Audio, transcription, gloss, and translation preserve different information. [N21] | Maintain a performance layer alongside a readable story summary; do not derive melody from typography. |

- Chiri Yukie’s thirteen narratives are a particular authored/transcribed collection, not a complete Ainu scripture. Strong situates their transmission through her grandmother and aunt in the Horobetsu tradition. [S11]
- The speakers include orca, wolf, fish owl, frog, hare, and pearl mussel: narrative personhood is not confined to impressive predators. [S11]
- A refrain is an identity-bearing performance resource; do not turn every refrain into a literal dictionary gloss or generic magic spell.
- NINJAL’s corpus identifies Kimura Kimi’s Saru material recorded by Nakagawa and Oda Ito’s Chitose material recorded by Bugaeva. Preserve narrator and dialect attribution when comparing examples. [N21]
- **Framework:** genre-sensitive performance analysis first; use the Parry–Lord toolkit in `linguistics/orality-formulaic-language.md` as comparative tooling, not proof that Ainu narratives were composed exactly like South Slavic or Homeric epic.
- **Design worksheet:** narrator identity → embodied viewpoint → refrain rule → human/nonhuman misunderstanding → social consequence → performance occasion. Change these relations, not just the animal names.
- Sakhalin and Kuril repertoires are thinly covered here, partly for documentary reasons: Kuril Ainu survives mainly in old documents ([L22], Satō’s chapter on the major old documents), and Sakhalin Ainu lost its last first-language speaker with Asai Take’s death in 1994 [DC24]. Hokkaido genre labels and refrain practices must not be projected onto them without further evidence.

## Kamuy reciprocity: relationships rather than a fixed god roster

- [F26, pp. 14–15] distinguishes `ramat` (spirit) from `kamuy`, spiritually consequential beings that strongly affect people or exceed human control. Do not flatten that distinction into “every pebble is an identical little god.”
- In the cited accounts, kamuy inhabit their own realm and appear in the human realm in animal, plant, implement, or other phenomenal forms; flesh and fur can be understood as gifts. These are reported beliefs, not externally certified supernatural facts. [F26; U92]
- Gratitude, hospitality, respectful communication, and sending-back form a reciprocal relationship. This is not the claim that every event is a bargain or every spirit benevolent. [U92, pp. 255–256]

| Being / domain in the cited accounts | Relationship to human activity | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| `apehuci kamuy` / `ape-kamuy`, fire/hearth | Hearth presence and mediation of prayers to other kamuy. [F26, p. 14; U92, pp. 260–263] | Recasting the hearth divinity as merely a minor “fire element” in a combat chart. |
| `kimun-kamuy`, mountain/bear spirit | Hunting, the bear’s gifts, and bear-sending relationships. [U92, pp. 265, 269] | Making the bear the sole spiritual being or a universal hereditary animal totem. |
| `repun-kamuy`, offshore/sea spirit | Marine relationships; Utagawa discusses orca/dolphin associations, while Strong includes an orca narrator. [U92, p. 265; S11] | Assuming every region uses identical names, species identifications, or ranks. |
| Disease and dangerous phenomena | Smallpox and other overwhelming forces can enter the kamuy category. [U92, pp. 255–256; F26, p. 14] | Turning spirituality into a universally gentle “nature harmony” ideology. |

- **Design:** model a relationship graph: visitor → human hospitality → useful material gift → thanks/offerings → return communication. Add failed hospitality, disputed obligations, and household differences rather than a single alignment label.
- **Calibration:** “animism,” “reciprocity,” and Utagawa’s discussion of a “balanced symbol model” are analytical frames. They are not substitutes for particular Ainu explanations or names for a unified doctrine.
- A shared ritual pattern does not establish an unchanging creed, a centralized priesthood, or a complete divine genealogy.

## Ainu-moshiri, landscape, and naming practices

- `aynu mosir` / `Ainu-moshiri` can be glossed as the human/Ainu land or world; `kamuy mosir` refers to the kamuy realm in the cited cosmological framing. Do not silently turn this contrast into fixed modern national borders. [F26, pp. 14–15; U92]
- A landscape name can encode lived relationships to rivers, settlements, and travel. It is not necessarily a proclamation of ownership, nor is its current official spelling its original analysis.

| Attested naming element | Working gloss | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| `aynu` | Human/person; also the ethnonym. [U92, p. 255 n. 1] | Translation depends on context; do not equate every use with a modern nationality label. |
| `mosir` | Land/world in the cosmological expressions above. [F26] | Do not assume it always means “island,” “kingdom,” or an administrative province. |
| `kotan` | Settlement/community. [F26, p. 16] | A community term does not by itself imply a centralized polity. |
| `pet`, `nay` | River/stream elements. [F26, p. 12] | Do not invent a universal large-river/small-river distinction from these glosses. |

- [F26, p. 12] gives Noboribetsu from `nupur-pet` and Wakkanai from `wakka-nay`. These support identifying the river elements; they do not license guessing every place name from modern Japanese syllables.
- **Toponymy workflow:** earliest recorded form → attributed local pronunciation → dialect → morphological analysis → physical landscape/history → later administrative spellings → alternative interpretations.
- Separate a sound-based Japanese rendering from a later explanation based on kanji meanings; apparent semantic transparency can be secondary.
- **Design:** create an Indigenous hydrological name, a trading-community adaptation, and an official map form in the fictional language history. Record the transformations; do not generate pseudo-Ainu names by concatenating this glossary.
- Personal names need a different evidence file from place names. The AMNH account notes forced Japanese naming under assimilation; a modern Japanese surname must not be treated as evidence against Ainu identity. [AM]
- Traditional personal-name assignment, avoidance rules, and family-specific usage are insufficiently documented in the sources checked here for a general generator; a dedicated naming study is still required before use (to-verify). The checked record mainly documents state pressure on names — Tokugawa and later Meiji officials ordered Ainu to change hairstyles, clothes, and names [F26, pp. 7–8] — not the traditional system itself. Do not invent an obligatory animal-name or clan-name formula.

## Symbolic system: form, placement, and use before “meaning”

| Carrier | Documented feature | Calibration / craft move |
|---|---|---|
| Embroidered/appliquéd clothing | `moreu` whorls and `ayus` thorn forms are named in the Foundation booklet. [F26, p. 23] | A form name is not a pan-Ainu dictionary assigning every spiral the same theological message. |
| Garment openings | Protective explanations attach to decoration at cuffs and hems; NMS also discusses collars. [F26; NS] | Couple symbolic placement to garment construction; do not scatter motifs as texture noise. |
| `inaw` | Shaved wooden ritual objects with multiple roles, including offerings and deity presence. [F26, p. 15; U92] | Not an alphabet or an interchangeable decoration; identify recipient, context, and maker. |
| `ikupasuy` and `tuki` | Libation stick and lacquered cup used in prayer communication. [F26, p. 15] | Distinguish handling, speech, and offered drink; an implement’s importance is relational. |
| Hearth, sacred window, outdoor ritual area | Communication joins domestic space to the spirit world. [U92, pp. 257, 261–263; F26, pp. 16–17] | Design routes, seats, and thresholds; do not impose one compass orientation on every house. |
| Traded treasures and ornaments | Lacquerware, swords, silk, and beads can be incorporated into valued local practices. [U92, p. 258; F26] | Foreign manufacture does not make an object spiritually inauthentic or socially insignificant. |

- **Source disagreement:** AMNH emphasizes nonrepresentational design and individual variation; NMS reports possible thorn, eye, star, and animal readings. Neither warrants a universal decipherment key. [AM; NS]
- Keep observable geometry, an attributed protective explanation, a maker’s interpretation, and an outsider’s speculation as separate claims.
- AMNH mentions a proposed relationship between clothing patterns and women’s tattoos; that is a suggestion, not an established derivation. What is documented is political: tattooing was a women’s practice banned by Hokkaido authorities in 1871 [HAA], with protective/beauty/afterlife interpretations recorded in later official descriptions [JTA]. Local meanings still require separate, community-specific checking.
- **Design:** choose a constructional boundary, an attributed danger, a family/maker’s variation rule, and a transmission path. Invent the resulting graphic vocabulary rather than tracing sacred designs.

## Attus: textile technology coupled to ecology and exchange

- `attus` / `attush` names bark-fiber cloth and, in descriptions, robes made from it; it does not name all Ainu clothing or all Ainu embroidery. [AM; NS]
- **Technical distinction:** the cited Ainu elm-bast cloth is woven, not simply a sheet of beaten barkcloth like many other traditions grouped under that English word. [NS]
- **Chaîne opératoire:** obtain suitable inner bark → soak/process and separate bast → prepare and join fiber lengths → weave on a backstrap loom → assemble garment → appliqué/embroider as appropriate. Elm (*ohyo*) bast work is described in recent technical studies, including the portable backstrap loom (*attushi karape*) and a staked warp setup [Pal24]; treat the chain above as a summary, and check species- and locality-specific harvest schedules against that literature. [NS; UP]
- The museum examples connect locally obtained elm fiber to traded indigo-dyed cotton applied as decoration. Material provenance and design identity can differ within one garment. [NS]

| Garment/material choice | Evidence base | Worldbuilding consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Elm-bast robe | NMS loom A.1909.499.1 and robe A.1909.499.48; tapered sleeves and robe construction. [NS] | Derive silhouette from cloth production, work, and cutting choices, not a generic “tribal tunic.” |
| Cotton coat with white appliqué | Shiraoi coat collected in 1901. [AM] | Date the object and exchange network; cotton does not automatically signify abandonment of local culture. |
| Nettle, fish skin, fur, bird skin | Regional material diversity documented by the Foundation; bird-skin clothing is especially relevant to Kuril evidence. [F26, pp. 18, 22] | Select ecology and region before assembling a costume; do not stack every attested material on one wearer. |
| Silk robes and other traded festive garments | Festive/ceremonial clothing could include Santan silk robes and jinbaori surcoats. [F26, p. 22] | Ritual value may increase through distant provenance and curated exchange. |
| Everyday versus highly decorated dress | Both Foundation and NMS distinguish ordinary clothing from festive/ceremonial attire. [F26; NS] | A museum’s finest robe is not an entire community’s daily uniform. |

- Historical accounts emphasize women’s weaving, sewing, decoration, and teaching across generations; AMNH also stresses individual variation. Do not turn a documented division of labor into a rule policing present-day creators. [AM; F26]
- Keep textile terms, stitch/loom techniques, garment categories, and motif names as separate lexical fields.
- **Design production sheet:** fiber source → access/harvest rights → processing labor → loom width → panel plan → thread/cloth imports → repair history → occasion → maker attribution.
- **Caution:** NMS’s broad contrast between hunting and agriculture is not evidence that Ainu communities never cultivated crops; Upopoy explicitly notes cultivation in some regions. [NS; UP]

## Era model: stage interactions, not “primitive → civilized”

| Stage / evidence window | What changes together | What cannot safely be back-projected |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier archaeological sequences | Epi-Jomon, Satsumon, and Okhotsk material histories overlap and interact regionally. [F26, pp. 2–3; U92] | An artifact assemblage is not a direct transcript of language, ethnic self-definition, or ritual speech. |
| Medieval transitions, region-dependent | Archaeologists use an “Ainu culture” period label for changes in material practices. [U92, pp. 266–267] | No single ethnic birth date; Upopoy explicitly cautions against a sudden ninth-to-thirteenth-century creation model. [UP] |
| Early-modern trade and coercion | Relations with Wajin, Matsumae authority, and northern exchange reshape access to goods and work. [F26, pp. 4–7] | Imported cloth, lacquer, and metal are not automatically recent contamination of an otherwise isolated society. |
| Meiji and later assimilation | The 1869 Hokkaido reorganization and 1899 “Former Aborigines Protection Act” belong with land loss, schooling, and livelihood restrictions. [F26, pp. 8–9] | Later name/language loss must not be naturalized as voluntary disappearance or cultural incapacity. |
| Twentieth century to present | Activism, creative transmission, language learning, and institutional change coexist with continuing inequalities. [F26, pp. 10–13, 26–27] | Revival is not proof of a dead people, a fake tradition, or restoration of one frozen precontact state. |

- Useful dated anchors: 1997 cultural-promotion legislation; 2008 parliamentary Indigenous-recognition resolution; 2019 legislation recognizing the Ainu as Indigenous; Upopoy opened in 2020. These are different events, not one discovery of Ainu existence. [F26, pp. 10–11]
- Pair every fictional era with language transmission, map labels, textile availability, performance settings, and ritual authority; these systems need not change at the same rate.
- Regional transport, epidemics, and forced relocations deserve their own research before a detailed Sakhalin/Kuril chronology is proposed; the checked record already supplies the key anchor — the 1875 Treaty of Saint Petersburg exchange and the forced relocation of Ainu from Sakhalin and the Kurils to Hokkaido and Shikotan, compounded by disease and many deaths. [F26, p. 8]

## Iyomante calibration: neither spectacle nor sanitized metaphor

| Claim | Status and evidence | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| A raised bear is ceremonially killed and sent back | Documented in Utagawa’s narrow definition of `iomante`; hospitality/gifts and return are central to its interpretation. [U92, pp. 255–259] | State the killing plainly; “sending-back” does not mean merely releasing a live bear. |
| All animal sendings are this same ceremony | Rejected as a blanket rule: Utagawa distinguishes raised-bear iomante from `opunire` for bears killed in the wild. [U92, p. 257] | Keep this author’s distinction visible; confirm local terminology before generalizing. |
| It is simply sacrifice to an unrelated superior god | Misleading for the relational account in the cited sources: the visiting spirit itself receives hospitality and departure gifts. [F26; U92] | Explain who visits, who gives what, and who is addressed rather than importing a generic sacrificial template. |
| Archaeological bear remains establish the full rite unchanged | Contested inference: Utagawa distinguishes older animal-related evidence from the developed cub-rearing ceremony. [U92, pp. 259–266] | Bones can support a material practice more directly than its exact liturgy, emotions, or theology. |
| Its origin has one settled date | Not established here; Utagawa’s late-eighteenth-century proposal is a dated archaeological argument, not a consensus timestamp — later scholarship records the same proposal rather than a fixed date. [U92; Uy15] | Leave exact origin and competing chronicle interpretations open. |
| Every community performed an identical annual event | Not established by this source set; regional practice and historical disruption matter. [F26; UP] | Do not invent a mandatory pan-Ainu calendar, participant roster, or present-day obligation. |

- **Comparative framework:** Utagawa critiques defining the whole culture through a “bear ceremony cultural complex” and offers a wider, hearth-centered network. This is a scholarly model, not an Indigenous constitutional text. [U92, pp. 256–264]
- **Design:** borrow the coupling problem—costly hospitality, animal personhood, communal distribution, ritual communication—not an operational reenactment of a community’s ceremony.
- Keep ritual participants’ explanations, animal-welfare judgments, colonial condemnation, tourist presentation, and contemporary practitioners’ choices separate; one account cannot speak for all five.
- Commissioned illustrations, travel accounts, photographs, and films need date, patron, audience, locality, and performance circumstances before they become reconstruction evidence.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Language ↔ myth:** let a performance register support nonhuman first-person testimony without pretending that grammar mechanically causes a religion.
- **Myth ↔ material:** make garments, cups, tools, and animal bodies participate in relationships; distinguish useful material, visiting person, and recipient of thanks.
- **Symbol ↔ costume:** place protection at a garment’s meaningful constructional boundaries; let makers vary a taught repertoire rather than stamping one ethnic logo.
- **Naming ↔ era:** preserve older landscape descriptions beneath newer pronunciations, scripts, and official labels; do not equate spelling change with population replacement.
- **Trade ↔ ritual:** allow imported objects to acquire local biographies and ceremonial value; invented “purity” rules need their own history, not presumed authenticity.
- **Handoff to lore-shokunin:** offer a locality/era matrix, register/genre specification, reciprocity graph, naming-history worksheet, textile production sheet, and source/confidence annotations as optional methods. Lore decides adoption and canon; this entry makes neither decision.

## Provenance cautions and thin coverage

- Direct checks for this entry include the Foundation’s booklet sections, Utagawa’s open article, NINJAL’s corpus introduction, Upopoy’s descriptions, the AMNH/NMS textile accounts, and Strong’s publisher synopsis; the latter is not a substitute for close reading of every translated song.
- Handbook edition, chapter authors/titles, pages, and DOI metadata were checked (the chapter set and DOIs verify); detailed phonology, alignment, reconstructed stages, and individual performances still require the actual chapter text before reuse, and nothing here summarises their contents.
- Utagawa’s article preserves valuable comparative detail but also dated evolutionary language and hypotheses. Do not adopt its “primitive communism” terminology or proposed cultural stages as timeless Ainu self-description.
- Ainu voices such as Chiri Yukie’s are authored, situated evidence—not anonymous folklore raw material. Outsider chronicles and colonial-era collections are likewise situated, often selective, and not transparent windows into a whole society.
- Chronicles and surviving objects do not securely supply a prehistoric ceremony’s complete sequence. Exact iyomante origins, traditional personal-name systems, Kuril performance traditions, and local tattoo meanings remain thin in this entry; the inline markers name the items that are still open, and none should be filled from analogy.
- Reject racial-origin typologies and the “vanishing primitive” frame; language genealogy, ancestry, material style, and political identity are different kinds of evidence. Do not convert conjectural long-range language links or Jomon continuity into an unchanged ethnic essence.
- Public access to a record does not make every story, name, motif, or sacred practice an unrestricted design asset. Attribution and community-specific guidance remain necessary; this is reference methodology, not a rights ruling or permission grant.
