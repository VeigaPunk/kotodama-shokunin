## 1. Core framework: Berlin & Kay (1969)

- **Basic term criteria** (operational, the whole game): (1) monolexemic; (2) not included in another term (scarlet ⊂ red fails); (3) not restricted to one class of objects; (4) psychologically salient — used by all informants, stable across sessions. Secondary flags: term doubles as an object name (orange, olive); recent foreign loanword. "Basicness" is judged against the whole system, not the hue.
- **Method**: 98 languages — 20 surveyed in situ with 329 Munsell chips, 78 via bilingual informants with a 40-chip subset; each term mapped as coverage area + focal (best-example) point.
- **Stage model** — implicational sequence, not a calendar:

| Stage | Adds | Note |
|---|---|---|
| I | black, white | light/dark poles |
| II | red | the only chromatic "early" hue |
| III | yellow *or* green | either may come first |
| IV | the other of that pair | |
| V | blue | |
| VI | brown | darkened yellow, per fuzzy-set account |
| VII | purple, pink, orange, grey | free order among themselves |

- Implication runs one way only: a stage-V system may lack all stage-VII terms but never jumps a stage. "Grue" (green+blue covered by one term) is the normal III/IV state of much of the world's lexicons.
- **The 1969 claim**: focal points cluster on canonical black/white/red/yellow/green/blue hues regardless of how much space a term covers — category *foci* are more universal than category *boundaries*. The refined claim has survived; the tight 1969 version did not (see §2–3).

## 2. Refinements after 1969

- Kay & McDaniel (1978): fuzzy-set semantics. Six "pure" hue terms (unique hues) vs **mixture terms** — brown = dark yellow, purple = red+blue, pink = whitened red, grey = desaturated; mixtures come last because they presuppose the pures. This is why brown is stage VI and why pink/orange/purple/grey are free-ordered.
- **World Color Survey** (WCS): 110 unwritten languages, 25 speakers each, 330-chip response sheets; data public since 2003. Confirmed focal clustering but showed boundaries are far looser than 1969 implied.
- Kay & Regier (2003): WCS naming centroids cluster in CIELAB space beyond chance; Regier, Kay & Khetarpal (2007): systems approximate **optimal partitions** balancing boundary smoothness against information load. This is the current respectable form of the universalism claim: constrained variation, not fixed stages.
- Kay & Maffi (1999): the sequence is a **dynamic, not a ladder** — systems gain, lose, and rename terms (e.g., loss of a term after the referent dye vanishes); aggregate trends, no law each language obeys.
- Lindsey & Brown (2006): WCS shows substantial within-language variability — individual informants differ a lot; usage patterns form graded "motifs," not crisp typological slots.

## 3. Critiques and calibration (say this plainly in any lore doc)

- **The model's prehistory is dead as vision science — do not resurrect it.** Gladstone (1858) read Homer's odd color words ("wine-dark sea") as underdeveloped color perception; Geiger (1868/69) and Magnus (1877) built a lexicon→retina recapitulation ladder (white/black → red → yellow → green → blue as a history of the eye). Rivers (1901, Torres Straits) showed the effect is nomenclature, formulaic phrase, and test conditions — not retina. "Few color words ⇒ poor color vision" is the classic error of the field; the inferring is the bug.
- **Saunders & van Brakel (1997)**: the method presupposes what it finds — Munsell chip arrays, translation through bilinguals, and "best example" tasks all assume prototype structure and a Western stimulus geometry; basicness criteria blur at the edges; the evolutionary story risks the 19th-c. ladder it claims to replace. Standard reply: Kay & Regier (2003). Honest reading: strong on focal universals, weak on stage narration.
- **Lucy (1997)**: "basic color term" is an analyst's unit, not an indigenous one. Usage-based (reference-in-context) studies can slice the same lexicon differently than naming tasks.
- **Conklin (1955) counterexample**: Hanunóo runs on 4 color-level terms — (ma)biru ~ dark/black, (ma)lagi' ~ light/white, (ma)rara' ~ red/dry, (ma)latuy ~ green/fresh/succulent — yet speakers describe color richly, because each term couples hue with material quality (dryness, freshness, gloss, age). Chip-naming tasks erase exactly this coupling.
- **Old English**: blāw existed but was marginal; the everyday 'blue' word was hǣwen (Biggam 1997). A language can contain a modern-looking term and not use it — basicness is a property of use, not of the dictionary entry.
- **Two basic blues**: Russian siniy (dark) and goluboy (light) both pass basicness tests (Moss et al. 1990), breaking one-term-per-stage neatness. Winawer et al. (2007) found faster cross-boundary discrimination in Russian speakers — a small, real language-on-perception effect, the honest ceiling of relativity here.
- **Relativity effects are real but modest**: Kay & Kempton (1984) — language nudges similarity judgments near boundaries; Özgen & Davies (2002) — categorical perception can be trained. Neither repaints the retina; design consequences should stay behavioral (sorting, matching, taboo) not perceptual (acuity).
- Small sparse systems are equilibrium, not "early": some small communities run on few terms with heavy idiolectal variation (Hadza-type lexicons — sparse term sets, low consensus across speakers, each informant using his or her own terms; Lindsey, Brown, Brainard & Apicella 2015). A 3-term language is not a 19th-c. European's missing rung.
- **Working consensus** (Hardin & Maffi 1997; Kay & Regier 2003): weak universal constraints from human visual physiology + cultural-linguistic history doing the rest. For worldbuilding: use stages as a **consistency checklist**, never as a timeline.

## 4. Case file — Japanese ao / midori (a grue split inside living memory)

- Old Japanese four-term base: shiro (white/bright), aka (red/bright), ao (pale/dull — spanning green and blue), kuro (black/dark). Standard lexical history: aka derives from akaru "brighten," ao is its dull counterpart — **brightness terms first, hues second** (Nihon Kokugo Daijiten s.vv. あを・あか・しろ・くろ).
- ao's old span is fossilized: it named white wall-clay (aonibe 青丹, pillow-word for Naniwa) and pale moonlight; lexical histories also record it on dark horse coats (NKD s.v. あを). One term, three modern hues.
- Modern Japanese keeps **both** ao and midori: midori for foliage/green things; ao persists in fixed collocations — sky, sea, unripe fruit (青りんご), vegetables (青果 seika, 青菜 aona), green juice (青汁), inexperience (青二才 aonisai, 青臭い "green-smelling = callow"), bruises (青あざ). Analyses count midori as newly basic while ao never fully vacated; some count both, which breaks naive stage arithmetic (Uchikawa & Boynton 1987 mapped the ao/midori foci; the boundary sits in the blue-green region and varies across observers and generations).
- The lexicon has overridden physics in public infrastructure: traffic lights are officially ao shingō (青信号 — the go-light is 青色の灯火 in 道路交通法施行令 art. 2); the lens shade sits at the blue end of the CIE green range since a 1973 administrative step to make the go-light as blue as possible — reported in press and technical explainers, but the instrument itself has not been located, so treat the date as reported, not settled (Atlas Obscura 2017; 照明学会誌 64(6) 1980 on the CIE 色度推奨範囲). Fossil collocations beat spectroradiometry.
- Dye side: indigo (ai 藍) is Japan's flagship hue — the "Japan blue" of foreign commentary (Balfour-Paul 1998); blue stacks into shade vocabulary (kon 紺, rurikon 瑠璃紺; ruri itself from Sanskrit vaiḍūrya via Chinese — NKD s.v. るり).
- Edo sumptuary austerity is credited with the refined-muting culture summed up as "48 browns, 100 greys" (四十八茶百鼠) — hundreds of named subdued colors recorded from Edo commerce; the catchphrase's numbers are figurative ("many", not a count), and the phrase itself is a later label of the traditional-color literature rather than a located Edo-period document (NDL レファレンス協同データベース no. 1000168280; 長崎盛輝 1988; 吉岡幸雄 2000).
- Modern inventory anchor: JIS Z 8102:2001 fixes ~269 traditional color names plus systematic names — a checkable official list if a Japan-flavored world needs one.

## 5. Case file — other culturally weighted systems (calibration grab-bag)

- **Chinese qing 青**: one term spanning green, blue, and black (bronze, sky, grass all qing). Embedded in the canonical **five colors** (五色: qing, chi red, huang yellow, bai white, hei black) bound to Five Phases and directions — east-qing/dragon, south-chi/bird, west-bai/tiger, north-hei/tortoise-snake, center-huang (Kaogong ji in the Zhouli; Needham 1956). An administrative-ritual system: court robes, city planning, medicine, alchemy. Japan imported it wholesale as goshiki (五色) for court dress, capitals, and onmyōdō.
- **Plant→color path, documented**: Xunzi, "Quanxue" (c. 3rd c. BCE): "blue comes from the indigo plant, yet is bluer than the plant" (青出於藍) — the dye-source word (lan 藍) becomes the hue word. Same path as Japanese ai, English indigo (Greek indikon "Indian [dye]").
- **Biblical Hebrew**: teḵelet (blue) and argaman (purple) are ritual-status terms whose referents are **dye species** — teḵelet tied to a banded murex, argaman to shellfish purple, shani ("scarlet," literally "worm") to a scale insect. The words name the dye works, not wavelengths (Spanier, ed. 1987).
- **Celtic glas**: Welsh/Irish blue–green–grey continuum centered on landscape and vegetation (grass glaswellt, grey hair gwallt glas); modern Welsh glas functions as basic blue while pastoral senses survive in compounds (Biggam 2012).
- **Latin**: purpura is a status term first and a hue term second (Reinhold 1970); caeruleus and flavus live in material complexes (sky, bronze, straw). Gladstone's "inconsistencies" are formulaic/material usage — the corpus answer to the Homeric puzzle.
- **Egyptian**: wadj (wꜣḏ) covers blue-green through its substances (malachite, papyrus, fresh water); khesbedj names lapis/turquoise blue as material. Color lexicon as inventory of pigments and stones, not abstract wavelengths (Wilkinson 1994).
- **Russian**: two basic blues with distinct foci (Moss et al. 1990) — a hue can double up when the culture invests in it; instrument for that investment: painting, textiles, flags, or liturgy.

## 6. Dye technology ↔ lexicon: the feedback table

| Regime | Cheap stable hues | Lexicon consequence |
|---|---|---|
| No mordant set (ochre, charcoal, clay) | red-yellow earths, black, white | black/white/red basic; quality-coupled descriptors (Conklin pattern) |
| Madder + kermes Old World | strong red everywhere | red is the near-universal early chromatic term |
| Woad/indigo diffusion | blue becomes dyeable | blue term arrives late, or loans arrive with the dye |
| Indigo heartlands (Japan, India, W. Africa) | deep blue is the workhorse color | plant-name→color-name path; blue stacks into many shade terms |
| Shellfish-purple courts (Tyre, Byzantium) | purple exclusive luxury | purple stays a status term, rarely a folk hue term (Reinhold 1970) |
| 12th–13th c. Europe: cobalt glass + Marian cult | blue rises in prestige | status flip is institutional (church/court), not perceptual (Pastoureau 2001) |
| Post-1856 aniline synthetics | all hues cheap and loud | full 11-term sets everywhere + flood of minor terms named after places/battles (mauve, magenta = Battle of Magenta 1859, solferino) (Garfield 2000) |

- **Borrowing follows the dye trade**: English crimson ← Arabic qirmiz ← Sanskrit kṛmi-ja "worm-made"; indigo ← Greek indikon; orange takes its color sense from the fruit only in the 16th c. (before: yellow-red compounds); scarlet originally names a fine wool cloth, route likely Persian–Arabic (OED s.vv.). Color lexemes are trade receipts.
- **Prestige flips are documented and portable**: Roman blue = barbarian/low; medieval blue = royal/Marian; ultramarine (ground lapis lazuli, pricier than gold) was contractually reserved for the Virgin's robes in major commissions (Pastoureau 2001; Ball 2001). Symbol value travels with supply + institution, never with hue perception.
- **Sumptuary law stabilizes vocabularies**: Elizabethan apparel law (1574) legislates color-by-rank (Hooper 1915); Edo edicts force muted palettes and fine distinctions (§4). Design shortcut: legal color restriction ⇒ many near-synonyms for permitted hues, few for banned ones.
- **Term death follows trade collapse**: post-Roman Latin Europe loses shellfish-purple technology and purpura sinks to liturgical/symbolic residue; kermes crimson is displaced by New World cochineal after the 1520s (Greenfield 2005) without renaming "crimson." Words outlive their referent industries.
- **Standard catalogs replace folk elaboration in industrial eras**: Munsell (1905), ISCC-NBS name dictionary (Kelly & Judd 1955, NBS Circ. 553), RAL (1927), Pantone (1963) — when your culture industrializes color, named private palettes cede to published systems.

## 7. Calibration flags (discredited or contested, in one place)

- "Primitive peoples can't see color" (Gladstone/Geiger/Magnus lineage) — dead; see §3. Alive only as nomenclature history.
- "The rainbow has 7 colors" — Newton (1704) fixed seven spectral colors, inserting indigo to match the musical octave; an aesthetic choice, not perception (Ball 2001). A pre-modern culture with a 7-color rainbow is an anachronism; give it 3 (dark/light/bright), 5 (the wuse pentad), or whatever its color industry supports.
- Stage model as chronological clock — superseded by gain/loss/renaming dynamics (Kay & Maffi 1999) and WCS variability.
- Strong Whorfian "language forces perception" — unsupported at acuity level; supported as small judgment/discrimination effects (Winawer 2007; Özgen & Davies 2002). Write consequences into behavior and institutions, not eyeballs.
- Color terms as pure perception vocabulary — most real color lexicons carry heavy loads of material class, value, and status; treat any lexicon as hue ∩ brightness ∩ substance ∩ rank.

## 8. Application protocol — building a fictional culture's color lexicon

1. **Set the dye palette first** (biome + trade routes): list 3–6 dyes actually producible and affordable. §6's table is your gate; palette precedes lexicon.
2. **Assign basic terms** against the B&K criteria checklist, then run the implication check: blue ⇒ a green (or grue) term exists; brown ⇒ red/yellow/green exist; stage-VII terms ⇒ all six previous exist. If you violate, justify like Russian (cultural investment in one region of space).
3. **Decide the grue question**: does the culture split green/blue, where is the boundary (foliage vs sky? fresh vs decayed?), and how recent is the split? Mirror Japan: uneven across generations, loaded with fossil collocations that lag the boundary shift.
4. **Add dimension-coupled terms** (the Hanunóo move): hue fused with freshness, dryness, gloss, maturity. Cheapest texture upgrade available; naming-task-only cultures are the unrealistic ones.
5. **Write 2–3 loan events**: each color loan arrives with its referent — dye, cloth, fruit, battle-town. Etymologies double as trade-route history on your map.
6. **Bind status**: pick 1–2 colors where the word legally means rank (the purple pattern). Specify the monopoly: who may wear, name, or sell it; watch the term outlive the dye (§6).
7. **Fossilize collocations**: every weighted term needs fixed phrases (ao shingō, aonisai, wine-dark sea, glaswellt). Collocations survive after boundaries shift — they are the visible sediment of old categories.
8. **Alien-merchant test**: what can this culture *not* name because no dye or referent exists? The gap is a story hook and a realism marker.
- **Failure modes**: small lexicon ⇒ "they see less color"; perfectly symmetric stage ladders (real systems lose and rename); all focal colors matching modern paint-chip marketing; seven-color rainbows; hue-only lexicons with no material or status load.
