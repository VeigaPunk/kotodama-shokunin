## 1. The gap this entry fills — and the three-way map

Gap filled: no committed linguistics entry owned sound symbolism; `phonetics-phonology.md` §8 compresses it into ten lines and its §10 names `sound-symbolism.md` as a prospective sibling. The corpus's existing treatment is operationalized in `synthesis/myth-to-language-derivation.md` (phonesthemes, ideophones, controls); this entry supplies the missing linguistics layer.

- **Arbitrariness** — the default: form and meaning related by convention only (Saussure's principle; the null hypothesis any sound-symbolism claim must beat).
- **Iconicity** — form *resembles* meaning (resemblance relation). Sub-types a worldbuilder actually deploys:
  - *imitative*: onomatopoeia proper (a word copying a sound);
  - *cross-modal*: bouba/kiki-type shape–sound, texture–sound, taste–sound mappings (no acoustic imitation of the referent — a correspondence across senses);
  - *embodied/articulatory*: the mouth gesture or vocal effort mimics the referent (Ohala's frequency code is a voice-level instance);
  - *diagrammatic*: structural resemblance — reduplication for intensity, lengthening for duration, repetition for iterativity.
- **Systematicity** — statistical form–meaning regularities that need not involve resemblance at all: phonesthemes, sub-morphemic clusters, class-level phonological profiles (Dingemanse et al. 2015; Blasi et al. 2016 uses English `gl-` as its example).
- **Design consequence (the reason to keep the three apart):** iconicity is *local and motivated*, systematicity is *statistical and historically contingent*, arbitrariness is the *baseline*. A fictional lexicon may license all three, but they enter by different doors: iconicity at coinage (names, ritual words, ideophones), systematicity by drift and analogy (a `gl-`-like cluster emerging over generations), arbitrariness everywhere else. Conflating them produces the classic failure mode — treating a statistical bias as a per-word meaning law (§9, hazard H1).

| register | default relation | where it lives in a fictional culture |
|---|---|---|
| ordinary lexicon | arbitrary | most nouns, verbs, function words |
| coined names | iconic-optional | divine names, taboo replacements, brand-like epithets (→ `naming-systems.md`) |
| mimetic/ideophone stratum | iconic + conventionalized | narrative performance, song, insult, praise (→ §6) |
| phonestheme clusters | systematic | inherited word families, poetic diction (→ §5) |
| ritual/efficacious speech | iconic claim made explicit | "the word does what it sounds like" — the kotodama move (→ `myth-to-language-derivation.md`) |

## 2. The cross-linguistic bias evidence, calibrated

### 2.1 Blasi et al. 2016 — the audited baseline (full text retrieved)

- **Design:** unsupervised search over 6,452 word lists from the ASJP database, 28–40 basic-vocabulary items each (a 328-list subset has up to 100 items); coverage ≈62% of the world's languages and ≈85% of its lineages; six macroareas; associations kept only when attested in ≥10 lineages and ≥3 macroareas with no reversing area; conservative tests (two classifications, FDR 5%, length/permutation controls) — the authors state the scheme is likely to have a *large false-negative rate* and call the results a **lower-bound estimate** (both statements in the paper's "Testing Associations on a Global Scale" section; full text retrieved).
- **Yield:** 74 signals (positive and negative), 30 concepts, 23 symbols. The load-bearing positives, with risk ratios (RR) as published (paper's Table S2, full text retrieved):

| concept | symbol | RR | lineages | areas |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| small | i (high front vowel) | 1.58 | 78 | 3/5 |
| small | C (palatal affricate) | 5.12 | 61 | 3/4 |
| round | r | 2.48 | 56 | 4/5 |
| tongue | l | 2.77 | 280 | 6/6 |
| nose | n | 1.47 | 334 | 4/6 |
| full | p | 2.11 | 231 | 5/6 |
| full | b | 1.91 | 213 | 4/6 |

- **The negatives are as design-useful as the positives:** *big* and *large* showed **no** global association with back/low vowels; *this* and *that* showed no i/o association — so the popular "front = near/small, back = far/large" narrative does **not** survive at the global scale for the size pair, and the proximal/distal story fails outright in this dataset.
- **Distribution finding:** the associations recur across continents and lineages and their areal/historical pattern suggests they **emerge independently** rather than being inherited or borrowed — i.e., they are re-created biases, not relics (abstract, full text retrieved).
- **What it licenses for worldbuilding:** population-scale *flavor* (diminutives leaning on high front vowels, liquids in body-part and tongue-adjacent terms, r-sounds in roundness words) and the confidence to build a lexicon that *feels* motivated without claiming any single word must obey.

### 2.2 Haynie et al. 2014 — magnitude symbolism is real but position-conditioned

- 120 Australian languages; expected pattern (smallness/nearness ↔ front vowels/palatals; largeness/distance ↔ back vowels/velars) confirmed **in aggregate** — but two corrections matter: several relationships are significant only for segments in **prominent positions** (initial syllable etc.), and the mapping **cannot** be reduced to a simple vowel-F2 gradient, contrary to earlier claims.
- Design reading: put the sound-symbolic vowel where the language's prominence system will actually expose it (stressed/initial syllable), not in a scattered coda.

### 2.3 Smaller classic anchors (in-corpus audited block)

- Sapir (1929): synthetic magnitude effect in elicited forms — the laboratory classic.
- Ultan (1978): typological survey of size–sound symbolism — the survey anchor.
- Both remain citations-of-record in `phonetics-phonology.md`; no fresh retrieval this round.

## 3. Bouba/kiki — the most robust single effect, with its calibration

- **Anchors:** Köhler (1929) maluma/takete; Ramachandran & Hubbard (2001) relaunched it (their neural "synthetic synesthesia" story is *not* established — cite them for the effect, not the mechanism; to-verify if a mechanism is ever needed).
- **Ćwiek et al. 2022 (abstract retrieved):** online experiment across **25 languages, nine language families, 10 writing systems**; strong evidence for the effect across languages; *bouba* elicited more congruent responses than *kiki*; Roman-script speakers only *marginally* more likely to show the effect; scripts whose written forms are rounder/spikier for the two words did **not** strengthen it → the effect is crossmodal (voice ↔ visual shape), largely independent of orthography.
- **Bremner et al. 2013 (abstract retrieved) — the calibration case, stated precisely:** among the Himba of Northern Namibia (little Western exposure, no written language), the **shape–sound** effect *was* observable — so bouba/kiki is not a Western artifact; but the Himba **did not** map carbonation onto angular shapes, and matched *less bitter* chocolate to *angular* shapes — the opposite of Westerners. The published moral: crossmodal correspondences beyond shape–sound are culturally shaped.
- **Correction to the neighbor entry, carried forward:** `phonetics-phonology.md` §8 says bouba/kiki is "**not universal** (Bremner et al. 2013, Namibia)". The retrieved abstract shows the Himba *did* show the shape–sound effect; what differed was the wider correspondence set (taste). Correct reading for handoffs: **shape–sound robust across cultures; other crossmodal mappings culture-dependent.** This entry supersedes that clause in future citations; the old line stays as written (append-only discipline).
- **Design use:** naming (which name "fits" a round clan-totem vs a spiky one), script aesthetics (→ `writing-systems.md`), iconography rules, and the fact that audiences *feel* the fit even when it is statistically modest — pair it with in-world doctrine that over-reads the effect, and the over-reading becomes characterization, not error.

## 4. The frequency code — voice-level symbolism (Ohala 1994)

- Mechanism: high pitch / small vocal tract ↔ smallness, submission, non-threatening intent; low pitch ↔ largeness, dominance, threat. Grounded in mammalian size–frequency scaling, applied by humans to voice.
- Uses: honorific vs commanding speech styles, ritual voice disguise (masks, megaphones — couples to `culture-material/` masks and `phonetics-phonology.md` §7 channels), animal-address registers, diminutive endearment vs threat prosody.
- Calibration: a *perceptual bias* usable by performers, not a law that every culture's status-pitch mapping runs the same way; keep it as a plausible default and let the fictional culture's doctrine override it with a stated reason.

## 5. Phonesthemes — systematicity you can plant and police

- Definition (Firth 1930; refined by Bergen 2004): a recurrent sub-morphemic form–meaning association not assembled from productive morphemes (English `gl-` ~ light/vision).
- Evidence status: Bergen 2004 found psycholinguistic reality for English `gl-`; Otis & Sagi 2008 gave corpus methods; Blasi et al. 2016 uses `gl-` as its systematicity example. All language-specific — nothing here licenses exporting English clusters.
- **Phonestheme-bank protocol** (complements the derivation pipeline in `synthesis/myth-to-language-derivation.md`):
  1. Pick 3–6 clusters (initial C, C+l/r, reduplicative shape) and one semantic field each.
  2. Write 5–8 licensed members *with* the field, then 3 deliberate **nonmembers** (same cluster, unrelated meaning) and 3 **near-misses** (different cluster, same field) as controls.
  3. Record the licensing rule and the era it entered (phonesthemes drift in by analogy and can die out — date-stamp them like any loan stratum, → `historical-linguistics.md`).
  4. Never count derivatives of one root as independent evidence (the counting discipline in `myth-to-language-derivation.md` §6).
- Failure mode: banks that swallow the whole lexicon (everything starts with the sacred cluster) read as constructed; keep member-to-nonmember ratios low and auditable.

## 6. Ideophones — the depictive class (the biggest lever for a mimetic-rich culture)

- **What they are (Dingemanse 2012, author-version retrieved):** a class of words that *depict* sensory imagery rather than describe it — performative, gradient, often prosodically marked, cross-linguistically common but unevenly distributed and unevenly integrated into grammar. The class is defined by depictive function, not by "imitates a sound" (that is only the onomatopoeic sub-case).
- **Grammar profile (Dingemanse & Akita 2017, title/venue to-verify):** an *inverse relation* between expressiveness and grammatical integration — the more depictive the word, the less it behaves like a normal clause element (special quotative frames, "say/do" support verbs, loose juxtaposition). Design consequence: a mimetic register is marked *by its syntax*, not only by its sounds.
- **Reduplication as grammar (Dingemanse 2015):** repetition is the cross-linguistically dominant intensity/iteration/continuation device in ideophonic systems — a cheap, learnable morphology for a fictional register.
- **Japanese mimetics (Hamano 1998; Kita 1997):** a large conventionalized system (giongo 擬音語 sound-mimicking vs gitaigo 擬態語 state-mimicking) with productive phonological templates. The corpus makes no claim here about individual forms; use the system as a design model, not a template to copy (→ `real-world-inspiration-boundaries.md`).
- **Ideophone register design procedure:**
  1. **Fields** — assign which semantic fields carry ideophones (canonical: manner of motion, sound, texture, light/glitter, emotion, bodily states, speech style). Not every field needs them; a culture with ideophones for *everything* is noise.
  2. **Shapes** — pick 2–4 morphological templates (full reduplication, CVCV-tone melody, lengthened vowel, "coda-type" signature) and let only those templates count as ideophones.
  3. **Slots** — choose the integration mode: free-standing with a support verb, adverbial, or verb-root slot; then make the *syntax* the marker (per the inverse relation).
  4. **Register politics** — decide who may use them and where (narrative performance, women's speech, hunting talk, ritual), and what their absence signals (formal genres; → `sociolinguistics-registers.md`).
  5. **Orthography** — if the script must render them, decide whether length/tone is written or silently lost (→ `writing-systems.md`; a real cost of tone-bearing scripts).
- **Diachrony:** ideophones are lexical items and undergo change too (Dingemanse 2018, retrieved, discusses their margins and life in language) — stage them with the rest of the lexicon rather than freezing them as eternal nature-sounds (to-verify for specific change-rate claims).

## 8. Calibration shelf (keep this section with any excerpt)

| # | claim | status | handling rule |
|---|---|---|---|
| C1 | shape–sound bouba/kiki across cultures | **robust** (25 languages, Ćwiek et al. 2022; Himba shape–sound in Bremner et al. 2013) | usable at full strength for shape–sound; bouba stronger than kiki |

| C5 | frequency code | **mechanism-level support** (Ohala 1994) | perceptual bias; let in-world doctrine override with stated reason |
| C6 | phonesthemes | **language-specific, real for documented cases** (Bergen 2004) | never export a real language's cluster; build local banks with controls |
| C7 | "sounds carry the essence of things" as empirical claim | **not a linguistic finding** — a metaphysical/emic doctrine | fine as in-world belief (the kotodama move); flag as belief, never as evidence |
