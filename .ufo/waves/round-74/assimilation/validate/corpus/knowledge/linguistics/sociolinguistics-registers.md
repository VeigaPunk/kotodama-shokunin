## 1. The three layers of "same language" variation

| Layer | What varies | How acquired | What it indexes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dialect | phonology, morphosyntax, core lexicon | inherited region/class | where you're from |
| Register | lexicon, morphology, style, honorifics | chosen per setting | the situation + your claim to standing in it |
| Diglossia | whole-language H/L split | H learned in school | which institution owns the moment |

Core machinery to reuse:

- Marker / indicator / stereotype (Labov 1972): an *indicator* differs by group but carries no judgment; a *marker* is socially evaluated but mostly below awareness; a *stereotype* is overtly mocked and invites hypercorrection. Fictional recipe: 1 stereotype + 3–6 markers per group, and the stereotype is what satire quotes.
- Enregisterment (Agha 2007): features become "the way X people speak" only through metadiscourse — etiquette books, jokes, songs, censorship. A world needs in-world metadiscourse or its registers will not read as belonging to groups. "Pittsburghese" is the modern textbook case (Johnstone, Andrus & Danielson 2006): T-shirts and columnists made local features iconic.
- Indexical order (Silverstein 2003): one feature carries layered meanings — first order "from the north", second order "the north = rough". Characters do identity work by aiming at the second order.
- The load-bearing gap: dialect is inherited, register is chosen. Social drama lives where a character's chosen register does not match their inherited dialect (the low-born speaker with perfect chancery grammar).

## 2. Dialect geography

- Isogloss: a line where a feature flips. Bundles of isoglosses usually mark old borders, rivers, or religious schisms (the Benrath line maken/machen across German lands is the classic). Focal areas (crossroads cities) converge innovations; relic areas (islands, valleys, endogamous quarters) keep archaisms (Chambers & Trudgill 1998).
- Wave model beats family tree for geography: innovations spread contact-by-contact from centers; a dialect can be genetically "old" while linguistically innovative, or vice versa.
- Koineization (Trudgill 1986; Kerswill & Williams 2000): when mixed migrants build a new town, a new variety emerges in 1–2 generations via mixing → leveling (minority variants die) → simplification → reallocation (surviving variants split the work). Colonies, frontiers, refugee cities, post-war capitals: new-city speech is real in a lifetime and unremarkable within three.
- Social networks (Milroy 1987): dense multiplex networks (everyone knows everyone in several roles) preserve local forms; loose uniplex networks adopt standards. Urban innovators are network-loose; conservative strongholds are network-tight — or elite-inbred.
- Overt vs covert prestige (Labov 1963, 1972): some changes climb the class ladder consciously (overt), others are defended from below as local identity (covert — Martha's Vineyard centralization). Norwich: women lead overt-prestige forms, men over-report vernacular use (Trudgill 1972; see Calibration).
- Era anchors: pre-print societies support sharp dialect chains — villages 30 km apart borderline intelligible; print + schooling make the standard own writing while dialect retreats to voice; mass internal migration (not broadcast — radio/TV are weak levelers) does the actual homogenizing; broadcast-era leveling happens through migration, commuting, and conscription.

## 3. Diglossia

- Ferguson (1959) H/L prototype: two codified varieties of one language; H is learned in school and used for sermons, protocol, news; L for home and market. Classic pairs: Classical Arabic / colloquial Arabic; High German / Swiss German; katharevousa / dhimotiki in Greece (settled 1976); French / Haitian Creole.
- Fishman (1967) widened it: H and L need not be "the same language". Medieval Europe = Latin H over vernacular Ls; a world can run a foreign or dead H (Sanskrit over Indic vernaculars, liturgical Coptic in Egypt, Church Slavonic over Slavic speech). The H/L genetic relation is a design choice, not a requirement.
- Badawi (1973) five Egyptian levels — a ladder, not two boxes: fuṣḥā al-turāth (heritage classical) → fuṣḥā al-ʿaṣr (modern standard) → ʿāmmiyyat al-muthaqqafīn (educated colloquial) → ʿāmmiyyat al-mutanawwirīn (basic-literacy colloquial) → ʿāmmiyyat al-ummiyyīn (colloquial of the unschooled). Speakers shift mid-utterance; education = your position on the ladder. This ladder ports directly to scripture + chancery + market worlds.
- Dynamics worth stealing: diglossia erodes when L gains written domains (Albirini 2016 — Egyptian pop lyrics, internet forums); it hardens when H is dead or foreign (then H is also a professional secret — interpreters, notaries, priests become a power class; Waquet 2003 on Latin as class barrier). Violent switching exists: Greek 1976; Chinese wényán → báihuà in the May Fourth era (DeFrancis 1950); Norwegian Bokmål/Nynorsk as a managed two-standard compromise (Haugen 1966).
- Pollock (2006): "cosmopolitan" (translocal, sacral — Sanskrit, Latin) vs "vernacular" eras is itself an era axis. Decide whether your world is *in* the age of Latin or the age of vernaculars; the politics of every register flips with it.
- Post-creole continuum (DeCamp 1971; Bickerton 1975): acrolect–mesolect–basilect graded by schooling — the same ladder logic after colonization; a ready-made social ladder for colonial and post-colonial settings.
- Common error: diglossia ≠ two dialects. H/L differ wholesale in morphosyntax and lexicon, and mixing them is itself meaningful (self-elevation, or a comic/contemptible affectation). Also do not conflate diglossia with bilingualism (Fishman 1967): a monolingual L-speaker can live inside diglossia fine.

## 4. Registers: the situation-specific layer

- Specification kit (Halliday 1978): field (what is going on) + tenor (who to whom) + mode (speech/writing/channel). Add the indexical target ("sounds like X") and the register is fully specified. Hymes (1974) SPEAKING model (Setting, Participants, Ends, Act sequence, Key, Instrumentalities, Norms, Genre) for scene-level audits.
- Joos (1961) five clocks — frozen, formal, consultative, casual, intimate: crude but excellent for drafting dialogue ladders fast. Biber (1988) is the correction: registers differ along several measurable dimensions (involved vs informational, elaborated vs situation-bound reference, …), not one "formality" dial; and writing is not frozen speech — newsletter, sermon, and chat differ in kind, not degree.
- Professional registers: legal language resists paraphrase *by design* — terms of art, procedural archaisms, doublets (Tiersma 1999). Medicine, seafaring, metallurgy, masonry, banking, and scribal work all generate jargon as both tool and wall. Guilds are register factories.
- Anti-languages (Halliday 1976): underworlds, soldiery, prisons relexicalize (new words for money, police, killing) while keeping the grammar; overlexicalization clusters around the group's central obsession. Elizabethan canting, Polish grypsera, Polari (Baker 2002). An anti-language dies with its institution — fossil argots are excellent archaeology-plot clues.
- Honorific systems, the deep end:
  - Japanese: referent honorifics in three families — sonkeigo (raise the other), kenjōgo (lower self/group), teineigo (neutral-polite) — plus bikago "beautification" (Harada 1976; official taxonomy: Bunkachō 2007). Choice also signals the speaker's in-group vs out-group footing (uchi/soto): honorifics *to* an outsider about your own boss is a classic blunder.
  - Korean: separates addressee honorification (sentence-final speech levels — seven classical, four to six alive) from referent honorification (subject suffix -si-): you can honor the person talked *about* while staying casual to the listener (Lee & Ramsey 2000). Mis-setting = insult, comedy, or sudden intimacy.
  - Javanese: ngoko (plain) vs krama (polite) vs krama inggil (high honorific vocabulary) plus mixed madya; a sentence can be re-graded word-by-word to fine-tune relative status — status talk is live negotiation, not table lookup (Geertz 1972; Errington 1988). Near-caste coding.
  - T/V pronouns (Brown & Gilman 1960): the power semantic is nonreciprocal — superiors give T and receive V; the solidarity semantic is mutual T among equals. European history moves from power to solidarity; nonreciprocal T is the period-fiction insult.
  - Politeness logic differs by culture: Brown & Levinson (1987) model strategic face-work (volitional); Ide (1989) contrasts wakimae ("discernment") — politeness as grammatically prescribed fit, read off social variables. Pick which logic a culture runs and "rudeness" itself changes shape.
- Avoidance registers: Dyirbal speakers use guwal (everyday) in most contexts and switch wholly to jalnguy (a complete replacement lexicon) in the presence of taboo kin (Dixon 1980). Parallels: names of the dead tabooed, hunter circumlocutions for dangerous game. Couples straight to myth-symbol.

## 5. Class, caste, and linguistic capital

- U/non-U English (Ross 1954; popularized by Mitford 1956): class indexed by tiny, arbitrary lexical choices (napkin = U, serviette = non-U). Lesson: class registers live in low-frequency, high-symbolic items. 20–40 seeded words per stratum carries a whole class system in fiction.
- Bernstein (1971) restricted/elaborated "codes": keep the usable observation (schools and bureaucracies reward one code) and drop the deficit hierarchy — Labov (1969) showed nonstandard varieties are grammatically complete and logically rigorous; what varies is which code the market values. See Calibration.
- Bourdieu (1991): linguistic capital — the legitimate language is an asset whose exchange rate is set by institutions. Practical engine: a character's speech is a balance sheet; courts, schools, and temples are the exchange counters where accents are converted into profit or loss.
- Caste-coded varieties: Brahmin vs non-Brahmin spoken Tamil (Schiffman 1999); classical Nahuatl reverential register as noble/market stratification, later eroding (Lockhart 1992; Hill & Hill 1986); Javanese levels as quasi-caste (above). Preservation mechanism: endogamy + ritual office + separate schooling maintain a caste acrolect.
- Standard language ideology (Lippi-Green 1997): the "standard" is a dialect with an army and a school system behind it. In-world, someone wrote the grammar and profits from it — a worldbuilder should know who, and what accent the villain's clerk corrects.

## 6. Gendered speech

- Sex-exclusive morphology: Yana men's and women's forms (Sapir 1929) — and men use women's forms when quoting women. Quotative indexicality is a strong trick: reported speech keeps its gendered register even in a man's mouth.
- Sex-preferential: Japanese joseigo/danseigo — pronouns, sentence-final particles, "refined" vocabulary. Real, but ideological as much as behavioral; the ideology polices the practice (Okamoto & Shibamoto-Smith 2004). Onna-kotoba is performed identity, not biology.
- Deficit framing: Lakoff (1975) framed "women's language" as powerless language; O'Barr & Atkins (1980) found courtroom powerless features track social power, not sex — low-power men use them too. Design rule: make power the underlying dial; map gender onto it locally (sumptuary-style, where law or custom demands it).
- Variationist correction: sex alone predicts poorly — local social categories do better (Eckert 2000: jock/burnout adolescents); communities of practice (Eckert & McConnell-Ginet 1992) are the right grain for fictional speech communities. Covert prestige is gendered too (Trudgill 1972), with contested detail (see Calibration).
- Amount-of-talk gender claims (women/men talk more) are unstable across studies (James & Drakich 1993) — do not rest a culture's characterization on them.
- Design kit, in ascending force: sex-preferential usage → gendered politeness asymmetry (wakimae logic) → sex-exclusive morphology (Yana logic) → gendered register as sumptuary law with sanctions.

## 7. Age-grading and slang

- Apparent time vs real time (Labov 1972; 1994): a generational difference in a synchronic survey means either change in progress or age-grading (each generation repeats the same behavior). Decide which before building a timeline; individuals do also move mid-life (Sankoff & Blondeau 2007, Montreal French /r/), but slowly.
- The adolescent peak: vernacular innovation and slang turnover peak in mid-adolescence (Labov 1972; Eble 1996). Elder speech = yesterday's slang plus frozen archaisms — a visible age clock your readers can read without explanation.
- Era anchor: age-graded registers (formal elder speech at rites of passage) are plausible in small-scale societies; youth slang as a market force (music, fashion, tech) belongs to mass society. Generational address terms (kinship-based honorifics for strangers) are near-universal and cheap to seed.

## 8. Mapping register to era and social structure

| Social structure | Dominant variation mechanism | H variety | Era anchor | Anachronism to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Clan / segmentary | taboo + kin-avoidance registers, chiefly oratory | none (oral "H" = formulaic liturgy) | neolithic–iron | an H/L split with no scribal class |
| Temple-state / script empire | diglossia: liturgical H vs market L | dead or translocal sacred tongue | bronze–classical | H readable by everyone |
| Feudal / estate | T/V, estate registers, court style | cosmopolitan foreign language | medieval | flat vassal–lord address |
| Absolutist / bureaucratic | honorific pyramids, chancery style | chancellery standard | early modern | flat politeness everywhere |
| Colonial / post-creole | acrolect–mesolect–basilect by schooling | colonizer language | early modern–industrial | continuum without institutional violence |
| Industrial mass-nation | dialect vs standard pressure, class accents | national standard | 19th–20th c. | intact dialects under universal schooling |
| Networked / converged | koines, anti-language churn, CMC registers | global lingua franca layer | 20th–21st c. | no in-group code for networked youth |

Cross-cutting era levers: literacy rate, migration rate, conscription, religious schism, broadcasting, and whether H is dead. Change any one and registers move within a generation.

## 9. Construction procedure

1. Choose era mechanics: who writes, who reads, who moves. Migration makes koines; schools make standards; temples make H.
2. Place the H: sacred? bureaucratic? foreign? alive or dead? Whoever controls access to H controls legitimation (Bourdieu). Decide its interpreters' power.
3. Draw 2–3 isogloss bundles on the map; anchor each at an old border, river, or schism. Add one focal area (innovation city) and one relic area (conservative periphery).
4. Define 3–5 speech communities as communities of practice — guild, order, regiment, market caste, youth scene — not as abstract class labels.
5. For each community: 1 stereotype feature (openly mocked), 3–6 markers (unconscious), 1 register signature (replacement lexicon, honorific set, or jargon list). Anti-language groups need overlexicalization zones naming their obsession.
6. Write the metadiscourse: etiquette manuals, satire, proverbs mocking others' speech, censorship lists — this is what enregisters the features. Decide who polices: mother-in-law, censor, academy, schoolmarm, algorithm.
7. Stress-test scenes: wedding, courtroom, market, confession, battle-camp, deathbed. Table every speaker/listener pair and pick codes; any empty cell exposes a missing social institution.

Worked micro-example (compressed): a river kingdom with a dead temple-tongue H (liturgy + contracts). Nobles slide mid-sentence down Badawi's ladder like educated Arabic speakers; the soldiery runs an anti-language overlexical on wounds and cowardice; oaths retain women's honorific particles (sumptuary residue of an older priestesshood); the capital's koine has leveled three isoglosses in sixty years while one relic valley keeps the old dative — in-world philologists date strata by it.

## 10. Calibration: contested, discredited, and commonly botched

- Bernstein's restricted/elaborated codes as class deficit: discredited (Labov 1969). Usable residue: institutions reward one code; that is a fact about the institution, not the speakers.
- Lakoff's "women's language = powerless language": reframed by O'Barr & Atkins (1980) — power, not sex, is the operative variable.
- "Women talk more/less" claims: unstable evidence (James & Drakich 1993). Avoid.
- "Women's language" as natural expression: treat as ideology; the practice is performed and policed (Okamoto & Shibamoto-Smith 2004).
- "Dialect = decayed standard": wrong. Dialects descend from older varieties independently; the standard is the latecomer (Chambers & Trudgill 1998). Prescriptivism inside a world is fine — as a character trait and an institution, not as narratorial truth (Lippi-Green 1997).
- Diglossia ≠ bilingualism (Fishman 1967); diglossia is stable for centuries unless an institution moves. Also the creole continuum is not universal across post-colonies.
- Ferguson's original H/L conditions are a prototype, not law; even "classic" cases (Swiss German) are argued over. Use the ladder (Badawi) rather than the two-box reading.
- Radio/TV as dialect-killers: overclaimed; migration, labor markets, and schooling do the leveling.
- "Small-scale languages have no registers": backwards. Small-scale languages often have *more* obligatory register work — avoidance speech, respect forms, taboo replacements (Dixon 1980).
- T/V reading: the nonreciprocal pattern is superior gives T and receives V — fiction routinely inverts it.
