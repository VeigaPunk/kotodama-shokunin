**Why this is not filler.** Divination is the load-bearing institution in fourteen civilization bases of this corpus, and it is the one myth-adjacent system that *produces institutions, offices, archives, registers and legal procedure* rather than only beliefs. A worldbuilder who has a pantheon and a cosmography but no reading technology has a world where nobody can ask the gods anything — which is historically the rare case. The entry also fills the lane's own namesake gap: 言霊 *kotodama* is a claim about the power of words, and the chance-utterance omen (Greek *klēdōn*), the oracle's verse, the chanted verse corpus of Ifa and the norito are exactly the places where that claim is operationalized (§7).

**What it does not claim.** No canon; no assertion that any divination system "works" in the sense of predicting the future; no claim to have retrieved the cited works in full (§"Limits" below).

## 2. Core frame — what a divination system is made of

A divination system is five slots, and a worldbuilder designs all five or inherits a contradiction:

1. **Question** — what may be asked, in what form. Roman *auspicia* were yes/no per action ("should this assembly proceed?"), not general forecasts; Azande poison-oracle questions were propositions the oracle was asked to kill or spare the fowl over (Evans-Pritchard 1937); Ifa questions are open and the answer is a *verse* that must be applied (Bascom 1969).
2. **Technique** — the physical procedure that constrains chance: cast lots, burn a bone, read a liver, watch birds, count days, sleep in a shrine.
3. **Sign space** — the closed inventory of possible readings. This is the design-critical slot: a *closed* code (0–5 floating shells; 16 Ifa figures; 12 liver regions) is disputable, teachable, and institutional; an *open* code (any dream, any utterance) needs a strong interpretive office to hold it together.
4. **Reader** — the licensed interpreter, and therefore an office, a training path, a fee, and a faction.
5. **Output** — prediction *and* prescription. The historically dominant output is prescriptive: sacrifice, wait, substitute, avoid, re-ask, pay. A system that only predicts has nothing to do after the answer; a system that prescribes has an institution's whole work queue (§5).

Two classical design axes, labelled as **etic** tools (the emic/etic rule is stated in `comparative-frameworks.md` §1 and its overclaim checklist in §7):

| Axis | Pole A — inspired / natural | Pole B — technical / artificial |
|---|---|---|
| Classical name (Cicero, *De Div.* 1) | *divinatio naturalis*: frenzy, dreams, oracular possession | *divinatio artificiosa*: lots, entrails, birds, stars |
| Where the sign comes from | a person or a state of person (medium, dreamer, prophet) | a physical configuration read against a fixed key |
| Institutional shape | sanctuary, cult, prophet's following | guild, office, archive, textbook |
| Failure mode | capture by a charismatic (or by the state that hosts him) | ossification: a code that outlives its question |
| Design use | gives a world its plot engine and its dissenters | gives it its bureaucracy, archives, and legal machinery |

The pair is Cicero's own (and his brother's rebuttal in book 2 is the ancient sceptic's case) — use it as a design axis, not as an emic self-description, and do not assume a culture picks one pole: Rome ran both simultaneously, with different legal status for each (§5).

**The vocabulary trap.** English carries a fossil stratum from exactly this institution: *auspicious* and *auspices* (< *avis* + *specere*, "bird-watching"), *omen*, *monster* (< *monstrum*, the sign that warns), *portent*, *prodigy* (< *prodigium*), *sinister* (the left-hand sign), *augur*, *haruspex*, *divine*. Cicero glosses the four omen-words in *De Div.* 1.93 as a graded set (that which warns / displays / foretells / portends) — a ready-made four-term omen vocabulary for a fictional culture, and a reminder that a culture's omen words encode *which kind of sign* it recognizes, not just "sign".

## 3. Technique typology — fourteen families with real exemplars

Rows 1–14 are not mutually exclusive; real systems stack them (Ezekiel's Babylonian king uses three at once; Rome ran augury, haruspicy, the Sibylline books, and prodigy procedure as four separate legal channels with different custodians).

## 4. The interpretive engine — seven rules that make a reading socially work

1. **Closure.** The sign space must be small enough that disputants can enumerate it. This is the same closure principle the symbol register files use (`symbolism-synthesis-method.md` §1, §3: a grammar, not an encyclopedia) applied to procedure. Consequence for fiction: an oracle with an unbounded sign space cannot be *disputed*, so it can only be obeyed or destroyed — it is a plot device, not an institution.
2. **Paired charges.** Shang diviners often posed the same proposition in positive and negative form (Keightley 1978); the pair makes the record self-checking and the reading non-trivial. Design lever: a culture that poses paired charges has a *rhetoric of fairness* already built into its ritual paperwork.
3. **The double-check.** The Azande second-oracle procedure re-asks the same proposition in a differently administered run; a contradiction is itself a sign (the oracle is refusing, or a taboo is active) — not evidence the method fails (Evans-Pritchard 1937). This is the single most useful institutional detail in the whole literature: it converts a failed reading into *more* procedure.
4. **Procedural escape.** Failure is attributed to the procedure, not the god or the code: the day was wrong, the reader was impure, the birds would not feed, the question was badly formed. Every surviving system has this valve; a fictional system without one dies at its first visible failure.
5. **Cost.** A consultation costs something (a fee, a fowl, a tide-wait, a pilgrimage), so answers carry stakes and clients do not consult idly. Cost is also the mechanism that makes oracle-shopping visible: multiple oracles in one city produce competing institutions and a market in verdicts.
6. **Prescription as the output.** The reading ends with an action: offer, substitute, wait, avoid the road, re-ask next month. This is what gives the reader institution its *work*: the sacrifice economy, the substitute-king ritual (see `celestial-symbolism-calendars.md` §7 for the Assyrian *šar pūḫi*, which belongs to that file and is only cross-referenced here), the amulet trade, the calendar of prohibited days.
7. **Dispute procedure.** Who may contest a reading, and how? Rome's answer was legal: *obnuntiatio* let a magistrate declare bad signs and block proceedings — a formal power, abused by Bibulus in 59 BCE to invalidate his co-consul Caesar's legislation (Suetonius, *Div. Iul.* 20; Dio 38.6). Design lever: if a world's divination is disputable, it is political; if it is not disputable, it is not a social system but a narrator's voice.

**Question-shape table** (what a system can be asked, and what that does to the plot):

| Question shape | Example system | Answer shape | Narrative consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Binary, per action | Roman auspices; Azande proposition | yes / no / refusal | permits a *veto*; supports procedural obstruction |
| Which-of-n | Shang "which ancestor?"; oracle of the lot | one of n named options | assigns blame or cost to a named party |
| When / how long | day-count systems; intercalation queries | a date or an interval | couples divination to the calendar file |
| What did I do wrong | Ifa; Azande witchcraft diagnosis | a prescription (offer X) | generates the offering economy and the confession scene |
| Open guidance | Delphi; istikhāra | an image, a verse, a felt resolution | requires a strong interpretive office; high plot yield, low auditability |

## 5. Institutions — who holds the reading license

| Slot | Real exemplar | How it is staffed | What it does to a world |
|---|---|---|---|
| Citizen-magistrate diviner | Roman augur (patrician college; the *ius auspiciorum* of magistrates) | public office, political career path | divination as *constitutional* procedure; the office-holder can be a faction leader |
| Imported professional guild | Etruscan haruspices; the imperial *ordo* of sixty (Pallottino 1975; Beard/North/Price 1998) | ethnicity/guild, then state retainer | a two-tier system: the citizen reads the sky, the foreigner reads the liver — useful for designing an alien specialist class inside a polity |
| State oracle | Delphi in the classical period; the Tibetan state oracle (Nechung) | sanctuary + attending priesthood + civic patrons | transregional diplomacy; the oracle as an addressable political actor |
| Court diviner | Shang royal diviners and scribes; Aztec day-count readers | attached to the palace | the king's monopoly on asking; records in the palace archive |
| Itinerant professional | Ifa *babalawo*; Arabic-script geomancers (*ʿilm al-raml*) | training + initiation, client fees, no fixed temple | a market in readings; knowledge as portable literature |
| Archive custodian | the Roman *quindecimviri sacris faciundis* guarding the Sibylline books; the Chinese omen bureau (司天監/欽天監) | board of priests or a bureaucratic bureau | **custody of a text is power**: who may consult, and who may *read* the archive, is a legal question |
| Arbiter | Azande poison oracle; oath-and-ordeal procedures | oracle operator + witnesses + chief's court | divination fused to law; the verdict is evidence |
| Dream-sanctuary | Asclepieia; incubation shrines | priests, physicians, votive inscriptions | a healing institution with a case archive (the *iamata*) |

Two craft notes. (a) **Heredity vs. training** is the sharpest single design choice: a hereditary office (Etruscan families, K'iche' daykeepers) preserves an archaic register and resists reform; a trained guild (Ifa initiatic networks) can expand, travel, and standardize. (b) **The archive is the real prize.** The Roman pontifical record (*annales maximi*, mined by later historians for prodigy notices — Beard/North/Price 1998) and the Chinese 五行志 treatises show that omen-keeping produces *the state's memory*: a fictional polity with 300 years of dated omen entries has historiography, chronology, and a priesthood that must maintain the secret of the cycle.

## 6. Omen archives and their byproducts

- **The compendium form.** *Enūma Anu Enlil* (astral), *Šumma ālu* (terrestrial), *Šumma izbu* (birth anomalies) are organized as protasis/apodosis chains ("if X, then Y"). Rochberg (2004) argues against reading them as "religion" opposed to "science": the same scribal enterprise that kept the omen lists produced the astronomy. Use this as a design instruction — the omen bureau and the observatory are one office, and their *separation* is a later reform you can date.
- **The omen list as a data genre.** Entries preserve observed correlations (eclipse dates, birth anomalies, weather) inside a divinatory frame. Consequence: an omen archive is the most plausible pre-modern *data* institution a fictional world can have, and the natural place to hide a plot's evidence (a scribe who notices a pattern the theory does not predict).
- **Eclipse and prodigy archives as chronology.** The 15 June 763 BCE solar eclipse in the Assyrian eponymy of Bur-Sagale anchors Neo-Assyrian absolute chronology by retrocalculation; Roman prodigy lists (Livy, *passim*) supply dated events for Republican history. Cross-reference `celestial-symbolism-calendars.md` §7 — that file owns the astronomy, rituals, and the substitute-king mechanism; this entry owns only the *archive-as-institution* point.
- **The byproduct rule.** Every omen institution produces a second, unintended product: astronomy from astral omens, historiography from prodigy lists, medicine from incubation case archives, law from oracle arbitration, and script literacy from the Shang bone archive (Keightley 1978). When designing, ask what the archive *accidentally* becomes — that is usually the most interesting thing in the world.

## 7. The word-omen channel — where this lane's namesake actually lives

言霊 *kotodama* (the Man'yōshū formula "the land where kotodama brings blessing") asserts that words carry effective power. Divination is where that assertion becomes procedure:

- **The chance utterance (*klēdōn*).** A word overheard is taken as the answer — the cheapest oracle in the world and the one that needs no institution. *Odyssey* 17 (a sneeze read as a sign) and 20 (the mill-woman's prayer-words); Xenophon's sneeze at *Anabasis* 3.2. Design lever: in a culture with word-omens, *ambient speech becomes evidence* — and the fiction must then police who may speak near a rite (couples `../linguistics/politeness-taboo-euphemism.md`).
- **Omen names.** A name taken from a sign (the child named for the omen of his birth; the throne name chosen from a dream; the Roman *omen nominis* in the naming of an undertaking). Couples `../linguistics/naming-systems.md`: name magic and divination are the same claim about words, applied at different moments.
- **The oracle's own register.** Delphic responses in verse, later in prose — Plutarch, himself a Delphic priest, treats the decline of verse as a symptom (*De Pythiae oraculis*). Ifa verses are chanted in an archaic Yoruba register and constitute a memorized literary corpus (Bascom 1969; Abimbola 1976). Norito are fixed liturgical formulas (*Engishiki*, 927 CE; trans. Philippi 1959). Craft rule: **an oracle speaks in a marked register**, and the register's archaism is datable evidence — a fictional oracle whose speech drifts toward the vernacular has a decline story built in.
- **Political word-prophecy.** The Han 讖緯 *chènwěi* prognostic-apocryphal texts were politically explosive (used in Wang Mang's accession; restricted in later dynasties — Dull 1966): verse prophecy as a weapon and a censorship target. This is the plot engine for a world where *texts* are oracles.
- **Calibration.** The modern Japanese *kotodama* discourse (national language ideology of the 1930s–40s) is a 20th-century construction, not a continuation of the Man'yōshū usage (Miller 1982). Keep the classical formula and the modern ideology in separate files of your world's history.

## 9. Worked design example (NON-CANON — a design exercise, not lore)

**The brine-cast of a tidal mercantile city.** Question shapes allowed: sail/await, which cargo to load, which partner to trust, whether to re-ask. Technique: five marked shells cast into a brine basin at slack water; the sign is the count of shells that float (0–5) crossed with which basin (floodward/ebbward) — a closed 12-outcome code, published on a stone at the quay so that any merchant can enumerate it (closure, §4.1). The code maps to twelve prescriptions: *sail*, *await one tide*, *split the cargo*, *offer at the sea-gate*, *substitute a crew member for the voyage* (a substitution logic borrowed from sacrifice typology), *re-ask at the next slack*, etc. Two readers: a hereditary tide-reader (keeps the archaic register) and a guild second-reader who must countersign any verdict on a cargo above a declared value — the two-tier office, invented from the Roman augur/haruspex split (§5). Anti-error rule: a counter-cast asks the opposite proposition; a contradiction voids the day and costs the client a second fee (§4.3, §4.5). Archive: every verdict is tallied beside the voyage's actual outcome in a tally-book kept by the second-reader's guild — which over three centuries becomes the city's chronicle, its insurance register, and the first place anyone looks for a pattern the code does not predict (§6).

Design levers demonstrated: closed code, prescription output, two-reader dispute rule, cost, archive-as-byproduct, and a hereditary/mercantile tension that generates faction politics without any new mythology.

## 11. Calibration — contested, discredited, and misused

- **The Delphic vapours.** The ancient story that the Pythia sat over a chasm of intoxicating vapour is itself ancient (Strabo 9.3.5; Plutarch; Diodorus), and de Boer, Hale & Chanton (2001) proposed a geological mechanism (fault-line gases at the site). The proposal is **contested** in the geological literature and the ancient "chasm" description is an ancient explanation, not an observation. Do not assert the mechanism; if you use it in fiction, use it as the *in-world theory* the priests hold.
- **"Oracles were always ambiguous."** A pop simplification. Fontenrose (1978) classified the surviving Delphic responses and argued that most historical responses are not ambiguous at all, and that many famous "oracles" (Croesus and the Halys, Herodotus 1.53; the wooden wall, 7.141) are legendary or post-eventum narratives. Keep the ambiguity *technique* (it is excellent for fiction) but do not attribute it to Delphi as a settled fact.
- **Dodds' dream evolutionism.** *The Greeks and the Irrational* (1951) ch. 4 remains the standard entry point for Greek dream-culture and incubation, but its developmental sequence (objective → symbolic dreams) is a mid-century evolutionism; use the material, label the frame.
- **The Azande "closed system."** Evans-Pritchard's account (1937) is the classic, but the reading that the Azande *cannot* see contradictions is a construction of the later rationality debate (Winch 1964; Wilson ed. 1970) as much as of the ethnography. The safe craft lesson is procedural (the second-oracle rule), not psychological.
- **Rune-casting.** The divinatory rune system is a modern esoteric construction (Blum 1982); the medieval record does not attest runic divination (Page 1987). Tacitus' *Germania* 10 describes lots with marked twigs, not the futhark. Calibration flag: any world where "the runes were always read" is importing a 20th-century system.
- **Geomancy's origin.** The 16-figure sand-geomancy (*ʿilm al-raml*) and Ifa's 16/256 structure are structurally parallel; the diffusion direction (Islamic world → West Africa, or independent/earlier African development) is **contested** (Maupoil 1943; Pemberton ed. 2000). Present the parallel, not a single origin.
- **"Divination is proto-science."** Half-true and dangerous: Rochberg (2004) shows the Babylonian omen enterprise and early astronomy are inseparable, but the Whiggish reading ("they were really doing science") and the dismissal ("mere superstition") are both distortions. Design with the *institution* (one bureau, two outputs), not with a verdict on its rationality.
- **The 2012 phenomenon.** The 13th *b'ak'tun* completion (2012) rests on real inscriptions (e.g., Tortuguero Monument 6 — Aveni 2009), but the "Maya prophecy" as a mass movement is a late-20th-century construction (Argüelles 1987; Sitler 2006). Use it as the template for *modern* date-divination, and keep the ancient evidence and the modern movement in separate layers.
- **"Panbabylonian" astral religion.** The claim that Babylonian astral religion explains world mythologies is discredited; see the same flag in `flood-traditions.md` §7. Cross-reference rather than repeat.
- **The shaman catch-all.** Do not label every diviner a shaman; the specialist slots in §5 are distinct offices with distinct training, and conflating them is the standard error of pop comparative religion.
- **Whorfian and stage-based readings of omen language.** Treat the "primitive mind"/"pre-logical" framing (Lévy-Bruhl and its heirs) as discredited; the emic/etic rule in `comparative-frameworks.md` §1 governs.

## 12. Build order and failure modes

1. **Pick the question shapes first** (§4 table): binary veto, which-of-n, when, what-went-wrong, open guidance. The question shape determines the institution more than the technique does.
2. **Fix the sign space and publish it**: 12 outcomes, 16 figures, 260 days. Write the table the disputants would know; closure is the credibility mechanism.
3. **Choose the technique's cost profile**: a fowl, a fee, a tide, a night in a shrine. Cost is what makes readings scarce and readers professional.
4. **Staff two readers with different tenure** (hereditary + trained, or citizen + foreign) and give them a countersignature rule. This single move generates the faction politics for free.
5. **Give the system one anti-error rule** (counter-cast, paired charges, second oracle) and one procedural escape (taboo, impurity, bad day). Without both, the first failed prophecy ends the institution.
6. **Make the output prescriptive**: the reading ends in an offering, a wait, a substitution, or a re-ask — then build the offering economy and the prohibited-day calendar around it.
7. **Site the archive**: who keeps the tally-book, who may consult it, and what else it becomes (chronicle, insurance register, astronomical table, medical casebook).
8. **Date the reforms**: the separation of observatory from omen bureau, the ban on private reading, the replacement of verse by prose — three datable reforms that make the system a history rather than a fixture.
9. **Stage the polemic**: an internal critique (Cicero's *De Div.* 2; Cato's quip about haruspices laughing at each other), a rival technique, and an imported specialist class. Every real system has all three.
10. **Failure modes to avoid**: an oracle with no closed code (undisputable, therefore unplayable); a system with no procedural escape (dies at first failure); a diviner who is never wrong and never doubted (not an institution, a narrator); sign spaces copied wholesale from one real culture onto a fictional one without the frame-of-reference and color-capacity checks (`symbolism-synthesis-method.md` §4.5); ritual detail lifted from living traditions instead of invented; and *retrospective prophecy* used to make a faction look right — the readers of a world should be as accountable as its generals.
