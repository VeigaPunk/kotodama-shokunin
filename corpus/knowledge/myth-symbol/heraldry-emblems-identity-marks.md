## 3. What does the mark name? (the bearer unit)

Decide this first; every later rule depends on it.

| Bearer unit | Worked case from the rows | Consequence for design |
|---|---|---|
| one person and descendants | arms granted or confirmed "to one person and their descendants in the legitimate male line" (S419, Q. Do coats of arms belong to surnames?); in Scotland "all Arms are individual to a person" (S421, served text) | no surname arms (§11); in Scotland a cadet matriculates with a difference (S420) |
| one crest, several unrelated grantees | Fox-Davies reports a Scottish practice in which the same crest "regularly is granted to half-a-dozen people of as many different surnames" (S413, chapter on crests, served text) | a crest alone does not prove kinship (C: one author's report of one jurisdiction) |
| a clan of unrelated families | in medieval Poland a coat set off a group from the wider community and gave it boundaries; Bieniak reads knightly clans as political factions and solidarity groups (S435, §1 and §2) | a shared coat is evidence of affiliation, not of kinship |
| an office | the Notitia Dignitatum pairs each office with its insignia and never names the incumbent (S448, served text); Mamluk emblems, "so far as we can understand them", with very few exceptions represented the office held by the amir (S446, served text) | the mark changes hands with the post |
| an affinity or following | Lancastrian kings gave silver or gold collars of SS to chief supporters, and lesser supporters wore pewter badges of the same shape (S447, served text) | rank shows in material, membership in shape |
| a community or institution | a priory's common seal paired its patron with a claimed founder and shields of arms (S442, served text); a roll carries 28 flags of bishoprics and monasteries (S425, served text) | corporate marks argue for a history as well as identify |
| property | Montana: livestock "must be branded with the Montana recorded brand" (S436, served text); a house mark was the main means by which property was recognized (S439, served text) | registration, not artistry, gives the mark force |
| a hereditary crest right | Northwest Coast crests represent "the history, and lineage of a family or an individual" and rights to them are carefully defined (S450, served text) | treat only through the holders' statements (§13) |

## 4. Who grants, registers and polices it? (the authority regime)

| Regime | Rows | What a story can do with it |
|---|---|---|
| a court of law grants and registers | in Scotland a person seeking arms petitions the Lord Lyon, whose court is a court of law; recorded arms are protected under Scots law (S420, served text) | usurping arms is a legal wrong, not a faux pas |
| statute with penalties on the carrier | the 1672 Act calls unlicensed use "expresly discharged", makes the Lyon's register "the true and unrepeallable rule", and escheats goods bearing usurped arms (S422, served text) | the cup or door bearing false arms can be forfeit |
| delegated crown officers grant on petition | Kings of Arms grant by letters patent on powers delegated by the Crown; the petition ("memorial") goes to the Earl Marshal (S418, Granting of Arms) | a courtier stands between applicant and grant |
| an honour assessed on merit | in Canada approval "is not automatic", applications are assessed on merit, and a grant is an honour from the Crown (S423, served text) | the grant marks standing; paying fees guarantees nothing |
| a state registry with inspection at movement | brands are read and recorded by the state; inspection is required at sale, county-line crossing and slaughter (S436, S437, served text); Colorado administers about 30,000 brands through a board its livestock industry formed about 1865 (S438, served text) | ownership disputes are settled by the register |
| a heraldry society's design guidance for public bodies | Norwegian Heraldry Society guidance: municipal arms should preferably have two tinctures and one figure, describable in blazon (S445, served text) | civic arms as recognition devices, not logos |
| a church instruction | the 1969 instruction abolished the cardinals' red hat as dress while allowing cardinals and bishops arms that are "simple and clear" (S443, AAS p. 336 §9 and §28) | dress and device change on separate schedules |
| self-assumed, sometimes granted | in Japan badges were at first assumed at will; later chieftains also conferred badges on retainers who distinguished themselves (S428, page 4) | grant by a lord can be a late addition beside self-chosen marks (T for the origin story) |

## 5. The grammar of a European blazon

- The metals are or (gold) and argent (silver); gules, azure, vert, purpure and sable are colours; furs (ermine, vair) are a third class (S413, p. 70). Scottish practice writes the names Or, Argent, Sable, Azure, Gules, Vert (S421, served text).
- Rule of tincture: no colour on colour, no metal on metal (S413, p. 85; S421, "Basic rule - no metal on a metal no colours on a colour"). Furs and charges "proper" may lie on either, and well-known British coats break the rule (S413, p. 85 and p. 86): use the rule as a contrast check, and an exception as a mark of antiquity (C).
- Order: name the field first, then the principal ordinary, then the charges in relation to it (S416, s.v. "Blazon"); the vocabulary derives largely from "old Norman French of the thirteenth and fourteenth century" (S416, Introduction). This is a British grammar, not a template for every emblem system.
- The verbal blazon, not the painting, governs a granted design, so one blazon may be rendered in many styles (S418, Granting of Arms). A blazon refers back instead of repeating a tincture: "of the First" means the first colour named (S421, served text).
- Beast attitudes are fixed terms: rampant erect, passant walking, sejant seated, guardant facing the viewer (S413, chapters on beasts, served text); Scottish usage adds regardant and dormant (S421, served text). Fox-Davies treats the lion/leopard split as a later French terminology that became obsolete in British armory (S413, served text).
- The crest is the device on top of the helm, not the whole achievement (S417, s.v. Crest; S419, FAQ on the crest); crests began as ensigns of high honour restricted to a few persons (S417, served text).

## 7. Office and rank emblems (mostly not descending)

- Late Roman: a commander's insignia show the distinctive shields of the troops under him, probably emblazoned on the officers' commissions (S448, served text). Hedge: every pattern in the manuscripts is drawn round, the document never labels the discs as shields, and the identification rests on outside objects (S449, served text) — C.
- Mamluk: the emblem named the office; the blazon was hereditary among amirs, yet sons who chose an ecclesiastical or administrative career apparently had no right to it, and Mayer counts five simple blazons with polo-sticks tied to named persons (S446, served text). Route period detail to `civilizations/mamluk.md:126`.
- Catholic hierarchy: the red hat hangs tassels in triangles, five rows for cardinals fixed only in 1832; patriarchs, primates and archbishops a green hat with four rows and a patriarchal cross (S444, §Cardinals and §Archbishops); the 1969 reform kept the right to arms while abolishing the red hat in dress (S443, AAS §9) — date-check any hat before reading rank off it.
- Chinese rank badges: a pair of square badges embroidered with a golden pheasant, "the insignia of a civil official of the second rank", on a ground of water, waves, rocks and clouds (S454, object description); the creature names the grade, not the family. Dated grade lists belong to `civilizations/china-ming.md:86`.
- Affinity badges: the collar of SS as patronage made visible, silver or gold for chief supporters, pewter for lesser ones (S447, served text).
- Imperial and shogunal crests: the chrysanthemum incised on a sword tang "in order to encourage a martial spirit among his subjects", on a blade said to be the cloistered emperor Gotoba's (S431, served text; the attribution is T); the Tokugawa hollyhock used by a metalworking line "with Tokugawa permission" (S432, served text) — restriction worked by licensed exception.

## 8. House, merchant and livestock marks

- A writer unable to write could set a house mark as his hand-sign; marks survived on Erfurt houses into the nineteenth century; the mark was the main means of recognizing property (S439, served text). Michelsen reports the von Gagern knightly arms as the family's former house mark (S439, served text) — a mark can climb into heraldry (C: one case, an 1853 claim).
- A merchant's mark can sit in a shield on a sheriff's memorial brass beside a carding comb, the tool of the trade (S440, served text).
- Brands: read left to right, top to bottom, outside in; "Lazy A" names a letter lying on its side (S437, served text). The vocabulary favours fast reading over expressive iconography.
- Tamga: "signs of group identity" of medieval Turkic nomads, on steles, coins, harness and rock registers called tamgalytas (S434, abstract); in the Great Salbyk barrow late tamgas run "in narrow stripes along the edges" (S433, abstract page). Tamga are not a writing system (§11).

## 12. Build order for a fictional mark system

1. Pick the bearer unit for each mark class (§3); one culture may run several at once (office, affinity and lineage marks coexisted in late medieval England: S447, S413).
2. Pick the authority regime (§4) and decide what usurpation costs: nothing, scandal, fine, forfeiture (S422).
3. Fix the grammar: palette and contrast rule (S413), word order (S416), or a non-heraldic grammar such as stroke-added tamga (S434) or read-order brands (S437).
4. Fix transmission: cadency, matriculation, marriage marshalling, or shared clan coats (§6).
5. Assign carriers and who may see them (§9); decide which carriers carry legal force (a seal, S441) and which only display (a flag, S452).
6. Date every layer (§10) and run the calibration bank (§11) before handing off.
