# Ordeal and Judicial Proof Rituals — what a culture does when the oath runs out: the proof ladder, the ordeal menu, the administrator's economy, the inspection of the body, and the two exits (condemnation and paid-for acquittal)

## 2. Six design decisions

| decision | what it fixes | design lever | anchor |
|---|---|---|---|
| reach | which disputes may enter a proof at all | every proof system has a docket: what is too small, too high, or too sacred to test | §3 a |
| carrier | who may carry which proof | rank, sex and age carve-outs decide who may swear alone, who needs oath-helpers, who must go to the element | §3 a |
| boundary | where the oath stops and the body starts | the vindicatory oath is only the preliminary: a believed oath settles, a doubted oath invites "deny my oath and fight me" | §3 b |
| element | which ordeal decides, and its falsifiable grammar | the element needs a rule that can fail: a fixed step-count with the iron, a fixed soak for the water, burn-traces read from bound hands | §3 c, §3 e |
| administration | who runs the ordeal and who profits | the priest blesses, fasts and adjures; the record keeps the reversals; the same office that guarantees the element also controls the trickery that defeats it | §3 d, §3 f |
| exit | what each verdict costs, and how the system ends | acquittal can be produced (official trickery survives disbelief as mountebank performance); the ladder decays charter by charter, and the battle proof's heir is the duel of honour | §3 f, §3 g |

## 3. Attested anchors

- S901: the Frisian code's pleader line states the boundary in one breath — the oath is only the preliminary to proof by the judgment of God: "I swear alone, if thou darest, deny my oath and fight me" — a worldbuilding proof system can be written as the answers cultures give to that dare.
- S901: the red-hot iron could displace the battle proof itself: when Poppo converted Harold Blaatand by the ordeal of red-hot iron in 965, the effect was strong enough to substitute that mode of trial for the previously existing wager of battle — an ordeal can be the reform candidate, not just the relic.
- S901: the ladder decays unevenly, charter by charter: in the laws of Ghent (1178) there is no allusion to any species of ordeal, while in the charter of Nieuport (1163), the ordeal of red-hot iron and compurgatorial oaths are freely alluded to as means of rebutting accusations, with no reference to the battle trial, "showing that it must then have been no longer in use".
- S902: the battle proof has a birth statute: the first legal establishment of these ordeals is found in the laws of Gundebald, King of the Burgundians, A.D. 501 — a fictional system can be dated the same way, by the statute that made combat a proof.
- S902: Gundebald's stated rationale is the distrust of oaths themselves: subjects were held to be corrupted by avarice or hurried on by obstinacy, so as to attest by oath what they knew not or knew to be false — the ordeal is legislated as an anti-perjury device, and its defendant-swears / plaintiff-demands mechanics are recorded in the same law.
- S903: the iron ordeal's grammar is falsifiable by design: in a Scandinavian law the red-hot iron is carried nine steps — the element's rule is a fixed, countable act, which is what makes the verdict readable.
- S903: the inspection is part of the rite: the examination of the hands to see if they show traces of burning, and the binding them up in leaves — the ordeal includes the examination protocol, not just the carrying.
- S903: the system outlives its own disbelief: the fire-ordeals survive as mountebank performance, keeping up the same means of trickery that were in official use when the accused was to be acquitted — an acquittal machine can become a paying show without changing its script.

## 4. Craft checklist

- [ ] Write the docket first: three disputes (one too small, one too high, one testable) and say which reach a proof.
- [ ] Name the carriers: for at least two ranks, what each may carry (oath alone, oath plus oath-helpers, the element, the battle).
- [ ] Mark the boundary line: the sentence a pleader says when an oath is doubted (the S901 dare is the period pattern).
- [ ] Give the element a falsifiable rule: a step-count, a soak, a walk — something a record can check.
- [ ] Write the inspection protocol: what is examined, when, and by whom, before the verdict is read.
- [ ] Price both exits: what acquittal costs the party, the administrator and the record; what condemnation costs the body and the kindred.
- [ ] Stage the decay: name the charter, statute or scandal that starts the proof's decline, and say what inherits its docket.

## 5. Worked example (NON-CANON)

A canal-league culture runs proof by tow-rope: a doubted oath escalates to the rope-walk — the swearer walks a wet rope laid along the lock-gate for nine knots, then the rope is inspected for slips; the lock-keeper's guild blesses the rope, fasts the parties and records the verdicts. The league's charters decay unevenly, exactly on §3's charter pattern: the upstream town's 1140 charter still names the rope-walk, the downstream port's 1153 charter names oath-helpers only, no rope. A merchant house eventually buys the guild's old acquittal trick (pre-oiled rope sections) when the practice has already declined to a festival — the §3 mountebank afterlife, priced and performed. Decides nothing about any world; demonstrates the checklist.
