# Guest-Right and Hospitality as Sacral Law — the degrees of guest, the host's package, the start and end markers, the seal, the breach ladder, and the guest's half of the bond

## 2. Six design decisions

The anchor column points at `## 3` bullets by number; the numbers are pointers, not citations, and no table in this entry carries a row id. Six decisions, six rows; every row is a lever a worldbuilder sets, not a fact about any culture.

| decision | options | attested anchor | worldbuilding consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| who counts as guest | one flat class (stranger equals acquaintance) / a graded ladder (stranger, guest-friend by inheritance, suppliant, envoy, trader) / status-bound (only the invited) | §3 #12, #3, #4 | A flat class makes refusal a sacral crime but gives no ladder for rank; a graded ladder lets a world price refusal by degree and turns the envoy into a walking exception; inheritance turns the bond into a durable alliance between houses, with the dead as parties. |
| what the host owes | fire only / fire and food / fire, food, clothing and a seat / the full package with a bath and protection for a counted number of days / means-scaled | §3 #10, #7, #9, #3 | Means-scaling keeps poor households inside the system and produces the relay, where a host who runs out hands the guest to a richer house; a full package with a day-count turns the house into a temporary sanctuary with a visible clock, and the clock is where plot lives. |
| when the bond starts and ends | starts at the door-crossing, at the first morsel, or at the shared cup / ends at the parting gift, at departure, at the guest's death, or never (inherited) | §3 #9, #13, #5, #6, #11 | The start marker decides who is protected before food — the threshold-crosser or only the fed; the end marker decides whether protection survives departure as remembrance-debt and whether the host may expel at all. A bond that ends at the gift leaves the guest unprotected on the road, which is a design opportunity, not a bug. |
| what seals it | oath sworn by a god / material token (salt, bread, a tessera) / shared meal or cup / hand-clasp / written clause with witnesses | §3 #1, #18, #14, #22 | The seal fixes the executor: an oath needs a god who avenges, a token needs a keeper and a house in which to show it, and a meal needs witnesses who eat. A culture with no god-executor can still run guest-right on tokens and feud, and that is a different politics. |
| what happens when it is violated | feud by kindred duty / sacral sanction (plague, flood, the god's visit) / compensation / outlawry / the stranger-god test | §3 #1, #19, #2, #4 | The opting-out polity — the one that does not care about the god — is the antagonist design: guest-right is only as strong as the violator's belief. The god-test makes every refusal a wager, and a world can price that wager by making the test rare, public, or mandatory on one night. |
| what the guest owes | silence and attention at the first meal / no scorn of host, house or fare / the ask at parting / remembrance and a return-gift / departure when sped | §3 #6, #13, #14, #5 | Guest obligations are the mirror that keeps the bond from becoming pure extraction. Load the guest and you get courtly restraint and a gift economy; load the host and you get the door-beam problem, where the always-open door becomes the house's ruin and the culture invents a lock, a limit, or a scapegoat. |

## 2.1 Lexicon interface — the five fields the institution forces

A conlang that has hospitality as an institution needs five lexemes before it needs a single proverb. The table is the design checklist; the Vethari column is one worked solution (craft, NON-CANON).

| field | what it must encode | test question | Vethari term (craft) |
|---|---|---|---|
| guest-term | the protected incomer, and whether the word also covers enemy or stranger | can the language say "guest" without saying "someone I invited"? | dawan — "the one who has crossed" |
| host-term | the party bound, not merely the owner | does the word name an obligation or a property relation? | hepan — "roof-keeper" |
| bond-term | the relation itself, said of both parties with different subjects | can the bond be stated without naming who is bound to whom? | sirat — "the tie" |
| violation-term | the breach and the person who is now in breach | is the violator a criminal, a breaker, or an outlaw — and which of those can be redeemed? | kobar — "breaker"; kobarat — "breach" |
| parting-gift term | the act that ends the bond and starts a debt | is the gift a payment, a memory, or a weapon? | melak — "sending-thing" |

**Worked derivation (Vethari, invented for this entry).** Phonology: consonants p t k b d g m n s š h r l w j, vowels a e i o u, CV(C) syllables, initial stress. Roots are biconsonantal plus a theme vowel; three suffixes do the institutional work: **-n** agent, **-t** abstract or result, **-k** instrument or gift. The derivation is deliberately asymmetric, because the institution is:

| root | verb | agent (-n) | abstract or result (-t) | derived institutional term |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| d-w | dawa "to cross a threshold" | dawan "one who has crossed" | dawat "the crossing" | **dawan** — guest |
| h-p | hepa "to keep a roof" | hepan "roof-keeper" | hepat "the keeping" | **hepan** — host |
| s-r | sira "to tie" | siran "one who ties" | sirat "the tie" | **sirat** — the bond |
| k-b | koba "to break" | kobar "breaker" | kobarat "breach" | **kobar** — the violator; kobarat — the breach |
| m-l | mela "to send away" | melan "sender" | melat "the sending" | **melak** — the parting gift |

Read the paradigm as a design statement. The guest and the host are both agent nouns, but of different verbs: the guest is named by what he did (crossed), the host by what he keeps (a roof). The bond is an abstract noun, so it cannot be said without a subject: *Sira hepan dawan* — "is.bound host guest" — "the host is bound to the guest"; the language forces the speaker to name which party is bound, and the answer is the host. The violation is an agent noun, not a legal-status noun: the violator *is* the breach, which is why the feud can be inherited. The parting gift is an instrument noun: *Melak dawan hepam* — "parting.gift guest host-DAT" — "the guest gives the parting gift to the host"; the gift is the means by which the bond is ended, not a payment for services.

- **Register rule (craft).** The host addresses the guest only as *dawan*; the guest addresses the host with the kin-escape *hepan-eth* ("roof-keeper-elder") or the sibling term *šen*. A host who uses *šen* to the guest claims equality and creates a lasting debt; a guest who uses *hepan* without the elder suffix is asserting that the host is a servant. This is the address asymmetry that `linguistics/kinship-terminology.md:119` leaves to the worldbuilder.
- **Colexification lever (craft, with a real-world precedent in §3 #22).** Vethari can merge "guest", "stranger" and "enemy" into one root and let the bond suffixes split them, or keep three roots and make the merger a marked poetic usage. The choice is a claim about the culture's threat model, not about any attested language.
- **The unknown-guest term.** *dawan-ves* — "guest-unseen" — names the incomer who may be a god. A culture that has the term can run the stranger-god test as a named institution instead of an ad hoc twist; a culture without it can still have the test, but the test has no lexical home and must be carried by ritual instead.

- S904: the poorest household is still bound, and the doctrinal reason is the god in the beggar — 「it would not be right for me to insult him, for all strangers and beggars are from Jove」 (Odyssey XIV, Eumaeus to the disguised Odysseus).
- S904: the host's term is bounded and the exit is a duty, not a mood — 「One should treat a guest well as long as he is in the house and speed him when he wants to leave it」 (Odyssey XV, Menelaus to Telemachus).

- S904: the arrival package includes heated water as a standing instruction — 「set a copper on the fire and heat some water; our guest will take a warm bath」 (Odyssey VIII, Alcinous to Arete).
- S905: the chapter opens with the superlative that makes hospitality a national trait — 「No nation indulges more profusely in entertainments and hospitality」 (Tacitus, Germania XXI, Church and Brodribb tr., Wikisource).
- S905: refusal is a religious offence, not a discourtesy — 「To exclude any human being from their roof is thought impious」 (Germania XXI).
- S905: the obligation is means-scaled, which is what keeps poor houses in the system — 「every German, according to his means, receives his guest with a well-furnished table」 (Germania XXI).
- S905: the bond is transferable by relay, and the host becomes the escort — 「he who was but now the host becomes the guide and companion to further hospitality」 (Germania XXI).
- S905: the flat class is stated as a legal fact — 「No one distinguishes between an acquaintance and a stranger, as regards the rights of hospitality」 (Germania XXI).
- S905: the guest may ask at departure, and the ask is normal, not greedy — 「It is usual to give the departing guest whatever he may ask for」 (Germania XXI).
- S905: the Germanic gift is explicitly outside reciprocity, which separates it from the potlatch family — 「they expect no return for what they give, nor feel any obligation for what they receive」 (Germania XXI).
- S906: the theoxeny begins with a failed census of doors — 「To a thousand houses did they go, asking for lodging and for rest」 — and a thousand houses bolted against them before one took them in (Ovid, Metamorphoses VIII, the Baucis and Philemon episode, Gutenberg #26073).

- S906: the sanction falls on the neighbourhood, not on a person — 「We are Divinities, and this impious neighbourhood shall suffer deserved punishment」 (Metamorphoses VIII).

- S907: the dictionary definition ties the guest to reception in another's house and, by extension, to the parasite — 「one who receives hospitality in the house of another」 — then gives the reciprocal "host" and applies the word to a parasite (Encyclopaedia Britannica, 11th edn, "Guest", Wikisource transcription, as served and as captured).
- S907: the etymology preserves the hostile-stranger polarity — 「originally a stranger, hence enemy」 (Britannica 11th edn, "Guest").

## 4. Craft checklist

- [ ] Write the degrees first: name at least three classes of protected incomer and say which one a refusal may lawfully turn away.
- [ ] Price the host's package: list what the house must give (fire, food, clothing, seat, bath, protection) and mark which items scale with means.
- [ ] Fix the start marker: door-crossing, first morsel, or shared cup — and say what protection exists before it.
- [ ] Fix the end marker: parting gift, departure, death, or inheritance — and say what survives the end (remembrance, debt, alliance).
- [ ] Choose the seal and its executor: a god who avenges, a token with a keeper, a meal with witnesses, or a written clause with an archive.
- [ ] Build the breach ladder: feud, compensation, outlawry, sacral sanction, proof — and give each rung a procedure and a price.
- [ ] Decide the opt-out: which polity or class does not believe in the sanction, and how the believing culture treats it.
- [ ] Write the guest's obligations as law, not manners: silence, no scorn, the ask, the return-gift, the duty to leave.
- [ ] Set the door limit: what the culture does when hospitality becomes ruin (a lock, a season, a scapegoat, a relay).
- [ ] Lexicon test: five terms (guest, host, bond, violation, parting gift) and one sentence in which the bond is said of the host — if the language cannot say who is bound, the institution is not yet designed.
- [ ] Address test: one direction of kin-escape only, and the penalty for using the wrong direction.
- [ ] Handoff test: the decision table can be read by a lore seat without this entry's prose.

## 5. Worked example (NON-CANON)

The river-port of Ashen Quay runs guest-right on thresholds and salt. Its law is written as six settings, one per row of §2.

**Degrees.** A *dawan* is any incomer who crosses a door-beam; a *dawan-sirat* is a guest-friend by inheritance — the tie passes to the eldest child of the pair who last exchanged salt, and the dead remain parties, so a host may owe a stranger because a grandmother ate at the wrong table. A *dawan-oph* is a suppliant who kneels at the hearth and may not be refused while the fire is lit; a *dawan-hal* is an envoy, who is owed the seat but not the salt, because the salt would bind the town to terms; a *dawan-mok* is a trader, who is owed the stall and the water but not the seat. The degrees are ranked by what a refusal costs: refusing a *dawan-oph* is a sacral offence, refusing a *dawan-mok* is a fee.

**Host package.** Fire, a bowl, a cloth, a seat and three nights' protection. The package is means-scaled: a house that cannot feed a guest for three nights must walk him to a wealthier door and say the relay formula, and the receiving house inherits the whole obligation — the Tacitean relay, priced by the town.

**Markers.** The bond starts when the guest's foot clears the beam, not when he speaks; a guest in the doorway is already protected, which is why the town's ushers stand outside and the tax collectors stand inside. It is sealed by salt from the host's dish; it ends when the guest is handed the *melak*, a shard of the host's salt-cellar, which is also the token the guest must present if he returns to claim the tie. A guest who leaves without the shard leaves with the bond still open, and the host must send a runner after him — a comic engine the town's plays use constantly.

**Seal.** The salt is the material seal; the oath is the public one, sworn on the hearth stone with the formula "the fire remembers". The executor is the hearth goddess, and her sanction is the tide: a house that breaks salt loses its boats within the year. The town does not believe in the goddess uniformly — the harbourmaster's faction holds that the tide is weather — which is the opt-out that makes the law a live argument rather than a settled fact.

**Breach.** Turning a guest out is *kobarat*; the kindred may feud, the guild may fine, and the hearth priest may set a proof: the accused walks the ebb-line at low water carrying the salt-cellar shard, and if the shard is wet when he returns, the tide has judged him. The ladder is legible because each rung has a different audience — the feud is for the families, the fine is for the guild, the proof is for the town.

**Guest's half.** Silence at the first bowl, no scorn of the fare, one ask at parting, the shard kept, and departure when sped. A guest who asks twice is not a guest but a creditor; a guest who stays past the third night is a resident and must be naturalized or expelled, and the town's charter argues about which.

**The unknown guest.** One night a year the town leaves the sea-gate unlatched and sets a bowl at the empty place: *dawan-ves*, the guest-unseen, who may be a god or a beggar or a smuggler. A host who refuses that night is tried by the tide. The custom is expensive, and the town's calendar keeps it to one night, because the always-open door is the door that cannot close: the beam wears, the household is eaten out, and the culture must invent a lock, a season, a scapegoat, or a relay. Decides nothing about any world; demonstrates the checklist.
