# Marriage as alliance and transfer — the payment direction, the prohibited degrees, the rite that binds, the change of name and dress, the return rules, and the guarantor of the union

- topic: marriage as an *alliance-and-transfer institution* for fictional cultures — not romance, not the wedding scene: the direction and form of the payment (bridewealth, dowry, bride-service, gift-exchange, none), the prohibited degrees that decide who may marry whom (exogamy, endogamy, consanguinity, affinity), the rite sequence and who performs each step, the change of name and dress, the dissolution and return rules, and the guarantor of the union together with the disposition of the children. ## 2. Six design decisions

The anchor column points at `## 3` bullets by number; the numbers are pointers, not citations, and no table in this entry carries a row id. Six decisions, six rows; every row is a lever a worldbuilder sets, not a fact about any culture. The rows are ordered as the institution is built: the payment first, because it decides who is a creditor; the boundary second, because it decides who is available; the rite third, because it decides when the fact becomes public; the marks fourth, because they advertise the transfer; the return fifth, because it is the default clause; the guarantor sixth, because the guarantor is whoever the other five leave holding the loss.

**Calibration.** The registry's CAL-06 forbids exactly the bundle a marriage entry is tempted to build — one universal primitive stage joining exogamy, clan descent and taboo — and orders the surface disaggregated (the row is quoted in `## 1`). This table is that disaggregation: six switches, six settings, no implication between them. A world may run bridewealth with no prohibited degrees, a Levitical table with no god, or a register with no rite, and each combination is coherent; the table's job is to make the combination chosen rather than assumed. The 1911 article is calibrated as a **dated legal survey**, not as stage theory: its comparative frame and its one use of the word "primitive" (about Roman legal forms) are dated vocabulary, it carries no sequence of social stages, and nothing in this entry rests on it. No unilinear sequence is asserted anywhere in this entry; where the corpus needs a sequence it belongs to the case, not to the institution.

| decision | options | attested anchor | worldbuilding consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| the payment direction | bridewealth (groom's people to bride's people) / dowry (bride's people to the couple or to the bride) / bride-service (the groom's labour for a term) / gift-exchange (both ways, no final settlement) / none (the state or the god pays) | §3 #3, #6, #29, #30, #36 | Direction decides who is owed, therefore who may refuse and who must ask. Bridewealth makes the bride's kin a creditor and the union a mortgage on the husband's lineage — the marriage is never finished because something is always still owed. Dowry makes the bride a creditor *inside* the house, which buys her standing and makes her the target of a household that wants the principal. Bride-service turns the union into a labour contract with a term and makes the son-in-law a servant who becomes a kinsman. Gift-exchange, the reverse dower of the Germanic chapter, makes the union a standing relation with no settlement at all — the most durable form and the most fragile, because there is no moment at which either side can say the account is closed. A world that runs two directions at once (a returnable herd one way, an outright chest the other) gets the most plot: two ledgers, each with its own default. |
| who may marry whom | clan exogamy / lineage endogamy (father's brother's daughter) / rank endogamy (marry inside the grade) / affinity bars (a deceased wife's sister, a brother's widow) / a closed scriptural table of degrees | §3 #17, #18, #22, #23, #24 | The table is the constitution: it fixes the size of the marriage pool and therefore the price. Count the degrees and you count the politics — a bar to the third degree leaves a village marriageable, a bar to the seventh makes everyone kin and puts the dispensation in the hands of a specialist who can sell it. Endogamy concentrates property and produces a class of kin who are also creditors; exogamy spreads alliance and turns the bride into an envoy with divided loyalty. A closed table (the Levitical list) makes the jurist necessary and makes the table itself a thing to be argued over, amended, and evaded; an open principle (no one you would mourn) makes the mourner the authority instead. A people that boasts of not marrying out has made endogamy an identity claim, and then every foreign marriage is a defection. |
| the rite and who performs it | domestic (hand-taking, a shared cake, the hearth) / public (assembly, priest, ten witnesses) / contractual (writing, registration, an archive) / processual (cohabitation plus time) | §3 #8, #9, #10, #11, #12, #25, #26, #27 | The performer decides who can annul and who keeps the record. A rite that needs ten witnesses makes marriage a public fact and makes elopement an offence against the town, not the family; a rite that needs only cohabitation makes marriage invisible, puts the burden of proof on the woman, and hands the question to whoever can produce neighbours. The sequence's *order* is the deepest lever: if the cake is broken before the woman moves, the cake binds and the move is a consequence; if the move comes first, the move binds and the feast is publicity. A processual rule (one year of cohabitation, broken by three nights away) gives the culture a legal escape hatch that is also a romantic device — the three-night absence is a plot engine with a clock. |
| the change of name and dress | nothing changes / the bride takes the husband's name / both change / a new name at the first child / dress only (veil, crown, the other house's colour, a shoulder-cloth) / a token that never comes off | §3 #13, #14, #15, #16, #31 | A name change is a transfer made audible: who may still say the old name is a taboo map, and a name that changes only at the first child says the marriage is not complete until it is fertile. Dress is the transfer made visible, and the supplier is the claim: a bride dressed by the groom's house enters wearing the captor's colours, while a bride who keeps her own house's cloth is a guest with a return ticket. The strongest version is a garment that is *returned* — the shoulder-cloth worn from the move until the first child, given back when the marriage becomes a lineage — because then the wardrobe dates the institution and the neighbours can read the marriage's stage off a person's shoulders. A token that never comes off (a ring, a thread, a bangle) converts the transfer into a permanent mark and makes the dissolution a visible wound. |
| the dissolution and return rules | no divorce / repudiation by the husband only / bilateral / dissolution by either kindred / return of the whole payment / return minus a schedule / no return once there are children / nullity (the marriage never existed) | §3 #3, #4, #5, #7, #19, #20, #21, #32 | The return rule is the real constitution. A payment that must be returned makes the marriage a debt and divorce a refinancing, so the poor cannot divorce and the rich divorce cheaply. A payment that is never returned makes the union indissoluble for the poor and merely expensive for the rich. A depreciation schedule (one beast per year) prices the marriage by duration and quietly tells you what the culture thinks a year of a woman's life is worth — a number worth stating out loud in the world bible. The children's claim on the dowry decides whether the wife is a custodian or a founder: if the dowry passes to her sons, she is the founder of a line and her husband's kin cannot expel her without disinheriting the future; if it reverts to her father's house, she is a loan. Nullity is the sharpest instrument, because it rewrites the past: a marriage declared void makes the children's legitimacy retroactive, and a court that can do that holds more power than one that can only end things. |
| who guarantees it, and the children | the two kindreds / the god or gods / the ruler's law / the guild or class / nobody (reputation alone) | §3 #1, #2, #28, #33, #34, #35 | The guarantor decides what a broken marriage costs and who may sue. A divine guarantor makes the sanction invisible and transferable to the whole community — the land itself vomits out its inhabitants — which is cheap to administer and impossible to appeal. A kin guarantee makes every marriage a treaty and every divorce a border incident, with the kindreds as the standing army. A ruler's guarantee makes marriage a fiscal instrument: the court charges the estate for the unmarried son's payment, keeps the register, and taxes the transfer. The children's disposition is the tail of the guarantee: legitimacy, rank and faith are three different rules that can point three different ways, and the cleanest case for a worldbuilder is the morganatic marriage — legitimate children with no claim to rank or fortune — because it shows that legitimacy and inheritance are separate switches, and a culture can set them independently. |

## 2.1 Lexicon interface — the six fields the institution forces

A conlang that has marriage as an institution needs six lexemes before it needs a single proverb, and the six are forced by the six decisions: a payment term, a counter-payment term, a sponsor term, a prohibited-kin term, a dissolution term, and a return term. The table is the design checklist; the Ishkari column is one worked solution (craft, NON-CANON).

| field | what it must encode | test question | Ishkari term (craft) |
|---|---|---|---|
| payment | the thing that moves at the union, and whether it must come back | can the language say "the cattle are a payment" without saying whether the cattle return? | radwar — "the returnable transfer"; tudwar — "the outright transfer" |
| counter-payment | the thing that moves the other way, and whether it is a receipt or a settlement | does the word for the counter-gift also mean "receipt", "price" or "change"? | rapalt — "the return of a returnable thing" (used for the counter-gift and for the divorce return alike) |
| sponsor | the person who stands between the two parties and answers for the transfer | is the go-between a servant, a witness, a kinsman or a creditor? | kasan — "surety-man"; kasat — "suretyship" |
| prohibited kin | the person one may not marry, and the table that says how near is too near | is the bar a person, a category, or a counted degree? | ungali — "the barred one"; ungalat — "the bar, the closed degree" |
| dissolution | the act that ends the union, and whether it ends the fact or only the relation | does the word mean "severing" or "untying" or "declaring void"? | shabat — "the severing"; a nullity is not shabat but a denial that dawat ever occurred |
| return | the transfer that closes the account, and who must make it | is the return a cancellation or a second payment in the reverse direction? | rapalt (the thing returned); palan — "the returner", the party liable for the return leg |

**Worked derivation (Ishkari, invented for this entry).** Phonology: obstruents p t k b d g, fricatives s z sh h, nasals m n, liquids r l, glides w j; vowels a e i o u; CV(C) syllables; final stress. Roots are biconsonantal with a theme vowel *-a*; three prefixes classify the noun, and three suffixes do the grammatical work: the prefixes **me-** mark a person or party, **ra-** mark a thing that must return, **tu-** mark a thing that does not return, with **un-** negating. The suffixes are **-n** agent, **-t** act or abstract, **-r** thing or result. The derivation is deliberately asymmetric, because the institution is:

| root | verb | agent (-n) | act (-t) | classified thing | institutional term |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| d-w | dawa "to hand over" | dawan "the hander-over" | dawat "the handing-over" | radwar / tudwar | **dawat** — the marriage; **radwar** — bridewealth; **tudwar** — dowry |
| k-s | kasa "to stand surety" | kasan "the surety-man" | kasat "suretyship" | — | **kasan** — the sponsor who answers for the transfer |
| g-l | gala "to be barred" | — | ungalat "the bar" | ungali "the barred one" | **ungali** — prohibited kin; **ungalat** — the prohibited degrees |
| p-l | pala "to send back" | palan "the returner" | palat "the sending-back" | rapalt | **rapalt** — the return; **palan** — the party liable for it |
| sh-b | shaba "to sever" | shaban "the severed party" | shabat "the severing" | — | **shabat** — divorce |

Read the paradigm as a design statement. The payment is not one word but a **class choice**, and the class is carried by a prefix on the noun that the verb must agree with: *radwar dawa* — "to hand over a returnable thing" — and *tudwar dawa* — "to hand over an outright thing" — are different verbs in use because the speaker cannot utter the transfer without committing to whether it returns. A language with only *tudwar* cannot state the difference between a sale and a marriage; a language with only *radwar* cannot give anything away at all, and its every gift is a loan. The sponsor is an agent noun of a different verb (*kasa*, to stand surety), so he is not the hander-over and not the receiver: the institution has a third party by construction, and a culture that lacks the word must run its marriages on the two kindreds alone or import a god to do the standing. The prohibited kin is a negative-classified person, so the bar is a *person*, not a rule — the language says "the barred one" and forces the speaker to name who is barred, which is why the table can be argued over person by person. The dissolution is an act noun, not a status noun: there is no adjective "divorced", only the deed (*shabat*) and the party (*shaban*), which makes divorce a thing that happened rather than a thing one is. And the return shares its root with the counter-gift, because in this language the counter-gift *is* the first return: *rapalt* covers the chest delivered the morning after the move and the herd driven back at the severing, so the ledger closes only by another transfer in the reverse direction, never by cancellation.

- **Two worked sentences (craft).** *Kasan radwar dawa dawan* — "the surety-man hands the returnable-transfer over to the bride's guardian" — the proposal and the deposit in one clause, with the direction and the liability both audible. *Shabat: palan rapalt dawan* — "severing: the returner returns the returnable to the guardian" — divorce stated as the return leg of the same instrument, which is exactly the design point: the culture cannot describe a divorce without describing a payment.
- **The colexification lever (craft, with the real-world precedent in §3 #36).** The lexicon can merge the marriage payment with the homicide compensation (one root for "the price of a person"), or keep them apart and make the merger a marked, shocking usage. Merging them is a claim that persons are priced on one scale and that a killing and a marriage are settled in the same currency; keeping them apart is a claim that the two arithmetics are incommensurable. Both are design choices, not linguistic facts.
- **The register rule (craft).** Before the transfer the two kindreds may not address each other directly — they are still *ungali* to one another in the classificatory sense — so all speech passes through the *kasan*. After the transfer they address each other as *dawan*-reciprocal. A kindred that speaks directly before the payment has insulted the sponsor and, in the league of §5, has to pay him a fee to be spoken to again.
- **The failed-lexicon test (method).** Write the six fields for the culture; if any field has no term, the institution has a hole and the world will route around it. A culture with no return term cannot have divorce by return, so its marriages either never end or end by violence; a culture with no sponsor term cannot have a guarantee that is not divine or legal; a culture with no prohibited-kin term can only bar marriages by custom, which means the bar is unenforceable the moment the custom is doubted. Holes are interesting — but they must be *chosen*, not left by accident.

## 3. Attested anchors

Each bullet carries exactly one row id; the quote is verbatim from the row's page with whitespace normalised, and the locus names where on the page it sits. No bullet carries two ids, and no table row in this entry carries any. The Britannica row is a **dated 1911 legal survey** and the weakest tier here (B): its comparative frame is period vocabulary, and its one use of the word "primitive" names Roman legal forms, not social stages. The served article (volume 17, pp. 770–776) is legal-institutional — Roman, canon, English and Scottish law, concubinage and morganatic marriage, Hardwicke's Act, the deceased wife's sister, foreign marriages, the German code of 1900 — and it carries no promiscuity, group-marriage or matriarchy sequence at all, so this entry attributes none to it and asserts no unilinear sequence of its own. Where the entry uses it, it is for the Roman forms, the canon impediments and the English statute, and those claims are dated to 1911 by the row's own recheck.

- S909: the code makes consummation constitutive, so that a transfer without it is no marriage — 「If a man take a woman to wife, but have no intercourse with her, this woman is no wife to him」 (Code of Hammurabi §128, King tr., Avalon Project, the marriage section of the laws).
- S909: a betrothed girl still living in her father's house is already another man's wife, which is the betrothal's legal effect stated as a capital case — 「If a man violate the wife (betrothed or child-wife) of another man, who has never known a man, and still lives in her father's house」 (Hammurabi §130).
- S909: the groom who brings chattels and the purchase-money and then withdraws forfeits everything, so the deposit is the commitment device — 「the girl's father may keep all that he had brought」 (Hammurabi §159).
- S909: the father who withdraws must return the whole deposit, so the return rule runs both ways — 「he shall give him back all that he brought with him」 (Hammurabi §160).
- S909: a slandering third party cannot acquire the bride even when the match collapses, so the woman is not a prize for whoever causes the breach — 「but his wife shall not be married to the friend」 (Hammurabi §161).
- S909: if the wife dies leaving sons, the dowry belongs to them and her father has no claim, which is the children's-lien rule — 「then shall her father have no claim on her dowry; this belongs to her sons」 (Hammurabi §162).
- S909: if she dies childless and the purchase price is repaid, the dowry reverts to her father's house, which is the reversion rule — 「it belongs to her father's house」 (Hammurabi §163).
- S909: when the father-in-law does not repay, the husband offsets the purchase price against the dowry and pays the remainder, which is the arithmetic of a failed settlement — 「he may subtract the amount of the "Purchase price" from the dowry」 (Hammurabi §164).
- S909: the estate of a dead father must set aside the purchase price for the unmarried minor son, so a marriage is a charge on the inheritance — 「they shall set aside besides his portion the money for the "purchase price" for the minor brother who had taken no wife as yet」 (Hammurabi §166).
- S909: a divorce with children settles the wife with her dowry and a usufruct so that she can rear them, which prices the children's maintenance into the return — 「he shall give that wife her dowry, and a part of the usufruct of field, garden, and property, so that she can rear her children」 (Hammurabi §137).
- S909: a divorce without children returns the purchase money and the dowry and nothing else, which is the clean default — 「he shall give her the amount of her purchase money and the dowry which she brought from her father's house」 (Hammurabi §138).
- S909: a guiltless wife may take her dowry and go back to her father's house, so the woman's exit carries property — 「she shall take her dowry and go back to her father's house」 (Hammurabi §142).
- S909: a father who defiles his son's betrothed before the son has known her pays half a mina, compensates what she brought, and she may marry whom she chooses — the sponsor's liability and the bride's exit in one rule — 「She may marry the man of her heart」 (Hammurabi §156).
- S910: the three Roman modes — confarreatio, coemptio in manum and usus — are said to produce one effect, the woman in the husband's *manus*, and the article states the result in a phrase that fits a slave as well as a wife — 「on the same footing as the children」 (EB11 "Marriage", Roman law section).
- S910: the oldest mode is a sacrifice before ten witnesses with a cake broken and divided by the priest, which fixes both the performer and the sequence — 「a religious ceremony before ten witnesses」 and 「an ox was sacrificed and a wheaten cake broken and divided between the spouses by the priest」 (EB11 "Marriage", Roman law section).
- S910: the second mode conveys the woman by mancipatio and is expressly a fiction, so the payment can be legally real while the sale is not — 「might be described as a fictitious sale per aes et libram」 (EB11 "Marriage", Roman law section).
- S910: the third mode acquires the wife by prescription, and the escape is a three-night absence, which is a time rule with a built-in loophole — 「the acquisition of the wife by prescription, through her cohabiting with the husband for one year, without having been absent from his house three continuous nights」 (EB11 "Marriage", Roman law section).
- S910: marriage without the manus is made by consent alone, so the consent doctrine and the transfer doctrine can coexist in one system — 「Marriage without manus was contracted by the interchange of consent, without writing or formality of any kind.」 (EB11 "Marriage", Roman law section).
- S910: the household head's consent is essential, which names the sponsor in the strictest possible way — 「The consent of the paterfamilias to the marriage of the children under his power was essential」 (EB11 "Marriage", Roman law section).
- S910: English statute adopts the scriptural list by name, which is the direct bridge between this row and the Leviticus row — 「no prohibition, God's law except, shall trouble or impeach any marriage without the Levitical degrees」 (EB11 "Marriage", England section, quoting 32 Hen. VIII c. 38).
- S910: the 1835 Act turns a bar into a nullity rather than a voidable fault, which is the retroactive instrument — 「all marriages which shall hereafter be celebrated between persons within the prohibited degrees of consanguinity or affinity shall be absolutely null and void」 (EB11 "Marriage", England section).
- S910: the morganatic case keeps the children legitimate and gives them nothing, which separates legitimacy from inheritance — 「the children being legitimate, but neither they nor the wife acquiring any right to the rank or fortune of the husband」 (EB11 "Marriage", concubinage paragraph).
- S910: concubinage is defined as marriage with no legal consequences, which is the zero point of the whole scale — 「marriage which has no consequences, or only slight and peculiar consequences, in legal status」 (EB11 "Marriage", concubinage paragraph).
- S911: the father gives the bride, and the giving is the act the hymn celebrates — 「The Sun-God Savitar bestowed his willing Surya on her Lord.」 (Rigveda 10.85.9, Griffith tr., sacred-texts).
- S911: the wedding is dated to a season and paid for in slaughter, so the rite has a calendar and a bill — 「In Magha days are oxen slain, in Arjuris they wed the bride」 (RV 10.85.13).
- S911: the departure formula tells the bride to rise because she now has a husband, which is the moment the transfer becomes speech — 「Rise up from hence: this maiden hath a husband」 (RV 10.85.21).
- S911: the prior claimant is told to find another willing maid, so the relinquishment is spoken to a rival and not to the bride — 「Seek thou another willing maid, and with her husband leave the bride」 (RV 10.85.22).
- S911: the union is perfected by named gods, so the sponsors are divine and the guarantee is not the kindreds' — 「Let Aryaman and Bhaga lead us: perfect, O Gods, the union of the wife and husband」 (RV 10.85.23).
- S911: the hand-taking is performed by a god and paired with an instruction about the bride's new office — 「Let Pusan take thy hand and hence conduct thee」 and 「Go to the house to be the household's mistress」 (RV 10.85.26).
- S911: the bride's robe is given away and treasure goes to the priests, so the garment is a transferable fee and not a keepsake — 「Give thou the woollen robe away: deal treasure to the Brahman priests」 (RV 10.85.29).
- S911: the assembled guests are made to look at the bride and then sent home, which is the public showing and the closing of the rite — 「Signs of good fortune mark the bride come all of you and look at her」 and 「Wish her prosperity, and then return unto your homes again」 (RV 10.85.33).
- S911: the groom's formula names the gods as the givers and the bride as given to be mistress of a house — 「I take thy hand in mine for happy fortune」 and 「have given thee to be my household's mistress」 (RV 10.85.36).
- S911: the counter-gift asked of the god is the wife with progeny, so the return leg is children — 「Give to the husband in return, Agni, the wife with progeny」 (RV 10.85.38).
- S911: the bride has had divine husbands before the mortal one, and the chain of giving is recited at the wedding — 「Soma obtained her first of all; next the Gandharva was her lord」 (RV 10.85.40).
- S911: the transfer is said to carry riches and sons along with the spouse, so the payment and the fertility are one package — 「Agni hath bestowed on me riches and sons and this my spouse」 (RV 10.85.41).
- S911: the bride is given authority over her husband's parents and siblings, which is the strongest attested counterweight to the transfer reading — 「Over thy husband's father and thy husband's mother bear full sway」 (RV 10.85.46).
- S911: the marriage is sealed by the gods joining hearts rather than by a payment alone — 「So may the Universal Gods, so may the Waters join our hearts」 (RV 10.85.47).
- S912: the general bar is stated as a single principle before the list begins — 「None of you shall approach to any that is near of kin to him, to uncover their nakedness: I am the LORD.」 (Leviticus 18:6, KJV, Project Gutenberg #10).
- S912: the list is spoken in the second person and names each relation, which makes the table a recitation and not a statute — 「The nakedness of thy father’s wife shalt thou not uncover: it is thy father’s nakedness」 (Leviticus 18:8).
- S912: affinity is barred as well as blood, so a brother's widow is forbidden on the brother's account — 「Thou shalt not uncover the nakedness of thy brother’s wife: it is thy brother’s nakedness」 (Leviticus 18:16).
- S912: two sisters may not be taken as co-wives in the same lifetime, which is a rule about the pair and not about the individual — 「Neither shalt thou take a wife to her sister, to vex her, to uncover her nakedness, beside the other in her life time」 (Leviticus 18:18).
- S912: the bar is framed against the neighbouring lands' customs, so the prohibited degrees are also an identity claim — 「After the doings of the land of Egypt, wherein ye dwelt, shall ye not do: and after the doings of the land of Canaan, whither I bring you, shall ye not do」 (Leviticus 18:3).
- S912: the sanction is territorial, and the land is the injured party — 「the land itself vomiteth out her inhabitants」 (Leviticus 18:25).
- S912: the kin sanction is excision, which is the community's own remedy and not the court's — 「the souls that commit them shall be cut off from among their people」 (Leviticus 18:29).
- S912: the resident stranger is bound by the same table, so the bar travels with residence and not with descent — 「neither any of your own nation, nor any stranger that sojourneth among you」 (Leviticus 18:26).
- S904: the father is the one who finds the husband and provides the gifts, so the giving party is named in the epic's own idiom — 「let her go back to her father, who will find her a husband and provide her with all the marriage gifts that so dear a daughter may expect」 (Odyssey II, Telemachus on Penelope; Butler tr., Gutenberg #1727).
- S904: a dowry received for a daughter is ordinary enough to be one clause in a slave's autobiography, which is what makes it evidence of a standing institution — 「they sent Ctimene to Same and received a splendid dowry for her」 (Odyssey XV, Eumaeus' story).
- S905: the reverse dower is stated as the chapter's plain rule, so the direction is the ethnography's headline — 「The wife does not bring a dower to the husband but the husband to the wife」 (Tacitus, Germania ch. 18, Church and Brodribb tr., Wikisource).

- S905: the whole people is said to be free of foreign intermarriage, which is exogamy's opposite stated as a purity claim — 「the tribes of Germany are free from all taint of inter-marriages with foreign nations」 (Germania ch. 4).

## 4. Craft checklist

- [ ] Fix the direction first and write it as a sentence with two arrows: who pays whom, and what comes back. A worldbuilder who cannot draw both arrows has not designed a marriage, only a wedding.
- [ ] Decide whether the payment is returnable, and under what schedule. State the default in the world bible as a clause ("on severing, the herd returns minus one beast per year"), not as an atmosphere.
- [ ] Decide who is a party: the couple, the two kindreds, the household head, the sponsor, the god. A marriage with more parties has more vetoes and more scenes.
- [ ] Write the prohibited-degrees table as a table, with the degree count named. Then price the dispensation, because a table with no dispensation is a table with no politics.
- [ ] Choose the counting authority: a mourner, a jurist, a priest, a genealogist. The authority is a character class, and the class is where the plots are.
- [ ] Fix the rite's sequence and its performer, and say which step is irreversible. If the irreversible step is the count in the yard, the drama is public and numeric; if it is the hand-taking at the hearth, the drama is private and sacral.
- [ ] Decide when the marriage becomes a fact that outsiders can see: at the move, at the feast, at the first child, or at the register. The visibility date is the date the rumours can start.
- [ ] Decide the marks: name, dress, token, none. Then decide who may remove the mark, because removal is the dissolution made visible.
- [ ] Write the return arithmetic for four cases: no children, children, wife's fault, husband's fault. Four cases is the minimum for a legal system that a reader can follow; two cases is a fairy tale.
- [ ] Decide the children's switches separately: legitimacy, inheritance, rank, faith, custody. Set each one deliberately; a culture that sets them all together is a culture with one kind of marriage, and one kind is a small world.
- [ ] Decide the nullity rule, including whether a court can rewrite the past. If it can, the court is the strongest institution in the setting; if it cannot, the strongest institution is the archive.
- [ ] Lexicon test: six terms (payment, counter-payment, sponsor, prohibited kin, dissolution, return) and one sentence in which the verb must agree with whether the thing returns. If the language cannot say whether the payment comes back, the institution is not yet designed.
- [ ] Failure test: name the person the rules ruin. In every attested system the ruin is a person with no herd, no chest, no kin, or no sons, and that person is the entry point for a story.
- [ ] Handoff test: the six-row decision table and the four-case return arithmetic can be read by a lore seat without this entry's prose.

## 5. Worked example (NON-CANON)

The upland league of Tammar runs marriage as a two-ledger institution. Its law is written as six settings, one per row of §2, and it uses the Ishkari terms of §2.1 for the parts the common speech has to name.

**Payment.** A marriage moves two things in two directions. The groom's people owe the *radwar*, a herd of cattle counted in the yard; the bride's people owe the *tudwar*, a chest of linen, a copper pot and a fleece, delivered the morning after the move. Only the herd returns. The chest never does, because it is the receipt: the bride's kin have answered the transfer with a thing that stays, and the staying is what makes them parties rather than sellers. A house that cannot raise a chest must borrow one from the surety-man, and a borrowed chest is a debt that outlives the marriage — the league's most common lawsuit is not over cattle but over a chest.

**Degrees.** The bar is counted on the mourning grades the league already uses for funerals: a man may not marry a woman he would mourn in the first two grades, nor a woman whose kin he would mourn there. The inner grades (a sister, a father's wife) are absolute; the outer grades can be dispensed by the surety-man for a fee, and the fee is published. Because the grades are graded, the bar is graded, and because the fee is published, the bar is a market. The league's reformers argue that the fee should be paid to the hearth, not to the man, and the argument is the league's politics.

**Rite.** The sequence is five steps. The ask, at the door, by the surety-man, who speaks for the groom's people because they are still barred from the bride's. The hand-token, a copper ring, which is forfeit if the groom withdraws. The count in the yard, aloud, by both kindreds, which is the record, because the league does not write. The hand-taking at the hearth, performed by the hearth-keeper, which is the irreversible step. The move, on foot, the bride walking and not riding, because the road is said to remember a walked bride. The chest follows in the morning; the marriage is not complete until the chest is in the house, which means a marriage can be a day old and still not finished.

**Marks.** The bride keeps her own name and takes the house-name only at the first child; until then she is addressed as *dawan*, "the handed-over one", and her old name is used by her own people only. She wears the groom's house shoulder-cloth from the move until the first child, and returns it when the child is named; the return of the cloth is the day the marriage becomes a lineage, and the neighbours read it from her shoulders. A bride who keeps the cloth past the first child is claiming the house too early; a bride who returns it before a child is claiming the marriage is over, and the claim has to be answered.

**Dissolution.** Four cases, four arithmetics. No children, husband's fault: the herd returns whole, plus a beast for the chest's wear. No children, wife's fault: the herd returns minus one beast per year of the marriage, and the chest stays. Children, either fault: the herd does not return, and the chest is held for the children by the hearth-keeper; the wife may go, and the children stay in the father's house, but the chest follows the daughters. Nullity: if the count was never completed, the assembly may declare that the *dawat* never occurred — the children are legitimate by a separate ruling, the herd never moved, and the surety-man must refund his fee. The nullity case is the league's hardest, because it is the one case where the chest is already in the house and the herd is already spent.

**Guarantee.** The surety-man guarantees the herd's return and the chest's delivery; the hearth-keeper guarantees the hand-taking; the assembly guarantees the children's share. Three guarantors, three courts, three audiences — the herd is argued in the yard, the hand-taking at the hearth, the children's share at the assembly — and a case that crosses two of them is the league's definition of a scandal. The gods are present but not decisive: the hearth is tended, not consulted, and the league's sceptics say the hearth-keeper's real power is the chest.

**The person the rules ruin.** A younger son with no herd borrows from the surety-man, marries, and cannot repay; the surety-man becomes his creditor and his children's godfather, and the marriage is quietly a service contract. Or a widow whose husband's kin hold the chest for her sons and who therefore cannot leave and cannot stay. Or a bride whose count was interrupted by a feud and whose marriage is therefore, in law, still only an ask. Pick one and the setting has a first act.

Decides nothing about any world; demonstrates the checklist.

## 6. Lore handoff

This entry is reference knowledge and method for worldbuilders. It hands off to lore-shokunin through `corpus/knowledge/synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md`: Kotodama proposes, lore records. It decides nothing about what is true in any world — not whether a given world's gods witness a marriage, not whether its hearth-keeper's sanctity is real, not whether any character is married, promised, barred or returned. The handoff packet should carry the six-row decision table, the four-case return arithmetic, the six lexicon fields with the one sentence in which the verb agrees with whether the payment returns, the prohibited-degrees table with its counting authority and its dispensation price, and the list of marks with the dates on which each becomes visible. A world that sets those three deliberately can have any wedding it likes, because the wedding is only the receipt.
