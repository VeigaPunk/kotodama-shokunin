## 1. One issue, three cuts, and what a worldbuilder takes from each

The three articles are one thematic issue — *Communications* 8 (1966), « Recherches sémiologiques : l'analyse structurale du récit », as each served notice states on its own reference line. They are not three chapters of one method. Barthes asks what the object is and at what levels it can be described; Todorov cuts the *récit littéraire* into categories and works the temporal one in detail; Greimas takes one myth and asks what an interpretation of it would consist of. A designer who treats them as one voice loses what each supplies.

Barthes opens with the observation that makes narrative a design problem rather than a genre problem: "Innombrables sont les récits du monde." (6 words, printed p. 1). The page then generalises in a way worth quoting because the universality is the argument, not decoration: "il n'y a pas, il n'y a jamais eu nulle part aucun peuple sans récit" (printed p. 1). Narrative is not confined to one medium — the page lists articulated language, image, gesture, and their ordered mixture — and it is not confined to one culture: "ces récits sont goûtés en commun par des hommes de culture différente" (printed p. 1). Its summary word for that condition is "transhistorique, transculturel" (S885, printed p. 1).

The consequence for an invented culture is immediate. "Does this people tell stories?" is not a design variable; on this page there is no people without them, and a setting that says otherwise is making a claim about human possibility rather than about a world. The design variable is the formal one: which narratives this culture tells, in which media, and under which grammar.

Barthes draws the methodological consequence himself. Because narrative is everywhere, the analyst cannot proceed genre by genre: "Comment opposer le roman à la nouvelle, le conte au mythe, le drame à la tragédie" (printed p. 1) — his parenthesis notes it has been done a thousand times — "sans se référer à un modèle commun" (printed p. 1). The model is not a story and not a plot shape; it is what makes the distinctions possible. His analogy is Saussure's: the analyst of narrative stands before the infinity of *paroles* and tries to reach the *langue* behind them, "en parvenant à décrire la « langue » dont elles sont issues" (S885, printed p. 1).

| Article | The question it answers | The design artefact it produces |
|---|---|---|
| Barthes, *Introduction* | at what levels can a narrative be described, and what does a level do | the culture's narrative grammar: what counts as a unit, and what a unit must integrate into |
| Todorov, *Les catégories* | which categories of the literary narrative carry meaning, and how time is handled | the three-axis sheet: order of telling, framing perception, type of discourse |
| Greimas, *Éléments* | what an interpretation of one myth consists of, and where its meaning sits twice | the two-plane sheet: surface sequence and content structure, with the mediator slot |

Two rules follow before any of the three is applied. First, a model is not a canon: naming the grammar of a culture's narratives is not deciding which of its stories are true, and this file stops at the naming. Second, the three cuts are not interchangeable; §5 tabulates what each one can and cannot carry.

## 2. Barthes: the model is presupposed, and it is not a content

The argument of printed p. 1 is not merely that narrative is universal. The load-bearing half is the next move: universality is exactly why a model is unavoidable. If genres differ but all are narratives, then the difference can only be stated against something shared, and that something is a set of levels of description rather than a list of plots (S885).

That is the whole of what this file takes from Barthes, and it is deliberately less than the sibling file takes. The levels themselves — how many, which ones, what integrates into what — are carried by `narrative-structure-frameworks.amend-2026-10-23b.md`, which quotes the method page. Here the point is only that the model exists, is presupposed by any genre distinction, and is a *grammar* (a langue) rather than a repertoire of stories.

Three design orders follow, and each one has a visible failure symptom:

1. **Write the grammar before the corpus.** State what counts as a narrative unit in this culture, and what a unit must be integrated into to mean anything. Failure symptom: the world bible lists stories and genres but cannot say what makes two of them variants of one form.
2. **Separate the model from the instance.** The model is what the culture's narratives have in common; the instance is this tale, told this way, by this teller. Failure symptom: a single famous story is written up as the culture's narrative system.
3. **Do not smuggle content into the grammar.** The page's model is formal — levels and integration — and says nothing about which stories a culture must have. Failure symptom: a "narrative grammar" that turns out to be a required plot (a founding myth, a heroic cycle) restated as a rule.

| Question to ask of a draft | What a good answer looks like | What the page supports |
|---|---|---|
| What is a narrative unit here? | a named unit type with a stated scope | a formal model, not a genre list |
| What does a unit integrate into? | a named higher level, per unit type | hierarchy as the presupposition of sense |
| Which distinctions does the grammar license? | genre distinctions that reduce to the grammar | the model as the condition of the distinctions |
| Which story is canonical? | no answer: grammar does not rank instances | nothing on the page ranks stories |

## 3. Todorov: meaning is correlation, and time is the seam

Todorov supplies the sharpest single design rule in the three articles, and it is a definition rather than a scheme: "Le sens (ou la fonction) d'un élément de l'œuvre, c'est sa possibilité d'entrer en corrélation avec d'autres éléments de cette œuvre et avec l'œuvre entière" (25 words, S886, printed p. 125). Meaning is a relational slot. An element that correlates with nothing has, on this definition, no meaning — it is inventory, not structure.

That rule is directly usable against a world bible. A setting detail, an object, a title, a ritual: each is meaningful in a story only through the correlations it can enter. The design check is therefore not "is this detail interesting?" but "what can this detail be correlated with, and does the draft actually correlate it?" A detail carried in the bible and never correlated in any narrative is dead weight, and the definition says why rather than merely disapproving.

The article's own cut of discourse is threefold — time, aspect, mode — and the served p. 139 carries the definitions of the last two and the whole of the time section: "les aspects du récit, ou la manière dont l'histoire est perçue par le narrateur" (printed p. 139), and "les modes du récit, qui dépendent du type de discours utilisé par le narrateur" (S886, printed p. 139). Time is defined by a relation rather than a clock: it is where "s'exprime le rapport entre le temps de l'histoire et celui du discours" (S886, printed p. 139).

The time section is the part a worldbuilder should read twice, because it turns chronology into an instrument. Its premise is a mismatch: "Le temps du discours est, dans un certain sens, un temps linéaire, alors que le temps de l'histoire est pluridimensionnel." (printed p. 139). Several events can happen at once in the story; discourse must put them one after another. From that constraint Todorov derives the necessity of breaking the "natural" succession, and then the historical claim that carries the section: "Les formalistes russes voyaient dans la déformation temporelle le seul trait du discours qui le distingue de l'histoire" (S886, printed p. 139). Deformation is not ornament; on this account it is what makes discourse discourse.

The article closes the argument with a rule about arrangement: "la disposition même des événements dans le récit" (S886, printed p. 139) — the disposition of the events, the combination of sentences, representations, images and actions — obeys the same laws of aesthetic construction as the combination of sounds does in verse. The worked example on the page is a murder told in two orders: threat, suspense, then the killing, against the corpse discovered first and the killing reported backwards. The order changes the impression while the events stay the same.

| Axis | What to write in the world bible | Failure symptom |
|---|---|---|
| Time | the culture's default order of telling, and which deformations it treats as marked | a chronology with no statement of how it is told |
| Aspect | whose perception frames the telling, and what that framing hides | a narrator with no stated perceptual limit |
| Mode | which kind of discourse carries the story (reported, dramatised, mixed) | a single discourse type for every narrative the culture has |
| Correlation | the partners each element can enter relations with | details listed with no correlatives anywhere |

Calibration: Todorov's Formalist sentence is a lineage claim made in 1966 about what the Russian Formalists held, and he adopts it and illustrates it with a 1925 Vygotsky text quoted at length on the page. It is reported here as Todorov's account, not as a survey of Formalist doctrine.

## 4. Greimas: one message, two planes, and a protagonist who mediates

Greimas's article is narrower and its narrowness is the useful part. The served opening dedicates it "en hommage à Claude Lévi-Strauss" (printed p. 28) and takes as its object one myth, "le mythe de référence bororo" (printed p. 28). Its first principle is a correction of scope: "Loin de se limiter à l'interprétation des énoncés, la théorie sémantique qui chercherait à rendre compte de la lecture des mythes, doit opérer avec des séquences d'énoncés articulées en récits." (30 words, S887, printed p. 28). The unit is the narrative, not the sentence, and the question the page sets itself is what an interpretation of one consists of.

The first answer is a whole: "Le récit, pour avoir un sens, doit être un tout de signification et se présente, de ce fait, comme une structure sémantique simple." (23 words, printed p. 29). The second answer follows from the first, and it is the one that matters for design: secondary developments that do not fit the simple structure form a subordinate layer, so that "la narration, considérée comme un tout, aura donc pour contrepartie une structure hiérarchique du contenu" (S887, printed p. 29). A told narrative has a content architecture under it, and the architecture is not the telling.

The components he works with are Lévi-Strauss's, and he says so: "1° l'armature; 2° le code; 3° le message" (S887, printed p. 29). His contribution is the interpretation of the third. The message "donne lieu à deux lectures différentes, l'une sur le plan discursif, l'autre, sur le plan structurel" (printed p. 30). Two readings of one message, not two messages and not one reading done twice.

The term for what makes a uniform reading possible is defined on the same page: "par isotopie nous entendons un ensemble redondant de catégories sémantiques qui rend possible la lecture uniforme du récit" (S887, printed p. 30). An isotopy is a redundancy that a reading can lean on. A worldbuilder's version of the question is therefore: which redundancies does this narrative provide, and on which plane?

The page also supplies the protagonist's structural job, which is the most directly reusable sentence in the three articles: "un héros asocial qui, se disjoignant de la communauté, apparaît comme un agent grâce à qui se produit le renversement de la situation" (printed p. 30), and who "se pose, autrement dit, comme médiateur personnalisé entre la situation-avant et la situation-après" (S887, printed p. 30). The narrative's own perspective is anthropocentric: "L'isotopie narrative est déterminée par une certaine perspective anthropocentrique" (printed p. 30). A protagonist is a device for turning a state into its reversal, personalised so that the reversal can be told as someone's doing.

| Plane | What is written on it | Design check |
|---|---|---|
| discursive | the sequence as told: actors, events, order, the anthropocentric perspective | can this sequence be followed without the content structure? |
| structural | the content categories and their transformations, the before/after pair | does the reversal survive a retelling in another medium? |
| mediator slot | the figure who disjoins from the community and carries the reversal | is the reversal caused by a person, or merely reported? |

Two limits belong here rather than in a footnote. The two readings are described as two; nothing on p. 30 makes them the same reading, and this file claims no equivalence between them. And the article's object is one myth read methodologically, not a typology of myths: its generalisations are offered as procedures for interpretation.

## 5. The three cuts side by side

| Dimension | Barthes | Todorov | Greimas |
|---|---|---|---|
| What is cut | levels of description | categories of the literary narrative | planes of the message |
| The seam runs between | a level and the level above it | the time of the story and the time of discourse | the discursive plane and the structural plane |
| Unit of analysis | the unit that integrates upward | the element and its correlations | the sequence and the content category |
| Named failure | a unit that integrates nowhere | an element with no correlative | a message read on one plane only |
| What it does not supply | a genre typology | a content architecture | a general theory of all narratives |

Read together, the three produce a checkable order rather than a synthesis. Barthes fixes the grammar (what units are and what they integrate into); Todorov fixes the telling (order, perception, discourse type) and defines meaning as correlation; Greimas fixes the content (the reversible state and the figure who carries the reversal). A draft that has a grammar and no telling is a taxonomy; one that has a telling and no content structure is a sequence of events; one that has content and no grammar cannot say what its units are.

What must not be done is to flatten the three into "structuralism says". The pages themselves show a chain of borrowings rather than a single doctrine.

Barthes credits the theory of levels to Benveniste on printed p. 5, the mythème to Lévi-Strauss, and names Todorov's two-level *histoire* / *discours* scheme as one proposal among others (S885).

Greimas builds on Lévi-Strauss's three components and says so on printed p. 29 (S887). A designer copying one cut should copy that cut and attribute it, because the three disagree about where meaning is carried — in an integration, in a correlation, or in a content structure.

## 6. Calibration

| Claim | Status | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative is a human universal | mainstream as an observation, contested as a law | the page asserts it for the narratives it surveys; no count or sample is offered |
| A narrative is describable as hierarchically ordered levels | mainstream as a descriptive method | it is the working assumption of the issue and of the linguistics it borrows from |
| A narrative's meaning is its content structure, readable on two planes | contested | Greimas offers it as a procedure for one myth, not as a result about all narratives |
| Temporal deformation is what distinguishes discourse from history | contested | Todorov reports it as the Formalists' position and adopts it; it is a lineage claim of 1966 |
| A narrative's meaning is an element's correlations | mainstream as a heuristic, contested as a definition | elegant and checkable, but it makes meaning wholly internal to the work |
| These categories are exhaustive or universal | discredited as an attribution | no page read here claims exhaustiveness, and the article's own list is presented as provisional |

The three articles are primary documents of a research programme, not consensus results, and the entry uses them at that grade: as method proposals whose usefulness is checkable against a draft, never as findings about how narratives work in general.

## 8. Checklist, runnable without the three articles

1. **Name the grammar.** For the culture, state what counts as a narrative unit and what each unit type integrates into.
2. **Separate model from instance.** Keep the grammar in one record and the individual tales in another; never let one tale stand for the system.
3. **Fix the telling.** For each narrative, name its order of telling, its framing perception, and its discourse type.
4. **State the marked deformations.** Which reorderings does this culture treat as normal, and which as marked or scandalous?
5. **Correlate every detail.** For each setting element used in a story, name at least one partner it correlates with; if there is none, cut it or plant one.
6. **Write the content structure.** Name the before-state and the after-state, and the transformation between them, separately from the sequence that carries it.
7. **Fill the mediator slot.** Name the figure who disjoins from the community and carries the reversal, or state that the reversal has no personal carrier.
8. **Check the two planes.** Retell the story in another medium: if the content structure survives and the sequence does not, the two planes were really kept apart.
9. **Hand off, do not decide.** Stop at the handoff to lore-shokunin; no entry sentence here fixes what is true in any world.
