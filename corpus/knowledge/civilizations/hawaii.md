## Coupling model: relations, not an island aesthetic
- **Analytical framework:** indexicality + speech-act analysis + performance transmission + material biography. These are research lenses, not translations of Hawaiian institutions.
- A name can locate kinship and an event; a chant can reactivate genealogy; a garment can make inherited authority visible; a restriction can regulate who approaches, speaks, eats, or performs.
- Do not infer a causal identity between these systems: an open-syllable language does not produce a particular religion, and a feather color does not determine a deity.
- Working record: `community/place → era → authority → utterance/name → mythic referent → access rule → material/performance → attestation`.
- **Unit of comparison:** a named practice, object, performance lineage, or historical episode; not an imaginary uniform “Polynesian civilization.”

## Language family, sound system, and writing
| Layer | Hawaiian baseline | Craft consequence / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy | Austronesian → Oceanic → Polynesian → Eastern Polynesian. [G] | Compare related languages historically; do not pool Hawaiian, Māori, Samoan, and Tahitian vocabulary into one lexicon. |
| Vowels | Five qualities /a e i o u/; short/long contrast, conventionally ā ē ī ō ū for long vowels. [G, D] | Track length in names, meter, and spelling; five vowel qualities does not mean only five contrastive vowel possibilities. |
| Consonants | Native core /p k ʔ h m n l w/; /w/ is also described with [v] realizations. [G] | Small inventory is not simple grammar or limited expressive power. |
| ʻOkina | ʻ represents a consonantal glottal stop, not decorative punctuation. [G, D] | Preserve it in names, search keys, and quotations; use U+02BB for normalized modern text. |
| Kahakō | Macron marks vowel length; it is not an accent meaning “stress here.” [G, D] | Removing diacritics can obscure distinct words; store original orthography alongside normalized metadata. |
| Phonotactics | Open syllables; no native consonant clusters or consonant codas; (C)V(V) is a useful introductory template. [G] | Decide separately how long vowels/diphthongs are analyzed; do not treat every adjacent vowel pair as one syllable. |
| Variation | [w]~[v] and [k]~[t] realizations occur; Niʻihau-associated usage matters. [G] | Standard written k does not license correcting every [t] to [k]; dialect-specific distributions need speaker evidence. |
| Prosody | Stress interacts with syllable weight and word structure. [G] | Do not generate pronunciation by an invariant “penultimate written letter” rule. |
| Grammar | Predicates, particles, possession, and discourse structure matter beyond sound inventories. [G] | A list of CV names is not a Hawaiian-like language; consult the seeded syntax and semantics methods. |

- **Writing history:** alphabetic print developed through missionary–Hawaiian collaboration in the early nineteenth century; Hawaiian writers subsequently made print a vehicle of theology, literature, governance, and resistance. [G, S]
- Earlier print frequently lacks modern ʻokina/kahakō conventions: that is an orthographic witness, not proof the contrasts were absent from speech. [G, D]
- Keep `diplomatic transcription`, `modernized spelling`, and `translation` as separate fields; never silently “repair” a name in a historical quotation.
- ʻŌlelo Hawaiʻi is distinct from Hawaiʻi Creole English, commonly called Pidgin; do not use one as a register of the other.
- **Fictional application:** independently specify vowel length, syllable licensing, sound change, and literacy history; then test how names and performed texts survive orthographic reform. Do not copy actual sacred names as conlang filler.

## Mythic and symbolic core: genealogy before a domain chart
| Node / relation | Reference knowledge | What not to flatten |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy and creation | The *Kumulipo* connects nonhuman and human generations and has chiefly genealogical/political significance. [M, S] | Not simply a universal creation catechism, an evolutionary-science textbook, or a free-standing monster catalogue. |
| Pō / ao | Darkness/night and light/day belong to cosmological and poetic fields. [M, D] | Do not mechanically translate them as evil/good. |
| Kāne, Kū, Lono, Kanaloa | Major akua have intersecting manifestations, relationships, and cult contexts; Lono is associated with Makahiki, agriculture, and fertility. [M] | A four-god game-role grid loses local forms; Kū is not exhausted by “war god.” |
| Pele and Hiʻiaka | Place, volcanic landscape, kin relations, journeys, and performance repertoires intersect. [M, H] | Not every hula is a Pele rite, and every volcanic place does not tell the same story. |
| Laka and hula | Laka is important in hula traditions. [M] | Precise identifications, offerings, and relationships to other figures are lineage-specific; details here remain thin `(to-verify)` before attribution to a hālau. |
| ʻAumākua | Ancestral guardian relationships belong to particular families; embodiments and obligations vary. [D, N] | An animal emblem is not automatically a person's universal “spirit animal.” |
| Mana, kapu, noa | Power/efficacy and restricted/released conditions interrelate with ancestry, persons, acts, and things. [D, M] | Neither a numerical magic resource nor a permanent sacred/profane binary explains every use. |

- **Symbol method:** annotate bearer, place, narrative, occasion, material, and interpretive authority before assigning a meaning to a bird, plant, color, or geometric form.
- **Kaona:** concealed reference or layered meaning in poetry; comprehension can depend on shared knowledge of a person, place, or event. [D, s.v. *kaona*; [online entry](https://hilo.hawaii.edu/wehe/?q=kaona)]
- Polysemy is productive evidence, not permission to claim every resemblance conceals an ancient message.

## Kapu as legal linguistics: authority, scope, and release
- **Attested baseline:** kapu joins religious, social, and political restrictions; noa includes being free of a restriction. Translation as merely “superstition” or merely modern secular statute is inadequate. [D, M, K]
- **Analytical claim only:** “legal linguistics” asks how authoritative communication establishes or changes obligations. It does not assert a historically separate Hawaiian legal language or special grammatical mood `(to-verify)`.
- Model each norm as `authorized issuer + affected persons + act/object + place/time + recognized communication + consequence + release`.

| Dimension | Historical anchor | Design operation, not reconstructed Hawaiian law |
|---|---|---|
| Person and relation | Chiefly genealogy and sacred status affect encounters; food restrictions distinguish social categories. [M, K] | Make a rule relational: who addresses whom, with what entitlement? |
| Food and commensality | ʻAi kapu includes restrictions on men and women eating together. [D, K] | A meal can communicate a jurisdictional change without a written statute. |
| Space and time | Sacred sites and ritual periods have access conditions. [M, K] | Give a rule territorial and calendrical scope rather than “always forbidden.” |
| Public uptake | Accounts of the 1819 ʻAi Noa include chiefly action, a shared meal, and messengers announcing change. [K] | Distinguish utterance, authorized act, witness, dissemination, and contested acceptance. |
| Sanction / refuge | Severe punishments are recorded; puʻuhonua offered sanctuary in specified circumstances. [K] | Include institutions of refuge and restoration; do not reduce the whole order to instant execution. |
| Release | Noa is a change of condition; lifting a restriction is not necessarily desecration. [D] | Specify who can release a rule and how people recognize that release. |

- **1819 hinge:** Liholiho/Kamehameha II, Kaʻahumanu, and Keōpūolani are central to accounts of ending ʻai kapu; resistance and conflict followed. Missionaries arrived in 1820, after this break. [K]
- Do not give missionaries sole agency for that event, or turn the actions of high-ranking women into proof that gender restrictions had never existed.
- Conversely, food restrictions do not mean all women were politically powerless; genealogy, office, and gender are intersecting, not interchangeable ranks.
- Neither every kapu breach nor every period had the same sanction; the sources here do not supply a complete offence-and-penalty schedule `(to-verify)`.
- Speech, bodily proximity, silence, names, and visible insignia can be investigated together; a specific royal naming prohibition or formula needs its own attestation, not inference from the word *kapu*.
- **Fictional test:** ask whether a validly announced release changes clothing access, performance rights, and address forms immediately or unevenly. Keep competing interpretations visible to lore-shokunin.

## Era model: overlapping institutions, not a frozen “ancient Hawaiʻi”
| Stage | Coupling to track | Historical guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Settlement and pre-1778 regional histories | Oral genealogies, changing chiefly formations, cultivation, barkcloth and featherwork technologies | Not one unchanging precontact era; a precise settlement date or phase sequence needs archaeological evidence beyond this entry `(to-verify)`. |
| 1778–1819 encounter and kingdom formation | Foreign trade and goods coexist with chiefly ritual authority; Kalaniʻōpuʻu's 1779 cloak transfer supplies an object-level anchor. [J, K] | Imported objects do not instantly replace local codes. |
| 1819–1820 institutional rupture | ʻAi Noa precedes missionary arrival; authority, worship, and public conduct are renegotiated. [K] | A formal break is not disappearance of all ancestral practice. |
| Nineteenth-century kingdom, to 1893 | Print, written governance, Christianity, inherited genealogies, mele, court performance, and imported dress coexist. [S] | Court innovation is not automatically inauthentic; print is not merely colonial speech. |
| 1893 overthrow; 1898 annexation and subsequent colonial rule | Hawaiian-language political organization and resistance; performance and family transmission under changing constraints. [S] | Do not equate loss of sovereignty with willing cultural replacement. |
| Late twentieth century–present | Hawaiian Renaissance, language revitalization, performance lineages, new naming contexts, and return of cultural treasures. [H, W, J] | Living knowledge is neither untouched antiquity nor a recent invention without antecedents. |

- **Staging rule:** date an object, its recorded use, a text's composition, its publication, and a current performance separately. Their dates often differ.
- A reconstruction set in one stage must not silently import a later spelling convention, tourist costume, or performance arrangement as timeless tradition.

## Costume and material signature: ʻahu ʻula as rank code
- **Attested:** ʻahu ʻula feather cloaks/capes and mahiole feather helmets are chiefly insignia, expressing rank, sacred authority, and power; they are not generic island clothing. [T]
- **Analytical code:** entitlement to wear + accumulated labor/material + genealogy/ownership + occasion + audience recognition. Not “one color equals one numerical rank.”

| Material / sign | Coupling | Evidence limit / craft control |
|---|---|---|
| Feather bundles on olonā cordage netting | Forest resources, specialized skill, time, and chiefly command become wearable authority. [T] | Describe a netted feather construction, not a woven feather textile; avoid unverified labor-hour totals. |
| Red and yellow fields / patterned arrangement | Spectacular visibility and prized materials communicate chiefly distinction. [T, J] | No universal red-to-yellow promotion ladder or documented rank-by-chevron table is established here `(to-verify)`. |
| Feather species | Bird ecology constrains available colors and supply. | Species identifications for any chosen cloak require its catalogue/conservation record `(to-verify)`; do not guess from hue alone. |
| Mahiole with cloak | Multiple objects frame a chiefly body as an ensemble. [T] | A helmet, cloak, genealogy, and occasion must be read together; shape alone is not a complete rank key. |
| Kapa; malo and pāʻū | Barkcloth production and garment forms supply a wider dress vocabulary beyond royal featherwork. [D, relevant headwords] | Kapa is beaten barkcloth, not loom-woven cloth; wearer, material, and arrangement require era-specific evidence. |
| Lei and plant adornment | Plant choice can couple locality, occasion, and performance. [D] | A specific plant-to-akua equivalence or mandatory hula costume needs lineage and occasion evidence `(to-verify)`. |
| Later imported clothing | Existing status systems can incorporate new materials and silhouettes. [S] | Do not assume a foreign textile makes the wearer less Hawaiian or the rank claim meaningless. |

- **Object biography:** Kalaniʻōpuʻu gave the cloak and helmet he wore to Cook in 1779; these treasures later entered overseas collections, returned on loan in 2016, and were permanently repatriated in 2020. [T, J]
- The gift establishes a recorded diplomatic event; it does not prove every future wearer acquired the donor's genealogical rank.
- Museum survival overrepresents elite, collectible garments; it cannot provide a census of everyday clothing or a universal dress law.
- Do not repeat “all birds were released unharmed” or precise bird/feather counts without species, object, and historical qualifications; ecological symbolism is not proof of harmless harvesting.
- **Fictional application:** build a resource-to-insignia chain, then test shortage, inheritance, capture, gifting, repair, and contested ownership. Invent materials and symbols rather than reproducing a specific chiefly treasure.

## Hula as myth-archive: repertoire, embodiment, and interpretation
- **Analytical label:** “archive” means a maintained repertoire of language, sound, movement, persons, and places; not a recording medium that preserves an event without selection or change.
- Mele and hula can sustain genealogy, cosmology, place memory, chiefly praise, and political argument; repertoire is not limited to stories about gods. [M, S, P]
- Hula is not synonymous with all chant; *oli* and *mele* require genre/context-sensitive definitions, not a simple sacred/secular opposition. [D]
- A kumu hula and hālau provide situated teaching authority; a published transcription does not replace a performance lineage. [H]

| Archive layer | Record together | Failure if separated |
|---|---|---|
| Words | Hawaiian text, orthography, translation, speaker/editor, witness date | An English paraphrase erases sound, ambiguity, and possible kaona. |
| Genre / form | Text organization, accompaniment, rhythm, and named genre | “Ancient chant” conceals differences needed to perform or compare it. |
| Movement | Performer/teacher, occasion, recording conditions, variant | A universal hand-gesture dictionary is inferred where lineage/context is needed. |
| Myth and place | Named referents, genealogy, landscape, interpretive source | A place-name becomes picturesque background rather than historical relation. |
| Costume and plants | Date, materials, availability, authority for their use | A recent staging choice becomes invented evidence of an ancient mandatory uniform. |
| Transmission | Who taught whom; what is public, restricted, revised, or uncertain | Variant becomes error, or publication is mistaken for permission. |

- **Concrete genre comparison:** Stillman (2009) describes hula ʻālaʻapapa with ipu heke accompaniment; its stanzas, when present, need not be equal in length. Her comparison describes hula ʻōlapa with repeated tune/stanza organization and an intervening pā. [H]
- Do not reverse that diagnostic: ipu accompaniment alone does not identify every performance as ʻālaʻapapa. This is a comparison within her account, not a rule for classifying all hālau.
- “Kahiko” and “ʻauana” are useful contemporary distinctions, not proof that one repertoire is unmodified precontact survival and the other has no ancestral continuity.
- A myth can remain recognizable while diction, accompaniment, staging, and costume change on different timelines; preserve those differences rather than invent a lost original performance.
- **Fictional application:** specify what words, teachers, costumes, and audiences jointly authenticate; let two repertoires preserve different claims without declaring either fictional canon here.

## Inoa: name-giving as an authorized relation, not a naming generator
- **Attested scope:** names can convey ancestry, historic events, place, aspirations, and spiritual relationships; one person or place can bear more than one name. [N via W; P]
- [N]'s naming discussion is culturally situated; the categories below do not establish a mandatory six-part ceremony for every Hawaiian family.

| Attested category | Coupling | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Inoa kupuna | Ancestral names transmit kinship and remembrance. [P] | Not automatically a hereditary surname or title; family-specific responsibilities need evidence. |
| Inoa hoʻomanaʻo | Commemorates a person or event; may attach to people and places. [P] | The Nihoa names associated with Kaʻahumanu's 1822 voyage provide a specific example, not a template for all names. |
| Inoa pō | A name received in a dream, understood in some accounts as binding for the infant's well-being. [D, s.v. *inoa pō*] | Reported belief, not a clinical claim; [direct dictionary entry](https://hilo.hawaii.edu/wehe/?q=inoa%20p%C5%8D). |
| Inoa hōʻailona / inoa ʻūlāleo | Sign/vision and heard-voice origins link naming to spiritual communication. [W, citing N] | Their recognition and authority are family/context-dependent; do not fabricate a universal omen-decoding chart. |
| Inoa kūamuamu | Reviling names can carry protective or adversarial purposes in the accounts summarized by [W]. | Do not turn these into comic “ugly baby names”; specific cases and renaming procedures require the original account `(to-verify)`. |

- **Ritual mechanics worth studying:** who may propose or receive a name; whose relationship validates it; who must agree; how it is bestowed and acknowledged; when a name can be changed.
- These questions are an analytical checklist, not an attested Hawaiian ceremony sequence. Exact prayers, officiants, timing, restrictions, and gestures are not established here `(to-verify)`.
- [W] discusses ʻoki, severing/cutting, in accounts of name change; that does not authorize supplying a reconstructed ritual script from a gloss.
- Do not presume every family maintains the same ancestral religious practice today, or that Christian and Hawaiian naming relationships are mutually exclusive.
- **Fictional application:** design the relationship before the phonetic form: `giver/recipient → grounds for authority → remembered referent → public/private use → consequences of misuse → change procedure`.
- Use independent fictional etymologies; a dictionary translation plus Hawaiian-looking syllables does not establish an authentic Hawaiian name or permission to bestow one.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **A coupled authority model:** speech and recognized acts alter access; costume indexes claims; repertoire and names retain the relations that make those claims intelligible.
- **A non-numeric rank model:** a garment's force can derive from entitlement, labor, ancestry, biography, and audience, not an arbitrary color ladder.
- **A distributed memory model:** names, places, texts, teaching lineages, and objects carry overlapping but non-identical histories.
- **A layered era model:** institutional rupture, language change, dress adoption, religious continuity, and performance innovation need not occur simultaneously.
- **A provenance-bearing design packet:** hand lore-shokunin options with `source + date/place + confidence + fictional transformation + unresolved contradiction`; lore decides whether anything becomes canon.
- **Consistency exercise:** propose a fictional change to naming authority; trace effects on address, performance entitlement, inheritance, and insignia. Where no link follows, leave the systems partly independent rather than forcing total integration.
- Take relational design principles, not named akua, ancestral names, sacred texts, chiefly garment designs, or living ceremonial protocols as interchangeable assets.

## Provenance cautions
- Hawaiian scholarship and community knowledge are not interchangeable with missionary, explorer, collector, or later folklorist accounts; retain author, language, translator, date, and purpose for each witness.
- [M] is valuable but historically situated; its comparative arrangement and older vocabulary must not become evidence of a single bounded “primitive religion.” [S] is a corrective to English-only colonial historiography, not a replacement source for every precontact detail.
- [K] supplies useful chronology, but blanket assertions that every breach meant death or that all religion ended in 1819 exceed what this entry can responsibly generalize.
- [T, J] include community perspectives and a traceable object biography; institutional ownership history alone does not settle the full indigenous meaning of a gift, treasure, or return.
- [W] is a journalist's synthesis and interview, not direct inspection of every source it cites. The [N] page range is a research lead; collate it before quoting exact wording or claiming a uniform ritual.
- Thin coverage here: island-specific legal speech, historical naming performances, everyday clothing distributions, and internal hālau protocols. Public chronicle evidence is uneven; absence of a written account is not absence of a practice.
- Living traditions require contemporary, named community authority for detailed attribution. A published description is evidence of a description, not blanket permission to reenact or commercialize it.
- Reject tourist essentialism, a universal color/symbol dictionary, “one people = one myth,” and a single Campbell-style hero path as historical explanations. Such grids may be optional fiction heuristics, never proof of Hawaiian structure.
- All fictional applications above are analytical proposals only; unresolved claims retain `(to-verify)` and no canon is written.
