## 1. Scope: a cultural field, not a single timeless population

- Distinguish the plateau's Tibetan-speaking cultural regions from successive states and present administrative borders; neither “Tibet” nor “Tibetan” fixes one polity, livelihood, dress, or pronunciation.
- Ü-Tsang, Kham, and Amdo are useful regional reference labels, not internally homogeneous culture blocks; borderlands and diaspora require their own staging.
- **Language family:** Tibetic languages belong to Sino-Tibetan, also called Trans-Himalayan; “Tibeto-Burman” is a broader classificatory label, not a synonym for Tibetan (Tournadre 2014).
- **Linguistic split:** Old Tibetan documentary language, Classical/Literary Tibetan, and living Tibetic vernaculars are different objects. A shared written standard does not establish mutual intelligibility.
- **Typological baseline:** verb-final structures, postpositions/case marking, and extensive register distinctions; tone and consonant-cluster preservation vary. Do not impose Lhasa pronunciation on Amdo or every Himalayan Tibetic language.
- **Design constraint:** assign each proposed word a spoken variety, written register, period, and social user before assigning mythic or costume significance.

## 2. Script creation: charter narrative versus historical reconstruction

| Question | Traditional account | Historical calibration | Coupling lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who creates writing? | Thonmi/Tönmi Sambhota, minister under Songtsen Gampo, learns in India and adapts letters | The individual and itinerary are not securely verifiable; later narrative witnesses vary | A named inventor can personify a collective institutional project |
| Why create it? | Royal wisdom, access to Buddhist teaching, ordering the realm | Earlier annal/chronicle evidence foregrounds government, laws, rank, and administration | Sacred literacy and taxation can share infrastructure without sharing an origin story |
| Which external model? | Different histories name Indian teachers and scripts | Indic derivation is strong; van Schaik argues for North Indian/Nepalese models, not an uncontested exact prototype | Separate visible script ancestry from the culture's preferred ancestry |
| Did the founder write the grammars? | *Sum cu pa* and *Rtags kyi 'jug pa* are attributed to him | Received texts show post-imperial writing or rewriting; attribution does not date every surviving passage | Founder-authority can authorize later standardization |
| Are alternative ancestries irrelevant? | Bön accounts connect script to Zhangzhung and sacred western origins | These are significant traditions, not securely demonstrated ancient script genealogies | Rival histories relocate the sacred center without supplying equivalent archaeological proof |

- Van Schaik (2011:49–54) treats the *Pillar Testament* and *Testament of Ba* as important Thonmi-story witnesses; do not repeat the claim that the fourteenth-century historian Butön supplies its earliest version.
- The *Old Tibetan Annals* entries for 654–655 describe administrative preparation and the writing of laws; they are records of events in those years, **not surviving physical manuscripts written in those years**.
- The same study discusses the Zhol pillar, approximately 767, and later eighth-/ninth-century documentary witnesses; distinguish event date, composition date, manuscript date, and modern edition date.
- **Scarcity:** the seventh-century material record is thin. A letter reportedly sent abroad need not have been written in Tibetan; Chinese-language diplomatic literacy is not proof of a Tibetan-script autograph.
- **Script mechanics:** a Brahmic abugida, with inherent /a/, vowel signs, and consonant stacking; a syllable delimiter is not a dependable word separator. Sanskrit transcription requires additional conventions.
- **Orthographic depth:** conservative spellings preserve distinctions not retained in every modern pronunciation; the written form cannot simply be sounded out using English letter values.
- **Register/material coupling:** headed *dbu can* and headless *dbu med* traditions are not different languages; associate a particular hand with a dated document practice, not “holy letters versus peasant letters.”
- **Transliteration discipline:** Wylie represents spelling, not speech. Keep `written_form`, `reader_pronunciation`, and `common_English_name` separate; `sprul sku` and “tulku” are not alternate phonetic spellings of equal precision.
- **Apply:** build a ledger of administrative adoption, translation norms, scribal training, and later founder legends. Their timelines may overlap without being identical.

## 3. Bön and Buddhism: lexical strata, not two sealed dictionaries

- **Contested:** whether early *bon* named an organized religion, a ritual domain, specialists, or overlapping categories is debated; Karmay and Stein are not interchangeable authorities (van Schaik 2009).
- **Established distinction:** organized Yungdrung Bön documented from the later diffusion period is not simply an unchanged survival of all pre-Buddhist practice. Its own accounts of antiquity remain meaningful traditional claims.
- **Avoid the inverse error:** shared monastic, tantric, and textual forms do not make Bön a “fake Buddhism”; exchange and rivalry can coexist with a community's distinct self-identification.

| Form / domain | Attested distinction or change | What not to infer | Craft operation |
|---|---|---|---|
| *bon*, *bon po* | Early textual uses concern rites or specialists; later uses can identify a religious tradition and its adherents | Every early *bon po* belonged to a later monastic school | Track category expansion and later retrospective labeling |
| *gshen* | Ritual specialists appear alongside *bon po* in Miran records | A universal priestly rank or automatic synonym for “shaman” | Define who officiates, commissions, assists, and records |
| *chos* | Commonly Buddhist Dharma in later contexts; early usage can denote other sets of practices, including *bon chos* | One word has always meant exclusively “Buddhism” | Let conversion reassign an existing prestige word |
| *g.yung drung* | Eternity/endurance vocabulary in early royal contexts; varied Buddhist translation uses; later central to Yungdrung Bön | A stable sect label wherever the word occurs | Stage semantic narrowing and rival claims to continuity |
| Buddhist translation register | Tibetan words can calque Indic concepts rather than merely import Sanskrit sounds | Every Buddhist technical word is a phonological loan | Choose separately between borrowing, calquing, and redefining |
| Sanskrit mantra register | Sacred sound can be retained and represented in Tibetan script | The mantra is ordinary Tibetan prose | Keep ritual language competence distinct from vernacular fluency |
| *yul lha*, territorial deity | Local supplication appears in early ritual evidence and persists in multiple religious settings | Every local deity or offering is exclusively Bön | Permit different institutions to negotiate with the same landscape |

- **Anchor, not fantasy reconstruction:** van Schaik (2012) discusses Miran docket IOL Tib J 255, recording a local-deity rite with specialist roles; its imperial-era placement is argued from context and palaeography, not an exact securely stated day.
- **Genre warning:** a Buddhist polemic describing funeral rites records an opponent's classification, not an unfiltered self-description. Van Schaik's 2009 reading of Or.8210/S.12243 explicitly retains translation problems.
- **Contact model:** add `earliest_attestation`, `genre`, `speaker_alignment`, `source_language`, `later_sense`, and `certainty` to each lexical item. A borrowed concept, a native word, and a later identity label can inhabit one entry.
- **Apply:** model conversion as semantic competition plus changes in patronage, ritual labor, and textual authority—not replacement of one complete vocabulary by another overnight.

## 4. Mythic and pantheon core: identify relations, not equivalent gods

| Domain | Tibetan reference point | Calibration / design use |
|---|---|---|
| Buddhist liberative hierarchy | Śākyamuni; buddhas and bodhisattvas; Avalokiteśvara/Chenrezig; Tārā | Not a family of creator gods; distinguish enlightenment, compassion, and ritual roles |
| Sacred kingship | Later Buddhist histories associate rulers with awakened figures, notably Songtsen Gampo with Avalokiteśvara | Retrospective sacred history is not a contemporary governmental job description |
| Transmission and landscape | Padmasambhava/Guru Rinpoche narratives connect teaching, sites, and the binding of hostile powers | A landscape can be incorporated through promises and obligations rather than extermination |
| Yungdrung Bön sacred history | Tönpa Shenrab Miwo as founding teacher; distinct sacred geography and iconographic lineages | Do not supply a historically verified ancient birth date from a sacred chronology |
| Territorial and environmental beings | *yul lha*, *lha*, *klu* and other context-specific classes | English “god,” “demon,” and “nature spirit” are approximate, not exhaustive translations |

- Use Stein (1972) and Kvaerne (1995) as orientation, then verify the specific image, lineage, and ritual before reusing an identification; this entry does not inventory every deity or regional practice.
- **Wrathful ≠ evil:** fierce iconography and protective or liberative functions can coincide; a peaceful/wrathful distinction is not a two-team moral alignment chart.
- **Calibration:** “Bön = primitive shamanism, Buddhism = rational philosophy” is a discredited civilizational ladder. “Lamaism” often imports an older outsider frame; use specific tradition and institution names instead.
- **Apply:** map `being → narrative role → ritual relationship → place → specialist → material sign`. Avoid mapping an entire pantheon onto a universal hero-journey or archetype grid.

## 5. Symbolic systems and prayer-wheel mantra economies

- **Core coupling:** Avalokiteśvara/Chenrezig and *oṃ maṇi padme hūṃ* link sacred sound, script, a rotating text-bearing object, and practices of compassion. Do not present one English word-for-word rendering as settled.
- **Object distinction:** a prayer wheel is not the same thing as the Dharma-wheel emblem, a mandala, a rosary, or a prayer flag, even where symbols or practices overlap.
- **Semantic history:** *g.yung drung* also names the swastika sign and carries endurance/eternity associations; its meanings vary by setting. Modern display also requires awareness of Nazi appropriation, not historical conflation.
- **Orientation rule:** record the community's prescribed rotation and circumambulation direction; precise Buddhist/Bön comparisons for a named rite are `(to-verify)` here. Never make “left = evil” a universal rule.
- **“Mantra economy” is an analytical framework here:** follow the production, circulation, authorization, and attribution of meritorious activity, not a claim that Tibetans treat all religion as a commercial market.

| Layer | Evidence / analytical distinction | Fiction-design question |
|---|---|---|
| Text | Repeated mantras enclosed in the wheel; outer inscriptions and inner contents are not the same evidential surface | Who verifies the hidden text, copies it, and corrects errors? |
| Motion | Handheld or installed wheels; practitioner teachings also describe water-, wind-, and heat-driven forms | Whose bodily work or maintained infrastructure sustains the action? |
| Merit attribution | Zopa's 1994 teaching explicitly equates one turn with recitation of the enclosed repetitions | Who states the equivalence, under which teaching, and for whose benefit? |
| Intention | The same teaching joins turning with visualization and benefit to beings | Does the community distinguish mechanical action, aspiration, attention, and authorization? |
| Patronage | A narrated donor hopes to benefit fish because family wealth came from fishing | How does a livelihood create obligations that motivate sponsorship? |
| Technology | The 1994 teaching describes microfilm as well as manuscripts | Does greater textual density change prestige, trust, access, or maintenance? |
| Distribution | Practitioners dedicate benefit beyond the immediate operator | Is the expected recipient the sponsor, household, dead, animals, or a wider community? |

- **Evidence limit:** these equivalences are emic—claims within the tradition—not measured conversion rates between physical motion and verified supernatural effects.
- **Do not import medical assertions:** the transcript includes healing claims; this entry uses it as religious testimony, not evidence that wheels cure cancer or HIV/AIDS.
- **Dating limit:** a teaching traces the practice through Nāgārjuna and Padmasambhava; this is a lineage narrative, not secure dating of the first physical prayer wheel. Specific origin date `(to-verify)`.
- **No reductive automation story:** textual sanctity, recollection, visualization, compassionate intention, skilled manufacture, and patronage may all matter; “a machine replaces belief” explains too little.
- **Apply:** specify the text-bearing medium, authority, ritual action, attribution rule, beneficiaries, fabrication costs, and maintenance. Then test a change of medium without assuming every practitioner accepts it.

## 6. Era staging: institutions develop on different clocks

| Stage | Language / religion / authority | Material and costume caution |
|---|---|---|
| Before imperial consolidation | Sparse direct evidence; later sacred histories claim deep antiquity | Do not back-project photographed twentieth-century dress or a complete later pantheon |
| Seventh–mid-ninth centuries: empire | Writing and administrative expansion; Buddhist patronage and translation develop alongside other rites | Separate imperial rank systems from later monastic hierarchies; retain uncertain material reconstructions |
| Tenth–twelfth centuries: regional revival and later diffusion | Renewed translation and institutions; increasingly visible organized Bön traditions | Textual histories may redefine the imperial past; no single plateau-wide costume transition follows automatically |
| Thirteenth–sixteenth centuries | Lineages, Mongol patronage, and recognized rebirth institutions develop | Do not assume all succession procedures or later court insignia already exist |
| Central Tibetan Ganden Phodrang rule, 1642–1959 on the plateau | Government and religious offices intertwine; late-eighteenth-century Qing interventions include the Golden Urn | Central Tibetan court dress is not the dress of every Tibetan region or occupation |
| Twentieth century onward: upheaval, revival, diaspora | State restructuring, exile, disrupted institutions, new print and digital environments | Date suppression/revival claims locally; ceremonial dress, everyday clothing, and political self-presentation may diverge |

- This is a working synchronization model, not an indigenous periodization or a full political history (Stein 1972; Gamble 2018; Dalai Lama 2011; Tibet Album; Yongdan 2018).
- **Recognition-history correction:** the traditional “first recognition” narrative centers on early Karmapas; Gamble emphasizes the Third Karmapa, Rangjung Dorjé (1284–1339), and the institutionalization of multi-life narrative. Name the historian and question rather than assert one uncontested invention event.
- **Apply:** maintain separate timelines for orthography, translation policy, monastery property, state recognition, textile supply, and public dress. Synchronize them only with a causal bridge.

## 7. Chuba and material signatures: codes are conditional

| Material / practice | Secure descriptive baseline | Status inference / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Chuba, written *phyu pa* | Wrap robe with generous front/waist; Smithsonian describes right-side fastening | Record cut, wearer, locality, season, and occasion before inferring identity |
| Wool / *pulu* | Spinning and weaving produce robe cloth; Smithsonian distinguishes several material constructions | Sheep wool, sheepskin, lambskin, and yak hair are not interchangeable labels |
| Skin-lined / skin robes | Insulation and material availability shape construction | Do not infer one uniform “nomad costume” from a skin garment |
| Light robes / decorative facings | Robe weight and collar, cuff, front, or hem treatment vary | Brocade, silk, and elaborate trim require dated trade and wealth evidence, not automatic religious rank |
| Belted volume and sleeve management | Modern descriptions record a capacious belted form and variable ways of wearing sleeves | A particular one-sleeve practice or exact fastening diagram needs a dated visual witness `(to-verify)` |
| Pangden, striped apron | A recognizable component in many Tibetan women's dress descriptions | Central Tibetan associations with married status are common; exact regional eligibility and historical rule `(to-verify)` |
| Jewelry and headwear | Can differentiate locality, resources, ceremonial role, and affiliation | A turquoise/coral combination alone cannot securely identify tribe, marital status, or spiritual attainment |
| Monastic / ritual garments | Institutional robes and specialized ritual attire are separate dress systems | Chuba is not a universal monk's robe; a ritual crown is not everyday headwear |

- **Construction first:** use Kyi/Smithsonian (2019) for robe materials and broad construction, then a dated object or photograph for a specific cut. Its “thousands of years” continuity language does not prove an unchanged ancient garment.
- **Economic signature:** separate fiber production, weaving, tailoring, imported luxury cloth, metalwork, and jewelry circulation; costly appearance is an outcome of supply and access, not just symbolic preference.
- **Values can reverse:** Yongdan (2018) documents otter fur's prestige and the changed consumption associated with the Dalai Lama's 2006 criticism. Do not freeze fur trim as an eternal marker of admired rank.
- **Visual-source protocol:** record image identifier, photographer, date, place, caption author, named wearer if known, and whether the scene is staged. The Tibet Album overrepresents certain Central Tibetan and official encounters.
- **Thin coverage:** this entry does not establish historical sumptuary laws, a complete regional hat taxonomy, or exact apron rules. Those claims require object-level research `(to-verify)`, not decorative extrapolation.
- **Apply:** write a conditional code: `material + cut + wearing practice + occasion + observer`. Allow observers to misread outsiders, borrowed clothes, or revived ceremonial styles.

## 8. Naming and reincarnation-recognition nomenclature

- **Personal names:** many Tibetan names combine auspicious or religious elements rather than a Western-style given-name/hereditary-surname pair; clan, house, estate, and place identifiers also occur. Do not ban surnames or treat one pattern as universal (Stein 1972; Goldstein 2001).
- **Lexical examples:** Tashi, *bkra shis*, auspiciousness; Sönam/Sonam, *bsod nams*, merit; Tenzin, *bstan 'dzin*, holder of the teaching. Spellings, local pronunciation, and English display forms are different fields.
- **Life-course layers:** a birth name, religiously bestowed name, institutional title, and later recognized-lineage name need not coincide; verify the specific biography before assuming a required renaming sequence.

| Term / form | What it identifies | Frequent category error |
|---|---|---|
| Tulku, *sprul sku* | Emanation body in doctrine; also a recognized reincarnate religious figure institutionally | “Any reborn person” or proof that recognition establishes enlightened attainment |
| Yangsi, *yang srid* | Rebirth / renewed existence; used for a predecessor's recognized rebirth | A surname, an academic degree, or necessarily a separate office from tulku |
| Lama, *bla ma* | Religious teacher/title | Every lama is a monk or every lama is a tulku |
| Rinpoche, *rin po che* | Honorific, “precious”; its use depends on social and institutional context | A unique recognition grade or evidence of a particular lineage |
| Karmapa | A particular lineage title borne by successive recognized figures | A generic word meaning “reincarnated monk” |
| Dalai Lama | A specific title/lineage; “Dalai” is Mongolian, coupled with Tibetan “lama” | An ancient universal headship over all Tibetan religions |
| *ngos 'dzin* | Identification/recognition vocabulary | Recognition is identical to enthronement, ordination, or governmental approval |
| *khri 'don* | Enthronement / installation | The ceremony by itself resolves every dispute over who recognized whom |
| Labrang, *bla brang* | A lama's institutional household/estate | Merely the biological family of the recognized child |

- Lexical senses follow Goldstein (2001); institutional distinctions and doctrinal caveats are explicit in the Dalai Lama's 2011 statement. Local title etiquette remains case-specific.
- **Procedures are plural:** the 2011 account includes predictive letters, recollections, recognition of possessions/associates, divination, oracles, and sacred-lake visions. Not every recognition uses every method or follows a universal checklist.
- **Actors differ:** predecessor, search party, recognizing master, monastery, estate managers, patrons, family, and state may perform different acts. Ask which act each source calls “recognition.”
- **Numbering is retrospective:** the title Dalai Lama was bestowed on the Third in the line; earlier numbering must not imply that predecessors used the same title in their lifetimes (Dalai Lama 2011).
- **Not always one-to-one:** the same statement discusses female and lay recognized figures, and multiple emanations; do not reduce the institution to one male monk's soul automatically inheriting a throne.
- **Doctrine ≠ a permanent soul:** Buddhist rebirth and emanation require their own conceptual distinctions; “the same immortal personality returns unchanged” is not an adequate gloss.
- **State protocol has a history:** the Golden Urn belongs to late-eighteenth-century Qing intervention, not the institution's medieval beginning. Exact use in individual successions `(to-verify)` against case evidence and Oidtmann (2018), not only the 2011 institutional statement.
- **Freshness limit:** that statement is dated 2011. It must not be presented as a complete current account of later declarations or recognition policy; this entry adjudicates no living claimant's legitimacy.
- **Apply:** store `personal_name`, `name_giver`, `seat_or_house`, `lineage_title`, `ordinal`, `recognizing_authority`, `procedures`, `recognition_date`, `installation_date`, and `disputed_by`. A title is a relationship claim, not just flavorful phonology.

## 9. What a worldbuilder can take

- **Literacy charter:** combine a practical administrative script project with a later heroic-founder narrative; preserve both the archival history and the community's sacred explanation without pretending they are the same evidence.
- **Semantic palimpsest:** let a term for enduring royal order become a translated religious term, then a contested identity label; pair each sense with an attestation and a user community.
- **Materialized liturgy:** design a sacred-text object whose fabrication, movement, maintenance, and sponsorship matter together. Treat disputed innovations as questions of authority, not “superstition versus engineering.”
- **Conditional dress grammar:** connect ecology and trade to cut, then connect occasion and institutions to wearing rules; allow a later ethical movement to overturn the prestige of a material.
- **Recognition as infrastructure:** design biography, sites, titles, institutional households, procedures, and patrons together. Recognition, enthronement, property succession, and political authorization can diverge.
- **Coupling test:** for each proposed title or object, identify its language layer, legitimating narrative, visible sign, production chain, institutional user, and earliest plausible era. Unsupported links remain proposals.
- **Handoff to lore-shokunin:** deliver alternative mechanisms, dependencies, reference sources, and uncertainty; do not decide a fictional lineage, prescribe canon costumes, invent a sacred name, or enter a world-bible fact.

## 10. Provenance cautions and stop rules

- **Evidence hierarchy is question-dependent:** an inscription can date a title, a manuscript can attest a sense, a ritual manual can prescribe a rite, and a photograph can document one outfit; none alone proves universal practice or historical continuity.
- **Sparse chronicles:** early records do not permit a complete pre-Buddhist creed, a Thonmi biography, or precise script-invention day. Later traditions are evidence of memory and authority, not automatically eyewitness reporting.
- **Colonial bias:** British photographs, collectors, captions, and older accounts select and interpret what they show. Retain useful object evidence while rejecting “timeless mystical Tibet,” civilizational ladders, and exoticizing social categories.
- **Community voices also have genres:** devotional testimony, institutional statements, chronicles, and polemics have different aims; identifying standpoint is not grounds to dismiss practitioners' knowledge of their own practice.
- **Access disclosure:** direct evidence here is strongest for van Schaik's script study/manuscript notes, the 1994 practitioner transcript, the 2011 recognition statement, Smithsonian description, and checked publisher abstracts. Bibliographic verification does not equal full-book or plate inspection.
- **Known thin areas:** regional chuba construction, apron/marital rules, ritual-direction exceptions, early Bön pantheon reconstruction, and specific Golden Urn cases require further verification. Keep their `(to-verify)` markers in downstream notes.
- **Stop rule:** if an attractive name, costume code, or ritual origin rests only on an unsourced shop page, an undated image, a modern analogy, or an asserted universal archetype, do not promote it to a Tibetan fact—or a lore-shokunin canon claim.
