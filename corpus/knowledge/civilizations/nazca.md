## Place and era model

- Core setting: the arid south coast of Peru, especially the Río Grande de Nasca drainage and Ica Valley; irrigable valley floors contrast with adjacent pampas.
- “Nazca” and “Nasca” are modern spelling variants in scholarship, not evidence for two ancient peoples or a recovered autonym.
- A useful working envelope for classic Nasca material is approximately 100 BCE–650 CE; broader regional schemes carry late/terminal developments farther into the first millennium CE.
- Do not synchronize every valley, ceramic phase, monument, and hydraulic structure to one start/end date. Proulx (2006), Silverman and Proulx (2002), and Sakai et al. (2024) use evidence with different chronological resolutions.

| Stage / analytical label | Material or institutional emphasis | Era-staging rule for fiction |
|---|---|---|
| Paracas antecedents and Initial/Proto-Nasca, including phase 1 | Inherited textile and mortuary traditions; changing ceramic technology; early geoglyph traditions | Show selective continuity rather than a civilization appearing fully formed |
| Early Nasca, conventionally phases 2–4 | “Monumental” ceramic style; Cahuachi's major ceremonial role | Coordinate pilgrimage, portable images, and ceremonial display without assuming an empire |
| Middle Nasca, phase 5 | Transitional iconography and social reorganization; a proposed early horizon for puquio construction | Separate technological adoption from the establishment of older ritual centers |
| Late Nasca, phases 6–7 | “Proliferous” elaboration of motifs; changed settlement and political relations | Alter visual density and institutions together; do not call abstraction artistic decline |
| Terminal phases / Middle Horizon contexts | Wari-era interactions and reworked local traditions | Treat style change, migration, and conquest as distinct hypotheses |

“Monumental,” “Transitional,” and “Proliferous” are ceramic analytical categories, not known native era names (Proulx 2006).
Climatic stress belongs in a multi-cause model with local land use and political change; avoid a single drought date explaining every transformation.
Geoglyph construction, use, and later visitation are different events: associated sherds can date activity without securely dating initial clearing (Sakai et al. 2024, “Dating of Nazca Geoglyphs”).

## Language family, writing, and oral knowledge

- **Language family:** not securely identified in the reference base used here; there is no recoverable Nasca grammar or phonological inventory on which to base a historically faithful conlang.
- **Writing:** no securely deciphered Nasca-language writing corpus; elaborate iconography is communicative evidence, not an established phonetic script (Silverman and Proulx 2002; Proulx 2006).
- Recurrent painted beings may encode categories or ritual relationships; repetition alone cannot establish glyph readings, syllabic values, or a deity's spoken name.
- Ceramic phases classify objects; they do not identify language boundaries. Shared motifs can circulate across language communities.
- Later Quechua vocabulary and Spanish descriptions of waterworks cannot establish what the builders spoke centuries earlier.
- Spoken ritual, instruction, and memory are reasonable design domains, but a specific Nasca chant, initiation formula, or taboo speech register is **unknown**, not reconstructible from a painted scene.
- Fictional application: independently design the language; then couple route directions, water states, textile operations, and public ritual roles through its semantic fields. Label all generated forms as invented.

## Naming practices: distinguish recoverable labels from invention

| Name class | What can be used responsibly | What cannot be claimed |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient personal names and titles | State that the sources here do not supply a secure naming system | Invented Nasca kings, priest names, patronymics, or honorific suffixes presented as historical |
| Cahuachi and other place names | Use as archaeological/topographic identifiers | Assume today's name preserves the original pronunciation, meaning, or settlement category |
| “Anthropomorphic Mythical Being,” “Mythical Killer Whale” | Attribute to modern iconographic classification | Treat English catalogue labels as translations of attested divine names |
| “Spider,” “Monkey,” “Astronaut” geoglyph labels | Identify the modern labelled figure, with identification caveats | Infer a native constellation name, original narrative, or extraterrestrial subject |
| Hydraulic terms below | Preserve their known modern language layer | Relabel Quechua or Spanish vocabulary as the lost Nasca lexicon |

For fiction, distinguish household names, ritual offices, route/place names, and later outsiders' labels; a change of language can rename an old monument without changing its fabric.
Do not derive a fictional naming phonology from the modern spelling “Nazca”; spelling history and ancient speech are different evidence problems.

## Mythic core and pantheon limits

- There is no surviving Nasca mythological text collection equivalent to an attested pantheon genealogy; colonial testimony is far later than classic Nasca society.
- The usable core is an **iconographic repertoire**, not a dictionary of gods: anthropomorphic supernatural beings, feline and bird attributes, serpentine appendages, marine creatures, cultivated plants, and severed heads (Proulx 2006).
- The Anthropomorphic Mythical Being is a scholarly grouping of composite imagery; whether particular instances represent deities, transformed humans, masked performers, or overlapping categories remains interpretive.
- Mythical Killer Whale imagery joins marine predation and human/head imagery in ways important to iconographic analysis; a definite native divine name and complete myth are unavailable.
- Agricultural renewal and fertility are substantial interpretive themes, especially where heads and plants are juxtaposed; this does not establish that every rite was a rain ceremony (Conlee 2007; Proulx 2006).
- “Shaman” is an analyst's comparative term, not a recovered Nasca office. A mask or composite body does not independently prove trance, a particular drug, or a universal shamanic theology.
- Do not import an Inka divine roster, a fixed three-world cosmology, or later Quechua concepts as direct Nasca testimony.
- Fictional application: define relationships among cultivation, bodily transformation, predation, and public authority before assigning named gods; multiple ritual specialists may interpret the same image differently.

## Symbolic grammar across surfaces

| Motif / operation | Evidential anchor | Coupling opportunity, not a recovered translation |
|---|---|---|
| Human–animal composite bodies | Ceramic supernatural imagery | Costume can change a performer's displayed category without requiring a distinct biological species |
| Severed heads and sprouting/plant associations | Ceramic imagery and mortuary interpretations | Link bodily potency, ancestry, cultivation, and ritual danger; keep specific meanings contextual |
| Birds, marine creatures, cultivated plants | Portable art and geoglyph repertoires | A valley society's symbolic horizon can extend beyond its immediate biome |
| Repetition, rays, streamers, proliferating appendages | Diachronic ceramic styles | Let an era's aesthetic grammar affect garments and public display, not just pottery |
| Line, cleared area, bounded path, repeated route | Geoglyph construction and spatial distribution | Meaning can emerge through movement and visibility rather than a readable sentence |

Compare motif combinations, placement, substrate, audience, and phase before comparing isolated silhouettes (Proulx 2006; Sakai et al. 2024).
A painted head, an actual curated head, and a head depicted on the ground are materially different acts, even where imagery overlaps.
No universal Nasca color lexicon can be read directly from a vessel's pigments; distinguish material palette from inferred symbolism and spoken color categories.

## Nazca Lines as ritual geography: calibrated theories

**Observed:** people manipulated desert surface stones to make geometric and figurative forms; superposition, associated ceramics, and varied techniques indicate multiple histories, not one simultaneous master drawing.
Some figures were visible from slopes or walking routes; an aerial view is useful to modern documentation but is not evidence that ancient makers required aircraft.

| Explanation | Calibration | Appropriate use / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ceremonial movement and processions | Substantial archaeological support in particular settings | Ruggles and Saunders (2012) identify a labyrinthine itinerary through ground-level and relative-sequence analysis; do not extend that function to every line |
| Distinct audiences and scales of activity | Strong distributional evidence; proposed social purposes remain inferences | Sakai et al. (2024) relate small relief-type figures to informal trails and large line-type figures to a formal line/trapezoid network |
| Pilgrimage and ceremonial aggregation | Well-grounded regional interpretation, not a universal destination map | Test route entrances, valley connections, dated use, and access to Cahuachi rather than assuming all lines point to one temple |
| Water, agricultural fertility, sacred landscape | Serious contextual interpretation; exact ritual referents uncertain | Water dependence is not proof that each image depicts a rain deity or each line marks a buried channel |
| Astronomical/calendrical purposes | Possible for particular alignments; comprehensive constellation/calendar readings unestablished | Require a dated feature, contemporary horizon, explicit selection rules, and controls against chance alignments |
| A literal map of all underground water | Contested extrapolation | Spatial correlation alone does not establish original intention or predict every water source |
| Alien runways or lost aerial technology | Unsupported pseudoscience | Do not frame these as equivalent scholarly alternatives; construction is within human technical capacities |

Sakai et al. (2024) support differentiated functions, not the proposition that AI has decoded Nasca religion; discoveries and spatial associations do not supply spoken meanings.
A useful fictional ritual landscape has approach routes, pauses, restricted or shared spaces, sightlines, maintenance labor, and seasonal reuse—not merely a picture for an overhead map.
Couple bodily experience to vocabulary: entering, turning, crossing, returning, and reaching a confluence can become distinct ritual actions without making the landscape a script.
Keep engineering function and sacred meaning compatible but independently evidenced; one need not explain away the other.

## Trophy-head practices: bodies, fertility, and political claims

- **Observed:** curated human heads and decapitation imagery occur in Nasca contexts; common preparations include a frontal perforation for a suspension cord and, in some examples, fastening of the mouth with thorns (Silverman and Proulx 2002; Proulx 2006).
- “Trophy head” is an archaeological category carrying a warfare hypothesis; it should not predetermine identity, circumstances of death, or every subsequent use.
- “Trophy-head cult” is shorthand for recurring ritual practices, not evidence for a single centralized institution or a known native cult name.
- Warfare, capture, sacrifice, ancestral relations, and agricultural renewal are not mutually exclusive explanations; different stages in a head's treatment could invoke different relationships.
- Conlee's La Tiza headless burial provides a contextual mortuary case rather than an isolated collected skull; her renewal/fertility reading is an interpretation tied to that context (Conlee 2007).
- Knudson et al. (2009) investigate geographical origins with isotope data. Exact local/nonlocal sample assignments and totals are **(to-verify)** here; do not invent percentages or turn a headline into an ethnicity claim.
- Even isotopic compatibility with the local region would not establish kinship, voluntary sacrifice, or absence of warfare between nearby communities.

| Evidence question | What it can constrain | What it cannot settle alone |
|---|---|---|
| Cut marks, preparation, preservation | Treatment sequence and handling | Consent, full ritual narrative, or political identity |
| Mortuary association and deposition | Relationship to a particular cemetery, building, or offering event | The meaning of all detached heads across Nasca history |
| Isotope comparison with regional baselines | Compatible childhood environments and mobility hypotheses | A person's name, allegiance, or genetic relationship |
| Ceramic imagery | Repeated associations and displayed roles | A photographic account of every depicted event |

Fictional application: distinguish acquisition, preparation, display, custody, and final deposition; assign material responsibilities and contested interpretations rather than making violence an exotic visual shorthand.

## Puquios: water technology and vocabulary layers

**Engineering anchor:** filtration galleries intercept subsurface water and convey it toward outlets, storage, and irrigation; distinguish groundwater collection from a canal carrying already-surfaced water (Schreiber and Lancho Rojas 1995).
Open-trench and covered-gallery forms belong in the comparison; the photogenic spiral access feature is not the whole system or a compulsory feature of every puquio.

| Term / register | Practical referent | Linguistic and historical caution |
|---|---|---|
| *puquio*, from Quechua *pukyu* | Spring/water source; regional usage also names the engineered system | A later attested vocabulary layer, not proof of the builders' language |
| *galería filtrante* | Spanish technical term: filtration gallery collecting subsurface water | A modern analytical description, not an ancient Nasca construction term |
| *ojo* / *ojos* | Spanish “eye(s)”: access openings or shafts along covered galleries | Maintenance access; a surviving opening's form does not securely date the first tunnel |
| *acequia* | Spanish term for an irrigation/distribution channel | Separate collection, conveyance, and allocation vocabulary; do not treat it as a Nasca word |
| *cocha* / *qocha* | Quechua-derived term used for a pool/reservoir | Context determines the feature; not every cocha is part of a puquio |
| Intake, gallery, outlet, reservoir | English engineering categories | Use for functional analysis before inventing native lexical distinctions |

- Access permits inspection, clearing, and repair; collection and downslope conveyance do not require an assumed ancient wind-powered pump.
- **Dating contested, not undatable in principle:** settlement patterns, construction relationships, and repair sequences have different evidential force.
- Schreiber and Lancho Rojas (1995) argue from settlement evidence for possible origins in Nasca 5 and probably no later than Late Nasca; this is an inferred construction horizon, not a direct date for every extant gallery.
- Barnes and Fleming (1991) foreground documentary evidence of Spanish construction and management of filtration systems. Colonial intervention elsewhere does not by itself date every Nazca puquio.
- Conversely, an inferred pre-Hispanic origin does not make today's masonry, spiral ramps, reservoir linings, or repairs uniformly ancient. Compare feature histories, not photographs alone.
- Similarity to Old World qanats is a technical comparison, not sufficient evidence of a specific diffusion route.
- Fictional application: build a lexical chain for finding water → collecting it → inspecting the gallery → sharing flow → repairing damage. Decide who holds knowledge and performs labor before assigning priestly or royal monopoly.
- Do not stage Cahuachi's early ceremonial prominence and the proposed later puquio expansion as one proven construction campaign.

## Cahuachi: ceremonial centrality without an automatic urban capital

- Adobe platforms, plazas, and remodeled natural elevations form a major ceremonial complex; Cahuachi is central to reconstructing Early Nasca ritual organization (Silverman 1988, 1993).
- Silverman's **non-urban ceremonial/pilgrimage-center model** challenges treating monument size as proof of a densely inhabited capital city.
- Non-urban does not mean empty: attendants, specialists, temporary residents, and periodic crowds are analytically different from a permanent city's household population.
- Fine ceramics, textiles, offerings, food remains, and musical instruments provide evidence for ceremonial activity; reconstructing the exact festival script remains inferential (Silverman 1993; Silverman and Proulx 2002).
- Distinguish the center's ceremonial influence from territorial administration, direct control of all crafts, and ownership of all waterworks; none follows automatically from monumental scale.
- Geoglyph-route links offer a regional coupling mechanism, but particular entrances and destinations require archaeological argument (Sakai et al. 2024, Discussion).
- Fictional application: design a host institution, visiting groups, provisioning arrangements, temporary gathering spaces, ritual display, and return journeys. A shared center can coordinate otherwise distinct communities.

## Costume and material signature

| Material / form | Nasca reference base | Coupling rule |
|---|---|---|
| Cotton and camelid-fiber textiles | Preserved south-coast cloth and garments | Separate fiber source, spinning, weaving, decoration, and distribution; materials need not all be locally produced |
| Mantles/wrappers, tunics, dresses, head coverings | Archaeological garment categories, including ceremonial and mortuary contexts | Do not turn all excavated finery into the everyday uniform of one sex or class |
| Woven, embroidered, looped, and painted surfaces | Multiple textile techniques, with Paracas–Nasca continuities and changes | Couple technical labor vocabulary to image placement and movement of the dressed body |
| Head ornaments and sheet-gold face ornaments | Portable ceremonial display in the material repertoire | Specify attachment, visibility, and occasion; do not infer a complete priesthood from one ornament |
| Polychrome ceramics, including double-spout-and-bridge bottles | Distinctive portable visual media alongside textiles | A motif can recur across media while its audience, scale, and practical use change |
| Ceramic panpipes and drums | Musical instruments in the archaeological repertoire | Include sound and coordinated movement; a landscape rite need not be visually intelligible from above |

Use Silverman and Proulx (2002) and Proulx (2006) for material/iconographic orientation; object-specific cut, fiber, date, attachment, and wear reconstructions remain **(to-verify)** before copying a particular artifact.
Desert preservation and funerary selection overrepresent certain materials and settings; a burial assemblage is not a census of ordinary wardrobes.
Paracas and Nasca overlap historically and in museum labels: never transfer a spectacular “Paracas” textile into any Nasca phase without checking its attribution and find context.
A fictional costume system should vary by task, occasion, community, access to materials, and era—not reduce a population to masks, feathers, and severed-head motifs.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Ritual geography as a social interface:** connect movement, named places, gathering, and repeated maintenance; distinguish what walkers see from what an overhead observer sees.
- **Authority without a monolithic state:** let shared ceremonies coordinate local communities without presupposing a king, capital, or uniform language.
- **Hydraulic culture as skilled practice:** couple technical vocabulary, water allocation, repair duties, and material constraints; sacred water does not make engineering metaphorical.
- **A grammar of transformation:** connect garments, composite imagery, bodily treatment, and cultivated growth while preserving differences between observed objects and inferred beliefs.
- **Diachrony across systems:** change ceramic/garment aesthetics, route use, technical terminology, and institutional power at different rates rather than replacing everything at an era boundary.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: provide alternative mechanisms, dates/confidence ranges, and explicit invented-vs-sourced labels. Only lore accepts or records fictional canon.

## Provenance cautions and limits

- This entry is thin on native-language testimony, personal naming, divine names, and complete narratives because the evidential base is thin there; no unattested lexicon or pantheon genealogy is supplied.
- Colonial chronicles and hydraulic documents are temporally remote, mediated accounts of later landscapes and practices, not transparent windows into classic Nasca speech or theology.
- Archaeological categories can conceal regional diversity; unprovenanced museum objects and looted burials weaken chronological and social inferences. Check excavation context before building a rule.
- Accessible checks for this entry include the Proulx publisher description/date, scholarly bibliographic records and available abstracts, and Sakai et al.'s open full text; this is not a claim to have freshly re-read every cited monograph.
- Detailed isotope assignments and artifact-specific costume reconstructions remain explicitly **(to-verify)** above. New evidence may change them; do not silently promote them to established facts.
- Reject alien explanations and civilizational “mystery” rhetoric; also reject the opposite shortcut of claiming that a single modern theory has decoded every line, image, and ceremony.
