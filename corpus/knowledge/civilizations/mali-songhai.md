## Scope and evidence labels

- **Attested** means supported for the people, place, and source period specified; it does not mean unchanged since imperial Mali.
- **Reported** marks what an observer or narrator says; testimony is evidence of a representation, not automatically of its explanation.
- **Design inference** marks a transferable mechanism, not an additional historical claim; `(to-verify)` marks an unresolved detail.
- Mali the medieval polity, modern Mali the state, Mande-speaking populations, Bamana communities, and Songhay-speaking populations are not interchangeable categories.
- Mande is the wider language-family label; Manding is a branch within it. Songhay/Songhai spellings do not identify a Manding dialect.
- Verification ceiling: the accessible primary extract, encyclopedia articles, publisher descriptions, contents, and catalogs were checked; complete monographs were not re-read. The Cairo price report and findings/bibliography for [S11–S14] were checked through secondary overviews, not full original chapters/articles. Do not treat a catalog synopsis as page-level verification.
- Secondary lookup trail, not independent primary evidence: [Musa and the Cairo report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mansa_Musa); [chronicle textual criticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarikh_al-fattash); [Mande classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mande_languages); [Songhay classification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Songhay_languages); [N'Ko chronology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N%27Ko_script); [epic edition bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sundiata_Keita).

## Language family, script, and competing kinds of literacy

| System | Reference distinction | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Manding speech | Maninka, Mandinka, Bamana/Bambara, and Jula belong to the Manding grouping within Mande; they are not identical varieties. [S13] | Select a speech community before selecting name forms, tone conventions, or praise formulas. |
| Higher affiliation | Mande's inclusion in Niger-Congo is debated; Dimmendaal treats it cautiously rather than as an established branch. [S13] | Do not turn a disputed linguistic family tree into a migration or ethnic-origin fact. |
| Songhay languages | A different family/group with debated external affiliation; the conventional Nilo-Saharan placement is not settled. [S13] | Contact and political succession do not prove common linguistic descent. |
| Arabic | Scholarly, religious, and documentary language used by locally rooted African authors as well as trans-Saharan correspondents. [S3, S10] | Script expertise, spoken repertoire, ancestry, and political allegiance are separate variables. |
| Ajami | Arabic-script writing of African languages; not another name for Arabic. [S10, Hassane chapter] | Date a particular language–script pairing from manuscripts; do not assume every imperial court used every later Ajami practice. |
| N'Ko | Souleymane Kanté's twentieth-century Manding script, conventionally dated to 1949; not an imperial-Mali alphabet. [S14] | A later literacy movement can reinterpret an oral past without being proof of ancient written copies. |

- **Design inference:** distribute competence across recitation, legal reading, copying, translation, and pattern interpretation; none implies mastery of all the others.
- Retain source spellings as aliases: Sunjata/Sundiata, jeli/jali/djeli, Songhay/Songhai. These transliterations alone do not reconstruct medieval pronunciation.
- Do not impose a universal spoken-versus-written binary: recitation, memorization, commentary, and manuscript transmission can reinforce one another. [S4, S10]

## Era staging: make a transition ledger, not an aesthetic blender

| Stage | Historical anchor | What may change together | Reconstruction limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before Mali's imperial expansion | Gao and trans-Saharan commercial/Islamic networks have histories predating Musa. [S1, S3] | Trading languages, settlement authority, imported goods | Do not credit all urbanism or writing to one visiting ruler. |
| Thirteenth-century Mali formation | Sunjata is associated with imperial founding; surviving epic performances are much later witnesses. [S7] | Alliance genealogies, heroic precedents, legitimacy | A modern epic is not a stenographic record of a thirteenth-century assembly. |
| Fourteenth-century Mali | Musa's pilgrimage, 1324–25; Ibn Battuta's Mali visit, 1352–53, under Sulayman. [S1, S2] | Court gift economies, overseas reputation, ritual display | Sulayman's observed clothing is not automatically Musa's wardrobe. |
| Sonni Ali's Songhay | Reign 1464–92; expansion and conflict with scholarly urban interests. [S3] | Military authority, river control, competing memories | Chronicle judgments have political and religious standpoints. |
| Askia period | Askia Muhammad takes power in 1493; scholarly and imperial networks develop through the sixteenth century. [S3] | Titles, learned patronage, diplomacy, taxation | Islamization is not one complete, simultaneous population conversion. |
| Conquest and successor regimes | Moroccan invasion in 1591; political resistance and intellectual life continue afterward. [S3] | Patronage, coercion, displacement, archive custody | Defeat does not mean every library or institution immediately disappears. |
| Later ethnography and modern Mali | Sources on Bamana mudcloth and Kita griots document later settings. [S5, S8, S9] | Market audiences, gendered production, national symbolism | Do not label a documented twentieth-century textile motif a medieval imperial uniform. |

- **Design inference:** for every transition, track who changes patron, who changes public language, which objects retain authority, and who disputes the new account.
- Avoid the universal sequence “tribal oral age → Islamic written age → modern decline”; these are overlapping institutions, not ranked civilizational stages.

## Jeli oral archives: registers, rights, and interested memory

- **Attested:** jeli, plural jeliw, names a Mande verbal/musical specialist; “griot” is a broader outside umbrella, not a precise equivalent for every West African profession. [S4]
- Jeli work includes genealogy, praise, mediation, counsel, performance, and historical transmission; its authority involves training, social relationships, and patronage. [S4, S5]
- Women practitioners, often called jelimusow, are not an incidental chorus; specify local divisions of repertoire and performance rather than declaring history universally male-owned. [S4]
- The labels in the table are **analytical register tags**, not a claimed universal indigenous genre taxonomy.

| Register tag | What the utterance does | Language–symbol–material coupling | Failure if treated as neutral data |
|---|---|---|---|
| Genealogical recognition | Locates a person among remembered relationships and obligations | Names and ancestry authorize greeting, praise, and patron recognition. [S4] | A lineage recital becomes an unquestioned biological pedigree. |
| Public praise | Makes reputation audible and invites recognition or reward | Performance, audience response, and gifts jointly produce standing. [S2, S4] | Payment is omitted, leaving the performer outside the economy. |
| Epic narration | Reworks founding actions and exemplary conflicts | Named heroes, instruments, kinship, and political precedent interact. [S7] | Every episode becomes a dated military report. |
| Mediation and counsel | Negotiates between people whose positions differ | Indirection, precedent, praise, and public respect can make intervention possible. [S5] | An archive keeper is mistaken for an apolitical recorder. |
| Peer-facing status speech | Contests and repairs the authority of specialists themselves | Kita's 1985 griot assembly joined speeches, music, institutional office, and reconciliation. [S5] | Hereditary identity is mistaken for permanent professional agreement. |
| Admonitory court performance | Reminds a ruler that predecessors are remembered for conduct | Ibn Battuta describes costumed poets counseling Sulayman through remembered royal examples. [S2] | Praise is assumed to exclude criticism. |

- **Medieval anchor:** Dugha appears in Ibn Battuta as interpreter and praise performer, with musicians, women performers, conspicuous dress, and publicly distributed rewards. [S2, “Festival ceremonial”]
- This supports a court coupling of speech, office, costume, and wealth; it does **not** prove every later jeli lineage or restriction already existed in the same form.
- **Design inference — archive record:** attach speaker/teacher lineage, patron, audience, occasion, permitted disclosure, compensation, variant account, and the consequence of contradiction.
- Let disagreement be structured: rival performers may share a framework while contesting rank, emphasis, or the right to speak; avoid both “perfect oral recording” and “oral evidence is worthless.”

## Mansa Musa's hajj: an economic-shock case with limits

- **Reported:** al-Umari's retrospective Cairo evidence links Musa's lavish gold disbursement with a reduction in gold's value relative to silver; he was not an eyewitness traveling with the caravan. [S1]
- **Attested event / contested magnitude:** the pilgrimage and spectacular spending are well grounded; a precise, empire-wide economic-collapse narrative is not established by that report alone. Schultz's chapter explicitly reappraises the textbook anecdote. [S1, S11]
- Distinguish a gold/silver exchange-rate movement from consumer-price inflation, a loss to particular holders from national impoverishment, and a large gift from the ruler's total assets.

| Link in the case | What can be used | What cannot be silently added |
|---|---|---|
| Portable wealth → foreign market | Concentrated spending can change local access to a monetary commodity. [S1] | An exact percentage change in the whole region's money supply. |
| Gold supply → relative valuation | Al-Umari reports depreciation relative to silver. [S1] | A controlled causal estimate excluding other monetary changes. |
| Display → credit exposure | Accounts also describe return-journey borrowing and merchant claims. [S1] | A verified story that Musa intentionally reversed all damage by borrowing back his gold. |
| Pilgrimage → international standing | Religious observance and diplomatic display make a distant polity legible to outsiders. [S1] | That religious motivation was merely a disguise for economics. |
| Elite expenditure → social cost | Ask how tribute, labor, and enslaved people sustained royal mobility. [S1] | A costless pageant funded by an abstract, uniformly prosperous population. |

- **Design inference — stress test:** specify initial circulating gold, release schedule, recipient access, silver availability, credit terms, and the local goods supply before predicting prices.
- Model separate winners and losers: recipients, gold holders, silver holders, lenders, importers, and providers of scarce goods need not experience the same “shock.”
- Use the case to couple sacred travel, finance, foreign descriptions, prestige garments, and memory; do not convert a medieval ruler's resources into a confidently ranked modern dollar net worth.
- Exact gold quantities, caravan headcounts, price-series duration, and particular loan terms require passage-level checking of [S11] before numerical use `(to-verify)`.

## Timbuktu: manuscript scholarship is a social infrastructure

- **Attested:** Timbuktu's scholarship connects mosques, teachers, households, scholarly lineages, libraries, copying, and long-distance exchange. [S3, S10]
- Sankore and the Great Mosque belong to that landscape; “the university” can be a convenient shorthand, not proof of one centrally administered institution with modern departments and degrees.
- Do not collapse the fourteenth-century Musa setting into the late-sixteenth-century world of Ahmad Baba and his scholarly networks. [S3]
- Arabic grammar, legal interpretation, and religious learning are central; paper, calligraphy, African-language Ajami, and collection histories are distinct research surfaces. [S10, contents and specialist chapters]
- Scientific subject claims need a manuscript or catalog entry and date; “every Timbuktu book was medieval astronomy” and “the libraries contained only Qur'ans” are both bad shortcuts.

| Layer | Evidence to request | Generative use, explicitly an inference |
|---|---|---|
| Learning relationship | Teacher biography, study lineage, transmission authorization | Authority follows a named relationship, not simply possession of a book. |
| Physical manuscript | Colophon, script, paper, corrections, ownership notes | A copied text can carry a different date and custody history from its composition. |
| Collection | Family inventory, provenance, loans, acquisition history | Custody and access become stakes in conflict without treating knowledge as one public vault. |
| Chronicle | Author, compilation date, textual witnesses, standpoint | Several communities may remember the same ruler differently. |
| Circulation | Correspondence, copying, trade connections | A scholarly town depends on material supply and travel as well as ideas. |

- **Calibration:** al-Sa'di's seventeenth-century *Tarikh al-Sudan* retrospectively narrates earlier dynasties; read its learned urban standpoint, not a supposedly neutral imperial camera. [S3]
- **Textual warning:** Nobili & Mathee distinguish layers/works conflated under *Tarikh al-fattash*, including substantial nineteenth-century rewriting; a colonial printed edition is not one unproblematic sixteenth-century witness. [S12]
- The inference to take is institutional pluralism: spoken teaching and manuscripts cooperate, while patrons, scholars, officials, and custodians need not agree.

## Mythic core: distinguish heroic memory, sacred history, and spirit traditions

| Reference complex | Core agents and relationships | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Islamic sacred order | God, prophetic revelation, Qur'anic learning, pilgrimage, legal and devotional obligations | Not an interchangeable “pantheon”; rulers and scholars can dispute conduct within shared religious commitments. [S1, S3] |
| Sunjata epic | Sunjata, Sogolon, Balla Fasséké, and Sumanguru/Soumaoro link kinship, heroic legitimacy, counsel, and extraordinary power | Use a named performance/version, such as Djanka Tassey Condé's translated by Conrad; heroes are not automatically creator deities. [S7] |
| Bamana cosmological accounts | Faro is associated with water, speech, and ordering in accounts synthesized by Zahan | Twentieth-century ethnographic synthesis, not a demonstrated empire-wide Mali creed; a fixed pantheon chart overstates agreement. [S15] |
| Songhay river/possession narratives | Faran Maka Bote, a fisher culture hero, confronts river powers; Dongo's thunder authority becomes connected with praise, music, sacrifice, and possession | Stoller's later Niger-focused account connects myth to specialists' performance; it is not a medieval Gao ritual transcript. [S16, web profile] |

- These are adjacent reference systems, **not ingredients to paste into a single historical “Mali–Songhay religion.”** Membership, practice, and interpretation vary.
- **Design inference:** a river narrative can authorize a specialist's repertoire; an epic can authorize counsel; a pilgrimage can authorize reputation; none needs to become a uniform state theology.
- Knowledge of exact local invocations, restricted teachings, and deity genealogies is thin here; the consulted profile notes that specialist knowledge passes through long apprenticeships and named lineages [S16], and details and permissions remain `(to-verify)` rather than invented secret lore.

## Nyama, names, and restricted speech: do not manufacture a true-name rule

- **Attested, later Mande settings:** nyama is a context-dependent concept of consequential, potentially dangerous power; translating it simply as “mana,” “evil,” or generic life energy loses distinctions. [S6]
- Nyamakalaw includes specialist groups such as verbal artists and smiths; the category's meanings, status histories, and internal differences require local treatment. [S6]
- Do not present a convenient decomposition of *nyamakala* as a settled etymology or “caste” as a timeless, region-wide Indian-system duplicate. [S6]
- Naming and powerful speech intersect, but the sources checked do **not** establish one universal Mande ban on speaking personal names. Treat any such universal rule as reported but not independently confirmed `(to-verify)`.
- Positive counterweight: jeli praise makes names and lineage relationships publicly consequential; Ibn Battuta even reports an oath invoking Mansa Sulayman's name. [S2, S4]

| Naming layer | What to distinguish | Safe craft operation |
|---|---|---|
| Personal name | A person's name versus its source-language, spelling, and pronunciation | Do not infer an etymology or ritual secret from a modern Latin spelling. |
| Inherited name, often rendered *jamu* | Relationship-bearing identity and praise associations versus an exhaustive family tree | Consult a community-specific treatment; no automatic occupational destiny for everyone sharing a name. [S5, S6] |
| Title | *Mansa* denotes rulership; Musa is the personal name, not part of a generic fantasy name prefix. [S1, S2] | Keep personal identity, office, and the right to use a title as separate fields. |
| Epic/praise designation | Sunjata's names connect maternal identity and heroic description in epic interpretation. [S7] | Preserve variant forms and identify whose explanation is being used. |
| Islamic scholarly identification | Personal names, genealogy, affiliation, and honorifics may coexist in Arabic records. [S3] | Parse the particular record instead of flattening every element into a Western given-name/surname pair. |
| Avoidance or restricted naming | Kin-name avoidance, concealed names, spirit-name restrictions, or ruler-name substitution are different hypotheses | Each exact rule, community, and date remains `(to-verify)` unless a source explicitly documents it. |

- **Design inference — permission matrix:** specify speaker × named referent × audience × occasion × authorized substitute × consequence; distinguish politeness, legal insult, reputation, and believed spiritual harm.
- If a fictional taboo is chosen, label it invented, not “the Mande custom”; decide who may waive it and how archive keepers refer to absent or forbidden persons.
- Keep operational knowledge distinct: the right to praise a patron does not automatically grant a smith's ritual knowledge, a scholar's legal authority, or a hunter's specialist repertoire.

## Bogolan: coded textile, not a universal pictographic alphabet

- **Attested, later Malian/Bamana contexts:** bògòlanfini/bogolan is mud-dyed cotton; the Bamana word is conventionally analyzed as mud/earth + instrumental element + cloth. [S8]
- Narrow woven strips are sewn together; plant treatment and repeated applications of prepared mud produce the dark pattern through a material reaction, not simply loose mud rubbed on finished clothing. [S8]
- Rovine describes male weaving and female dye/pattern work in the documented production arrangement; contemporary production and market roles change. Do not turn that description into biological or timeless law. [S8]
- Patterns can evoke proverbs, events, animals, or other culturally meaningful references; their combination and explanation matter. A motif is not necessarily a letter with one universal translation. [S8, S9]
- Hunters' tunics and women's wrappers have different uses; protection, camouflage, occupation, life stage, and gendered knowledge can overlap without becoming one code. [S8]
- Brett-Smith treats women's cloth as testimony concerning marriage, childbirth, excision, and death; the interpretation is ethnographically situated, not permission to claim every maker shares a single secret dictionary. [S9]
- Accounts link some wrappers to childbirth and initiation involving female genital cutting; describe the harmful practice accurately, not as an exotic design feature or universal Malian custom. [S8, S9]

| Textile layer | Record before borrowing | Fictional design inference |
|---|---|---|
| Manufacture | Fiber, strip assembly, plant treatment, mud preparation, repeated work | Technique constrains cost, apprenticeship, labor organization, and visible seams. |
| Motif | Maker's explanation, locality, date, neighboring motifs | Meanings can depend on arrangement and audience competence rather than fixed translation. |
| Use | Hunter's garment, wrapper, fashion item, studio work, export object | The same visual family can carry protective, personal, national, or commercial meanings. |
| Restricted interpretation | Who taught it; who may explain it; what is actually published | Invention can model partial legibility without copying or pretending to reveal private teachings. |
| Recontextualization | Named designer/artist, intended audience, market history | Modern adaptation is cultural production, not simply a corrupted ancient original. |

- Rovine credits Chris Seydou's fashion adaptations and describes bogolan's movement into Malian identity and foreign markets; those uses are historically different from ritual wrappers. [S8]
- Exact motif translations and claims of medieval origin remain `(to-verify)`; the consulted article documents named motif references (proverbs, events, animals) and modern adaptation rather than a medieval textile tradition. Do not dress Musa or every Songhay official in bogolan by default.

## Costume and symbolic authority: build an evidence-tagged wardrobe

| Evidence setting | Supported signature | Coupling and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Sulayman's court, 1352–53 | Ibn Battuta describes silk audience furnishings, a gold-surmounted canopy, a red tunic, headgear, weapons, and ceremonial sound. [S2] | Rank is staged across body, furniture, pacing, and music; his textile labels are translated descriptions, not surviving patterns. |
| Court access and appointment | The account describes altered dress for approaching the ruler and a Timbuktu investiture gift of robe, turban, and trousers. [S2] | Clothing can enact subordination or delegated office; do not generalize one report to every province. |
| Friday worship in Mali | He reports clean white garments, including washed old shirts. [S2] | Ritual presentation is not reducible to wealth or a universal meaning of “white.” |
| Later Bamana settings | Mudcloth tunics and wrappers carry documented occupational and life-stage associations. [S8, S9] | Different evidence period from imperial costume. |
| Later Songhay/Niger settings | Stoller describes robes, cotton outfits, wrappers, tailoring, and Western clothing in changing rural/urban contexts. [S16, web profile] | Contemporary garments are references for change, not automatic sixteenth-century reconstructions. |

- **Design inference — wardrobe record:** wearer/office, occasion, textile supply, cut, fastening, maintenance, sound/movement, permitted observers, date, evidence type, and uncertainty.
- Treat earthen architecture, manuscript paper, imported prestige cloth, woven cotton, and metal ornament as products of different supply chains, not one undifferentiated “desert aesthetic.”

## What a worldbuilder can take

- Take **distributed authority**: a praise specialist, manuscript scholar, textile maker, officeholder, and ritual specialist can preserve different, overlapping forms of knowledge without one being the “real historian.”
- Take **consequential naming**: names locate obligations and reputations; restricted speech can be designed precisely without asserting an unsupported pan-Mande true-name taboo.
- Take **materially constrained symbolism**: a textile's process, maker, wearer, occasion, and market can matter as much as a motif's explanation; visible resemblance need not imply identical meaning.
- Take **uneven era change**: religious travel, monetary circulation, conquest, copying, and fashion can alter different systems at different rates; document the lag instead of declaring a total cultural reset.
- Hand lore-shokunin a proposal containing community/era, coupled mechanism, supporting source, alternatives, uncertainty, and expected consequences. Lore chooses and records canon; this file supplies no fictional names, events, laws, or wardrobe as established world facts.
