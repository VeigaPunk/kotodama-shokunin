## Scope and calibration

- Treat Sogdiana as a region of cities and changing political affiliations, not a single enduring nation-state with one uniform religion or wardrobe.
- Distinguish the homeland around Samarkand, Bukhara, and Panjikent from communities in the Hexi Corridor, northern China, and the Turfan oasis; diaspora evidence is not a census of the homeland (Reck 2021, §§2–5).
- **Secure:** a claim directly supported by the letters or the surveyed manuscript evidence. **Interpretive:** a scholarly model or iconographic reading. **Design inference:** an optional fictional mechanism, not a historical assertion.
- `(to-verify)` marks details not established at the necessary resolution here; it is a work queue, not permission to repeat them as facts.
- The strongest coupling is **network position → communicative register → institution → material display**, not “a trading people naturally has a syncretic personality.”
- This entry is strongest on letters and Turfan multilingualism. Its pantheon treatment is selective; individual deity images, garment cuts, and historical name etymologies require specialist checking.

## Language family, scripts, and the limits of “lingua franca”

- Sogdian is an Indo-European → Indo-Iranian → Iranian language, conventionally classed as Eastern Iranian; it is not Turkic, Mongolic, or a dialect of Chinese (LANGUAGE).
- “Middle Iranian” is a historical-linguistic classification, not a claim that every surviving text occupies one political period or one invariant spoken dialect.
- **Lingua franca:** a contact function adopted across communities. It does not imply universal literacy, equal proficiency, or exclusive use from the Mediterranean to China.
- AL demonstrates long-distance Sogdian correspondence among Sogdian networks. Those letters alone do **not** establish that all caravan partners spoke Sogdian.
- RECK §§3, 5, 20–21 supplies a complementary test: Sogdian texts and scripts also served Uyghur communities; language of a manuscript cannot by itself identify its writer’s ethnicity.

| Written repertoire | Attested uses | Coupling rule and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Indigenous Sogdian script, ultimately Aramaic-derived | Letters, documents, Buddhist, Manichaean, Christian, and possibly Zoroastrian texts | A shared graphic infrastructure crosses institutions; script alone does not identify religion. |
| Manichaean script | Manichaean texts in several languages, including Sogdian | Community-specific scribal training can coexist with a wider everyday script. |
| East Syriac script | Syriac and Christian Sogdian literature; multilingual Christian materials | Liturgical authority and vernacular instruction need not share a language. |
| Sogdian-derived Uyghur writing | Old Uyghur textual traditions; later transmission into Mongolian writing | Graphic inheritance is not linguistic descent; Sogdian did not “turn into Mongolian.” |

- Script/use distribution follows RECK §§5, 8, 10–12, 20. This is the main repertoire, not an exhaustive denial of marginal scripts: RECK §8 also notes fragmentary Brāhmī material.
- The Sogdian → Old Uyghur → Mongolian graphic transmission must be staged separately from individual word loans; exact letter-shape and orientation chronology is `(to-verify)` before designing a historical facsimile.
- **Design inference:** assign separate competencies: negotiating orally, writing a credit instruction, copying a hymn, translating doctrine, and reading a foreign script. Do not distribute all five automatically to every merchant.

## Letter case studies: institutions visible at sentence scale

- The Ancient Letters were recovered by Aurel Stein in 1907 near Dunhuang; AL’s introduction favors a date around 313–314 CE while acknowledging a history of dating controversy.
- They are early substantial Sogdian correspondence, not a complete archive of the trade route. Their non-delivery preserves a disrupted network, not representative annual business.

| Case | What the translated document supports | Coupled craft mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Letter 1: Miwnay → her mother Chatis | Miwnay lacks clothes and money, seeks assistance from relatives and a councillor, and reports a priest’s charity and an offered camel/escort. | Clothing deprivation exposes loss of social protection; kinship, transport, and ritual assistance intersect. The priest’s confession is not established by the generic title. |
| Letter 3: Miwnay → husband Nanai-dhat; marginal message from daughter Shayn | Repeated unanswered letters; blocked departure; formal obeisance followed by fierce reproach; Shayn reports that she and her mother have become servants of Chinese people because of Farnkhund’s debts. | Put dependency and coercion inside the supposedly cosmopolitan network; distinguish rhetorical “servant” from an account of actual debt-related subordination. |
| Letter 2: Nanai-vandak → Varzakk in Samarkand | News of destruction and famine in China, interrupted correspondence, deposit/interest/transfer instructions, a dependent child’s future, and division of musk proceeds. | A commercial letter can be intelligence report, financial instrument, and household succession plan simultaneously. |
| Letter 5: Fri-khwataw → chief merchant Aspandhat | Isolation in Guzang, disrupted departure, stocks including pepper and “white,” and a dispute over silver actually received and weighed. | Cargo labels, measurement, reputation, and route access generate vocabulary and conflict; trade is not only silk and luxury spectacle. |

- **Register comparison:** Letters 2 and 3 both use extravagant health wishes and homage likened to that offered to gods. Similar formulae do not imply identical power relationships or sincere affection.
- **Naming evidence:** Letter 2 supplies an addressee, father, and family designation; the envelope and verso participate in delivery and social authentication, not just decorative calligraphy.
- **Material caution:** “white” in Letter 5 is interpreted as probably white lead in AL’s introduction; retain the uncertainty. Other commodity names and units remain unresolved.
- **Chronicle caution:** Letter 2’s “Huns” belongs to an early-fourth-century Chinese crisis and a particular translated ethnonym; it cannot simply identify every later European group called Hun.
- **Exercise:** draft a fictional letter with an address, blessing formula, delayed-news interval, weighed commodity, named guarantor, and an instruction effective only if another person survives. Label all invented content as design inference.
- **Failure test:** if removing the greeting, name chain, or commodity units leaves every institutional implication unchanged, the linguistic layer is probably ornamental rather than coupled.

## Religious multilingualism: institutions, not one blended creed

- RECK §§4–5 distinguishes a homeland religion with Zoroastrian affinities, chiefly reconstructed from material evidence, from the rich Buddhist, Christian, and Manichaean manuscript remains of eastern communities.
- Manuscript abundance measures preservation, collection, and institutional copying as well as use; it cannot directly rank the historical populations of those religions.

| Community/repertoire | Language and textual channel | Mythic/institutional consequence | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local cults with Zoroastrian affinities | Ritual images, ossuaries, theophoric names; comparatively thin textual base | Civic patrons, funerary purity, and inherited Iranian sacred vocabulary need not form a centrally standardized creed. | Interpretive reconstruction; do not transplant an entire later orthodox ritual system. |
| Manichaean | Middle Persian and Parthian church languages; Sogdian and Old Uyghur community literature; multiple scripts | Mani’s teachings, cosmic captivity/liberation of Light, and the distinction between Elect and Hearers organize texts and obligations. | Secure institutional distinction; not a synonym for all Iranian dualism. |
| Church of the East, often called “Nestorian” in older scholarship | Syriac ecclesiastical language; Sogdian and Uyghur translations, rubrics, and instruction | A transregional church maintains inherited liturgical authority while teaching local-language congregations. | Prefer “Church of the East”; the older label is not a precise description of every local theology. |
| Buddhist | Much surviving Sogdian literature translated from Chinese; other transmission relationships also occur | Translation can move back westward from Chinese versions, not only eastward from India. | RECK §9 records diverse textual affiliations; “Sogdian Buddhism” is not one uniform school. |

- **Concrete multilingual case:** Parthian/Middle Persian hymns could be transcribed into Sogdian script rather than translated; script accessibility and semantic comprehension are different problems (RECK §§12, 20).
- **Concrete Christian case:** Bulayık material includes Syriac, Sogdian, Uyghur, and New Persian; Sogdian New Testament translations depend on the Syriac Peshitta, not a presumed direct Greek translation (RECK §§10–11).
- **Concrete identification trap:** a Manichaean text may name Zarathustra and reuse Zoroastrian vocabulary without becoming a Zoroastrian text (RECK §15).
- **Contested:** the religious program of Wirkak’s funerary monument has received competing Zoroastrian and Manichaean readings. RECK §14 contrasts Gulácsi and BeDuhn (2012) with de la Vaissière (2019); do not settle the disagreement by calling every image “syncretic.”
- **Design inference:** let a temple, household, merchant association, and court retain overlapping but unequal language repertoires. Contact can generate translation, rivalry, exclusion, or mutual borrowing; it does not guarantee tolerance.

## Pantheon, heroic narrative, and symbol grammar

- **Named framework—polis religion:** treat cult organization as locally embedded in cities and civic institutions (POLIS, via RECK §4). The comparison with Greek civic religion is heuristic, not a claim of Greek origin.
- Do not merge local divine patrons, the Manichaean cosmic drama, Buddhist beings, and Christian sacred history into a single undifferentiated “Sogdian pantheon.” Shared speakers do not erase incompatible doctrines.

| Element | Usable reference knowledge | Identification boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Nana/Nanai | A major name in discussions of Sogdian cult; the Nanai- element recurs in AL’s personal names. Lion-associated and astral representations are commonly discussed under Nana. | Specific image, attribute inventory, and name etymology `(to-verify)` against PANTHEON and the object publication; not every goddess with a lion is Nana. |
| Wēšparkar/Veshparkar | Specialist discussions associate a Sogdian divine figure with Śiva-like visual forms. | Exact spelling, attributes, and Iranian/Indian identification `(to-verify)` in PANTHEON; visual borrowing is not proof of wholesale theological equivalence. |
| Rustam narrative | British Library Or.8212/81 preserves a Sogdian heroic episode; RECK §15 notes that the episode itself does not occur in the Persian *Shāhnāma*. | Shared hero does not establish one fixed plot, liturgy, or textual original; do not backdate Ferdowsi’s later compilation. |
| Fire altar and funerary imagery | Supports discussion of Zoroastrian affinities when contextualized with rites, objects, and inscriptions (RECK §§4, 14). | The word “fire” alone does not diagnose a religion; ambiguous fragments are discussed in RECK §§16–19. |
| Pearl-bordered medallions/roundels | Repeated textile and garment decoration in DRESS’s pictorial corpus. | A motif can circulate independently of wearer, loom, language, and faith; it is not a national flag or a unique doctrinal badge. |

- **Design inference:** create a symbol ledger with separate fields for depicted form, local name, claimed sacred identity, workshop origin, wearer, setting, and rival interpretation.
- Reuse one motif across settings only with a mechanism: trade, gift exchange, imitation, translated narrative, or court patronage. Mere visual resemblance is not a transmission history.
- Give storytellers permission to vary a hero’s episode while priests dispute the story’s meaning; narrative circulation and doctrinal agreement are separate axes.

## Era staging: do not flatten the fourth to thirteenth centuries

| Historical window | Evidence anchor and change | What must not be imported automatically |
|---|---|---|
| Early fourth century, eastern diaspora | AL’s Dunhuang/Gansu correspondents, family dependencies, and disrupted credit networks | Seventh-century court wardrobes; later Uyghur manuscript institutions; a seamless stable trade highway. |
| Fifth–sixth centuries, homeland dress corpus | DRESS proposes an earlier “Hephthalite” phase within its reconstruction | A political label as a precise ethnic identity, universal cut, or instantaneous fashion boundary. |
| Seventh–early eighth centuries, homeland | DRESS’s later “Turkic” phase; Panjikent/Afrasiab images underpin much visual reconstruction | Every painted person as a Sogdian merchant; every garment change as direct conquest causation. |
| Eighth–eleventh centuries, Turfan | RECK’s major surviving religious manuscript setting; Sogdian/Uyghur institutional entanglement | The homeland and diaspora changing religion or language in perfect synchrony. |
| Islamization and Persianization of the homeland | A transition involving language replacement, migration, and continuing eastern textual traditions (RECK §§3–4) | Conquest as an overnight erasure of names, households, techniques, or memory. |
| Later Mongolian textual inheritance | Words and writing transmitted through Turkic/Uyghur intermediaries | Direct contact with living Sogdian-speaking merchants for every later Mongolian item. |

- These windows overlap; they are an evidence-indexing model, not a universal ladder of civilization.
- DRESS’s two-period scheme is one author’s historically situated reconstruction. Use local image dates and functions before assigning an individual garment to an era.
- **Design inference:** stage political sovereignty, spoken-language change, liturgical copying, naming, and wardrobe change on separate timelines; let their lag generate credible historical texture.

## Costume and material signatures: cosmopolitanism with an evidence brake

- DRESS’s main evidence is painting and terracotta, with scarcer actual clothing remains, including Mount Mug textiles. Written costume descriptions are limited and externally mediated.
- Especially important: DRESS’s section on images of “Others” says Sogdian/Tokharistian **merchant costume is poorly known**. A rich sartorial repertoire is not a securely reconstructed caravan uniform.

| Component | Attested/reconstructed visual repertoire in DRESS | Responsible fictional use |
|---|---|---|
| Outer garment | Open-front coats/kaftans; one- and two-lapel variants; belted silhouettes | Separate riding/work needs, audience dress, and age/rank coding; do not turn one lapel into an infallible ethnicity test. |
| Trousers and footwear | Trousers, differentiated colors/trim, and high boots in the pictorial corpus | Couple movement and repair to occupational vocabulary; a practical travel ensemble remains design inference unless tied to an object. |
| Headwear and hair | Conical and rounded caps, other headwear, and changing braid styles | Specify date, depicted person, and setting; hairstyles can express court affiliation without proving ancestry. |
| Belts and ornament | Plate-decorated belts and elaborate elite trimming | Distinguish rank display from a functioning pack/weapon suspension system; exact fittings require object-level checking. |
| Fabric pattern and lining | Patterned silks, pearl medallions, contrasting linings and borders | Track cloth production, finished-garment construction, ownership, and repair separately; an imported textile can become a locally cut coat. |
| Women’s dress | DRESS distinguishes coats, dresses, and performers’ ensembles rather than one female silhouette | Do not extend dancers’ depictions to every woman, or let male elite imagery stand for all genders and classes. |

- “Kaftan/caftan,” “coat,” and “roundel” here are analytical labels, not established historical Sogdian garment words; any proposed local lexical equivalent is `(to-verify)`.
- **Cosmopolitanism mechanism:** imported cloth + local tailoring + a patron’s preferred belt + a travel repair can preserve different histories in one outfit. This is a design model, not a recovered individual costume.
- **Status counterexample:** Miwnay’s lack of clothing in Letter 1 belongs in the same reference system as elite patterned robes; network membership does not ensure prosperity or protection.
- Avoid “silk = everyone’s daily clothing,” “caravan = camel rider only,” and “Chinese-produced image = exact ethnographic portrait.” DRESS itself disputes several figure identifications.
- A proposed garment vocabulary should encode parts, fastening, lining, fabric, origin label, repair, and register; do not invent a single exotic word that conceals all those distinctions.

## Sogdian loan layers in Turkic and Mongolian

- **Transmission model:** ultimate source → Sogdian mediation → Turkic/Uyghur adoption or derivation → Mongolian adoption. Any arrow may require independent evidence; ultimate origin and immediate donor are different claims.
- LOANS demonstrates mediated cultural vocabulary using sound adaptation, not simply similar meaning. Its older “Proto-Altaic” discussion is not a requirement to accept a genetic Altaic family.
- The layers below are semantic/institutional groupings, **not** independently established dates of borrowing. “Turkic” is a family label; specify Old Turkic/Old Uyghur when the edition permits.

| Layer | Checked example/path in LOANS | What a worldbuilder can model |
|---|---|---|
| Scribal technique | Mongolian **üsüg** “letter, writing,” via a Turkic term for written character/letter/syllable, discussed among Sogdian-origin cultural words (p. 40) | A writing technology exports terms while recipient languages retain their own grammar. |
| Ritual commodity | Mongolian **küji** “incense,” through Turkic, likewise classed as Sogdian-origin vocabulary (p. 40) | A traded substance can carry ritual terminology without converting every buyer. |
| Ascetic practice | Mongolian **bačaγ/mačaγ** “fast,” via a Turkic noun related to a Sogdian-derived verb (p. 43) | Recipient-language derivation can intervene between two borrowing events; do not copy a root unchanged at every stage. |
| Buddhist monastic office | Sanskrit **śrāmaṇerī**, through Sogdian and Turkic, behind Mongolian “nun” terminology (p. 43) | “Sogdian loan” can name the intermediary rather than the ultimate inventor; distinguish novice-specific source meaning from later glosses. |
| Buddhist lay affiliation | Sanskrit **upāsikā**, through Sogdian and Turkic, behind Mongolian “lay woman” terminology (p. 43) | Institutions distribute different terms for monastic and household participation. |

- Exact Sogdian donor spellings, the complete recipient variant sets, and first attestations are `(to-verify)` in clean editions before using these as a phonological dataset; no regular sound law is inferred from damaged OCR.
- Court titles and commercial words need separate dossiers. Do not automatically label **bäg/beg** or **qatun/khātūn** securely Sogdian on resemblance or a popular etymology alone: donor direction, form, and chronology here are `(to-verify)`.
- Keep script inheritance out of the lexical tally: a Mongolian word written in a Sogdian-descended alphabet is not thereby a Sogdian loanword.
- **Design inference:** build strata by institution, then assign donor date, contact route, phonological accommodation, recipient morphology, semantic shift, and later prestige. Distinguish direct loans from loans-of-loans.
- **Diagnostic test:** if two proposed loans require incompatible sound substitutions, test different borrowing periods, dialects, or intermediaries before inventing an exception.

## Naming practices and addressable identities

- Use AL’s actual name chains before extrapolating: **Varzakk**, son of **Nanai-thvar**, of the **Kanakk** family; sender **Nanai-vandak** addresses the recipient as his lord and describes himself as his servant.
- Other directly attested names include **Miwnay**, her mother **Chatis**, her daughter **Shayn**, husband **Nanai-dhat**, and chief merchant **Aspandhat**. These are a small documentary sample, not frequency statistics.
- Theophoric naming couples household identity and sacred vocabulary, but a name is not a confession of current belief. RECK §§4, 15 also shows inherited Iranian names crossing religious narrative settings.
- Do not segment every personal name by visual resemblance. The exact devotional value of **-vandak** and the etymology of each compound should be checked against NAMES before historical reuse `(to-verify)`.
- Separate personal name, patronymic, family designation, occupational title, and letter-writing honorific. None should automatically become a modern-style hereditary surname.
- Chinese-form names and place-associated surnames need a separate bilingual dossier; mappings such as Kang/An are `(to-verify)` here rather than a ready-made “nine clans” naming generator.
- **Design inference:** allow different institutions to address one person differently: household genealogy, vernacular nickname, trade title, sacred name, and an exonym in another writing system.
- Transfer a naming **rule** to a conlang only after specifying its morphology and sound history; do not assemble purported Sogdian names from unsourced syllables.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **A network without a universal empire:** shared correspondence practices can connect politically divided cities and diaspora settlements.
- **Asymmetric multilingualism:** common commercial speech can coexist with multiple sacred languages, community scripts, and uneven literacy.
- **Institutional rather than decorative loans:** vocabulary travels with books, offices, ritual commodities, and habits; a second recipient may inherit an already transformed word.
- **A wardrobe as a contact archive:** track textile source, cut, patronage, age/status, and repair independently; label the inferred travel ensemble as invented.
- **Religious difference with shared resources:** a name, image, or Iranian divine term may cross communities without making their doctrines interchangeable.
- **Chronological lag:** let language, cult, clothing, and political allegiance change at different speeds rather than replacing an entire culture at each era boundary.
- **Letters as the integration test:** material scarcity, deference, names, measures, household duties, and unreliable routes should all affect the same communication.
- **Handoff to lore-shokunin:** provide candidate mechanisms, source IDs, date/place constraints, unresolved alternatives, and explicit design inferences; lore decides which, if any, become canon.
