## 1. Scope and calibration key

- **Three senses of "Kongo".** Tag every claim as one of: the kingdom ruled from Mbanza Kongo (São Salvador); Kongo-speaking peoples and the wider region; or a museum's attribution label. The Met's power-figure records name "Kongo peoples" and a nineteenth-century Chiloango River workshop, not the kingdom (S395, title block and Artist field); a Cleveland cap is credited to a Kongo-style maker, possibly Yombe (S397, Culture field).
- **Period.** UNESCO's inscription dates the kingdom's active life from the 14th to the 19th century (S411, Brief synthesis, sentence 1). Most power-figure evidence is nineteenth-century and must not be staged as early court practice (§9).
- **Witness filter.** §3 names each witness's vantage. Kongo kings also wrote: Afonso I's letters are a royal voice (S359, doc. 142, p. 469).
- **Out of scope.** Present-day Kimbanguist and other religious movements, and minkisi procedures, are not described: no community statement was opened for them this round.

## 4. Language: a cluster, a court variety, and the first books

- **A cluster, not one language.** De Schryver and colleagues call the Kikongo Language Cluster a disparate continuum of closely related Bantu languages (S378, p. 87) and split it into North, East, South and West subgroups that already existed about 1,500 years ago, so the kingdom's founding does not explain them (S378, p. 146). Glottolog files the cluster as a family-level node with Kisikongo as a dialect inside it (S379, JSON fields level and newick). A world that says only "Kikongo" has not chosen a variety.
- **Guthrie codes are not peoples.** The authors call Guthrie's H16 codes geo-location shorthands with no classificatory value (S378, p. 106); one H16a label covers Kisikongo, Mboma, Songo and Solongo (S378, p. 103).
- **The kingdom's one linguistic trace** is a convergence zone, Central Kikongo, where three subgroups met around the unnavigable Congo (S378, p. 147).
- **The first book.** The 1624 *Doutrina Christãa* names its target only as the "lingoa do Reyno de Congo", translated by order of Mateus Cardoso and dedicated to King Pedro Afonso II (S374, title page). Bostoen and de Schryver count it among only three surviving seventeenth-century Kikongo works, the earliest book-length sources in a Bantu language (S375, p. 60), place its variety most likely at the capital (S375, p. 76), and conclude it is not ancestral to the whole cluster (S375, p. 100).
- **Outsiders misname varieties.** Bentley held that Brusciotto's 1659 grammar recorded the speech of Soyo, not of the court (S361, p. xii), while Torrend ranked the old grammar's dialect above Bentley's San Salvador one (S363, p. 35, note 156); the chapter on the catechism cautions that the spelling variation behind the Soyo reading is not systematic (S375, p. 77). Brusciotto's own text was not opened this round; the entry gives no rule from it.
- **Noun classes for offices.** Bentley traces every Kongo noun to one of eleven archaic classes; his first class holds names, titles and classes of persons, with *a* or *ba* plurals (S361, p. 541). He glosses *mfumu* as free man, nobleman, gentleman and *mfumu ankento* as queen, lady (S361, pp. 348-349): in this compound, rank is marked by adding a noun. He also calls the Kongo of San Salvador, the old capital, the best medium of speech as far as Stanley Pool (S361, Preface p. xi), a nineteenth-century prestige judgment, and notes that *untotela* was used for majesty (S362, Preface p. vii).

## 5. Titles, names and succession

- **A stack of royal titles.** *Ngangula a Kongo*, "blacksmith of Kongo", co-existed with *ntinu* and *ntotila* (S390, p. 55). Higher political orders were called *wene* and their holders *mwene*, a title whose field included dispensing justice (S410, p. 9).
- **Mani plus place.** Lopes explains that *Mani* means lord, joined to a place, so Mani Bamba is lord of Bamba (S404, Book I, ch. VIII, pp. 42-43). The royal style of 1512 opens "Rei de Manicongo": a style a herald proclaims, not a personal name (S371, p. 6).
- **Two names per person.** A 1624 list names each king twice, Nginga ancu who became D. João and Nbenba a Nginga who became D. Afonso (S371, p. 175); a source calls a king Pedro II Afonso, keeping both systems in one style (S390, p. 70, note 17). The founding tradition names Nsaku Lau and Nimi a Nzima, and Lukeni lua Nimi, also called Ntinu Wene, is the figure traditionally seen as the founder (S390, p. 73).
- **Succession was chosen, and contested.** The same 1624 list says the great men used their right to elect a king to choose a bastard son over the legitimate heir (S371, p. 175); Lopes tells of three princes claiming the throne at once (S404, Book II, ch. IV, pp. 92-94). The Mwene Vunda played an important role in selecting and crowning kings (S390, p. 69). Mbata's part in electing Kongo's kings diminished over time and the tradition was reworked to fit, so date any electoral rule (S410, p. 3).
- **Offices were revocable.** Kings appointed and dismissed officials down to the sub-province at will, and noble income rested on revocable revenue shares, *rendas* (S410, p. 8). Lopes says no one held estates; all land belonged to the Crown (S404, Book II, ch. VII, p. 110). After the 1512 Regimento provincial chiefs carried the Portuguese titles count, marquis and duke (S360, section "Conversão e catolização").

## 6. Capital, provinces and settlement

- **The mbanza is an office's seat.** Excavators summarise Kongo capitals as open, scattered garden-cities with a ceremonial centre (S389, p. 467); *mbanza* names where a constituency's headman is settled, and capitals moved after succession disputes (S389, p. 474). A provincial seat can be archaeologically almost invisible (S388, postprint p. 2).
- **The royal capital.** ICOMOS describes a royal residence inside a fence with dignitaries' houses and one public square, Mbazi, for justice, ceremony and troop review (S412, p. 35); UNESCO has the Portuguese adding stone buildings to a large town already built in local materials (S411, Brief synthesis, sentence 3), and credits ritual powers to the brotherhood of the Leopard Ngo (S411, Criterion (iii), sentence 2). The name São Salvador was given to mbanza Kongo after the kings' conversion (S360, section "Congo descoberto").
- **Provinces.** Lopes lists six: Bamba, Sogno, Sundi, Pango, Batta and Pemba (S404, Book I, ch. VIII, pp. 42-43); each had a governor (S412, p. 35). Ngongo Mbata supplied raffia cloth and linked the Atlantic harbours with the Kwango (S389, pp. 468, 496).
- **The capital and the slave trade.** ICOMOS writes that it is not even known whether enslaved people were brought to Mbanza Kongo (S412, p. 36).

## 7. Kinship: kanda, mvila and the mother's brother

- **Do not project late clans onto the founding.** Thornton argues kanda clans emerged in the collapse of central authority in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries (S410, p. 8).
- **Two words, two levels.** A Luanda ethnography makes the kanda the exogamous matrilineal group from a common ancestress, and *mvila* the wider category without exogamy (S377, p. 19); the eldest nephew inherits from the maternal uncle, while a father gives his son land and insignia (S377, p. 22). Its scope is twentieth- and twenty-first-century Luanda, and it warns that the "matrilineal" label imposed rigidity (S377, p. 21).
- **Kanda as category.** MacGaffey notes *kanda* means group or category (S376, p. 9) and describes Kongo society as patrilateral links between matrilineal nodes (S376, p. 10); a title could be acquired for tribute and equipped with insignia (S376, p. 12).

## 8. Kongo Christianity and the Antonian movement

- **A voluntary convert.** The ruler Nzinga a Nkuwu took the name João I at his 1491 conversion while keeping traditional practices (S412, p. 35). Thornton argues Christianity in Kongo took a distinctly African form, accepted as the country's religion, with royal control of church organization and finance (S383, Extract, paras. 1–2). The Met's essay says Kongo kings did not feel they had renounced one religion for the other (S394, essay, second paragraph). The 1624 list also says the baptized king later turned back to the old cult (S371, p. 175): conversion could reverse.
- **Vocabulary is contested.** Bentley calls the older missionaries' use of *nkisi* for "holy" a very objectionable use (S362, Preface p. vii); his 1887 dictionary glosses *nkixi* as charm, enchantment, fetish and medicine (S361, p. 383), so the word's English glosses are themselves missionary choices.
- **Church offices.** *Mestres* were interpreters and leaders of the Church, marked by a white shoulder cloth (S384, p. 12). A Kongo chief's staff topped by Saint Anthony of Padua, held by the Met, served as an insignia of office (S399, Overview label, object 318318).
- **The Antonian movement (from 1704).** Dona Beatriz Kimpa Vita taught that Christ was born in São Salvador, which she called Bethlehem (S380, relatione para. 91); her Salve Antoniana names Saint Anthony "the restorer of the kingdom of Kongo" (S380, relatione para. 84). Followers wore bark crowns, *musanda* in Gallo and *ensanda* in Da Lucca (S380, relatione para. 93; S382, p. 226), so fix no single Kikongo form. The sentence was pronounced by the *basciamocano*, glossed as the judge who pronounces sentences (S382, p. 230). Gallo dates the sentence to Sunday 1 July 1706 (S380, relatione para. 136); Batsîkama gives 2 July at Kimbangu (S392, p. 32), and Farias places the execution in Mbanza Kongo (S381, p. 210): date and place stay contested (to-verify). Farias follows the devotion into Brazil (S381, p. 210).

## 9. Minkisi and the cosmogram dispute

- **A container word.** Mack writes that all such objects are called nkisi or minkisi and are thought of as containers, neutralised without their medicines (S403, p. 56), and calls the "fetish" view an entirely spurious external one (S403, p. 54). The Met says the term covers cloth packets and filled vessels as well as carved wood (S396, essay, first paragraph).
- **Two offices make one object.** The Met calls power figures collaborative creations of sculptors and ritual specialists (S400, object label, first paragraph); Cleveland says sculptors carved and banganga placed the substances (S402, object record description). The Met describes Mangaaka as the preeminent force of jurisprudence, its metals recording vows sealed and treaties signed (S395, essay, first and second paragraphs). The Pitt Rivers glosses *nkondi* as "hunter at night" and calls "nail fetish" an inadequate gloss (S401, section What is Mavungu?).
- **Dated and political.** The Met presents fifteen Mangaaka figures from the Chiloango River region as a defensive measure against Western incursion (S393, exhibition text, fourth paragraph); Cleveland records disempowering as anti-colonial resistance and says it does not know why its figure was stripped (S402, object record description). These are nineteenth-century objects of Kongo peoples, not kingdom-era court objects.
- **The cosmogram is contested.** Gundaker presents the dikenga as a relational system, not a unitary symbol (S369, p. 176), with the yowa as its simpler cross-mark form (S369, p. 178). Steen finds "no direct, unambiguous evidence" for the cosmogram reading of North American cross marks (S370, p. 169). Cleveland's record calls the dikenga a "likely pre-Christian symbol" (S398, Description); the Met asserts the four-moments reading without argument (S394, essay, third paragraph).
- **One-way pointer to the diaspora.** In Cuba the minkisi became the centre of palero practice while every rite tied to the royal function disappeared (S385, p. 76), and *nganga* moved from naming a healer to naming the spirit vessel (S385, p. 61, note 5). Colonial ethnonyms there followed trading posts more than peoples (S385, p. 59).

## 10. Dress, regalia, cloth and money

- **Rank is worn.** Fromont writes that mpu caps served as emblems of status (S384, p. 22); claws, crosses and other additions turned the mpu into an emblem of Christian nobility (S384, p. 25); the Order of Christ's hollow-branched cross linked its noble wearer to the order (S384, p. 24). A Christian noble wore an mpu, an nkutu net over the shoulders, a staff and a red coat with the order's insignia (S384, p. 12). Cleveland gives *mpu*, plural *zimpu*, and *mpu ngola* for the long high-status cap, a late-1800s cap by a maker possibly Yombe, its type known since at least 1607 in the former Loango Kingdom and the adjacent Kongo Kingdom (S397, Description). Order of Christ membership was restricted to the king and his closest relatives (S389, p. 494).
- **Regalia can be lost.** After Mbwila (1665) the European-style crown was gone and Pedro IV was crowned in 1696 with a hat carrying the royal arms (S384, p. 23). Crucifixes served as regalia beside large iron swords (S384, p. 19).
- **Cloth licences.** Lopes reports that only the king and those he pleased could wear the finest palm cloth (S404, Book I, ch. VI, pp. 30-31), and that nobles began to dress in Portuguese fashion after conversion (S404, Book II, ch. VII, pp. 108-109). Afonso acknowledged Portuguese garments item by item in 1526 (S359, doc. 139, pp. 455–457). Kongo sovereigns sent raffia cloths and ivories to European courts as diplomatic gifts (S393, exhibition text, second paragraph). Such cloths entered European court and cabinet collections, the first recorded in Prague in 1607, and a 1709 description placed them in the Kingdom of Angola (S409, body paragraphs 6–7): a buyer's record can move the border.
- **Cloth as money, hedged.** Plain raffia could serve as currency and pay tribute, but a student paper hedges that it was not necessarily currency in everyday transactions (S407, p. 84); the British Museum's "possibly used as currency" record is Lele and Babunda work, not Kongo (S408, Description field).
- **Nzimbu.** The island of Luanda furnished the shells used as money by the king of Kongo; women dived for them, and gold and silver bought nothing there (S404, Book I, ch. IV, pp. 18-19). The shell is *Olivancillaria nana* from the Luanda region (S405, p. 23); a curated source says shells were spent by quantities, not one by one (S406, card translation). No opened page gives an exchange rate. The word moved: in Bentley's San Salvador of the 1890s *nzimbu* named hexagonal blue pipe beads, not shells (S362, p. 739), and dowry was glossed as nzimbu or other named goods (S362, p. 744).
- **Envoys in two wardrobes.** SMK's record dresses the Soyo envoy Dom Miguel de Castro in European clothing (S386, KMS7 gallery labels 1-2); Lowe identifies the raffia tunic on Antonio Manuel ne Vunda's bust as a garment reserved for chiefs (S387, p. 104).

## 13. Calibration bank

Codes as in `../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`.

- **B, not abolition — "Afonso I tried to abolish the slave trade."** His July 1526 letter states the wish that his kingdoms have no slave trade (S359, doc. 142, p. 469), but his October 1526 letter sends each sale to three court officials who judge captive or free and lets cleared captives be shipped (S359, doc. 147, p. 489): the letters regulate the trade in practice; they do not end it.
- **C — "Kongo Christianity was a thin veneer."** UNESCO says the kingdom adopted Christianity while retaining Kongo custom (S411, Criterion (iii), sentence 3); Thornton argues an African church accepted as the country's religion (S383, Extract, para. 1).
- **C for one fixed meaning, T for "ancient" — "the cosmogram is an ancient universal Kongo symbol."** Gundaker supports a relational reading and disputes a unitary symbol, Steen tests only North American cross marks, and the only dating opened is a museum's hedged "likely pre-Christian" (§9); attribute every reading.
- **B — "Kikongo is one language."** A cluster with subgroups (S378, pp. 87, 146); name the variety.
- **R — "nkisi = fetish."** Mack and the Pitt Rivers reject the label (S403, p. 54; S401, section What is Mavungu?).
- **H — "empire."** UNESCO's 14th–19th-century frame and Lopes's six provinces support a kingdom with provinces and claimed titles over regions governed by others (S404, Book I, ch. VIII, pp. 42-43); "empire" is a label choice.
- **R — "no writing, so no history."** The 1624 book and the 1526 royal letters exist (S374, title page; S359, doc. 142, p. 469); the two Antonian eyewitness relations opened here are by Capuchins hostile to her (§3).
- **T — exact day and place of Kimpa Vita's execution** (§8).
