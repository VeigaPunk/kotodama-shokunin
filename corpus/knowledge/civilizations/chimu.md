## Scope and evidence calibration
- Geographic anchor: the Moche Valley and Chan Chan on Peru's north coast; distinguish the capital, conquered valleys, and the broader north-coast cultural region.
- **Chimú** is an archaeological/cultural label; **Chimor** names the polity in much scholarship. Neither licenses a single ethnicity, language, costume, or timeless religion throughout its territory.
- Status key: **attested** = documented object, excavation, or linguistic record; **reported** = historical testimony through a named source; **inferred** = interpretation; **contested** = competing readings; **(to-verify)** = not established by the sources checked here.
- A seventeenth-century observer describing a surviving coastal practice is not an eyewitness to the independent fifteenth-century court. A museum attribution is not equivalent to a securely excavated context.

## Language family, documentation, and writing
| Layer | Conservative description | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Muchik / Mochica | Extinct language isolate in the conservative classification; unusually substantial colonial and later documentation for this coast | Not a dialect of Quechua; no demonstrated family relationship follows from proximity to Quingnam |
| Quingnam | Poorly attested extinct language associated in colonial accounts with Chimú rulers and expansion | Its grammar cannot be reconstructed by relabeling Muchik; genealogy remains unestablished |
| “Yunga” | Colonial cover designation connected with warm lowlands/coastal peoples; also specifically used for Mochica | Not a demonstrated language family or an invariant ethnic self-name |
| “Pescadora” | Colonial label with disputed scope; sometimes equated with Quingnam, but Eloranta adduces its use for Mochica too | Not proof that every fishing community had one secret occupational language |
| Quechua | A family of languages/varieties involved in coastal–highland contact before, during, and after Inca expansion | Not every Quechua-looking place-name or loan dates to the conquest |
| Spanish | Language of colonial administration and the eventual dominant language in former Muchik-speaking communities | Its spread was neither instantaneous nor evidence that coastal people ceased to exist |

- Basis: Eloranta-Barrera Virhuez (2020), §§1.1, 2.1–2.3, 9.2–9.3; Herrera Wassilowsky (2016). Older titles such as Middendorf's *Das Muchik oder die Chimu-Sprache* (1892) can perpetuate the misleading equation “Muchik = the Chimú state language.”
- Muchik is predominantly synthetic and suffixing; possession and numeral classification are productive comparison topics. Treat these as linguistic structures, not explanations of an allegedly collective “Chimú mentality” (Eloranta 2020, chapters 4, 6, 8).
- **Writing distinction:** the checked sources do not establish a deciphered preconquest Chimú phonographic corpus. Architectural reliefs, repeated textile motifs, and brick marks are not thereby sentences in Quingnam.
- Oré's (1607) religious texts and Carrera's (1644) *Arte de la lengua yunga* preserve Muchik through colonial Roman-letter transcription; missionary purposes and Latin grammatical categories filter the evidence (Eloranta 2020, §§2.4.1, 3.1).
- At Magdalena de Cao Viejo, an early-seventeenth-century paper carries Spanish number equivalents in another language. Quilter et al. (2010) identify a decimal system and probable Quechuan loans, but explicitly cannot securely name the language Quingnam or Pescadora. It is colonial writing, not discovery of an ancient Chimú script.
- Design rule: maintain separate columns for **language**, **writing medium**, **observer's spelling**, and **modern editorial reconstruction**. A legible alphabet does not make the language identification secure.

## Muchik language loss: a transmission case, not a conquest date
| Evidence horizon | What is actually available | Coupling lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Seventeenth century | Missionary texts and Carrera's grammar document a functioning language under Spanish rule | Conquest and continued vernacular speech can coexist |
| Late nineteenth century | Middendorf reports Spanish-speaking children and contraction of Muchik, with Eten an important surviving center | Track household transmission separately from the survival of prestigious names |
| Early twentieth century | Brüning's 1905–1924 materials and Lehmann's 1929 consultations preserve further language evidence | A collector's visit dates a record, not the death of an entire language |
| Mid-twentieth-century collections | Word lists, remembered expressions, and copied manuscripts; provenance varies | Distinguish fluent speech, remembered vocabulary, and copying of older records |
| Modern reclamation | “New Mochica” initiatives use colonial/republican materials, teaching, and cultural activity | Revival is present-day community work, not an untouched fifteenth-century survival |

- Evidence chain: Eloranta (2020), §2.4.2, especially pp. 55–66, and §2.4.3. Her discussion itself uses differing extinction horizons, and later material adds twentieth-century recordings: no single exact last-speaker year can be cited from the sources checked here (cf. Herrera Calderón et al. 2019).
- **Attested pressure:** the reported switch to Spanish among children. **Not established here:** the relative causal weights of schooling, particular decrees, disease, marriage patterns, or migration in each town.
- Do not write “the Inca extinguished Muchik in 1470”: the 1644 grammar and later consultations contradict that compression by centuries.
- Fictional application: stage domain loss—court, trade, household, prayer, craft vocabulary—at different rates; then distinguish speakers, semi-speakers, rememberers, archivists, and revival learners. This is a design model, not a complete recovered Muchik social history.

## Chan Chan: a spatial lexicon with labeled provenance
| Working term | Architectural referent | Craft use / calibration |
|---|---|---|
| **ciudadela / conjunto amurallado** | Large bounded monumental compound containing several activity areas | Spanish archaeological labels; “citadel” does not prove a military fortress |
| **palacio** | Royal-residential interpretation of a compound with ceremonial, storage, and funerary functions | Do not reduce the institution to a private bedroom block |
| **audiencia** | Conventional label for characteristic U-shaped structures | Neither the Spanish colonial court nor a recovered Quingnam word; exact administrative/ritual function is interpreted |
| **patio / plaza** | Open gathering space within the complex | Correa-Trigoso & Soberón prefer *patio* because bounded access differs from a universally accessible civic plaza |
| **corredor / acceso / antesala** | Passage, entrance, and waiting/preparation space | Lexicalize permission and controlled movement, not simply directions |
| **banqueta / rampa** | Raised bench/platform and inclined connector | Changes of level can stage visibility, waiting, movement, and ceremonial rank |
| **depósito** | Storage room or facility | Name goods, containers, responsibility, and access separately; storage alone does not prove a particular taxation system |
| **plataforma funeraria** | Funerary platform within the monumental complex | Couples dead rulers, household continuity, and architectural memory |
| **huachaque** | Named water-related feature associated with compound layouts in the excavation discussion | In the Chan Chan literature the term denotes sunken cultivation dug to the water table (Moseley & Deeds 1982); any particular excavated feature still needs context-by-context checking — “well,” “reservoir,” and “sunken garden” are not interchangeable |
| **adobe / tapia / enlucido** | Mud brick, rammed-earth construction, and surface plaster | Different materials/processes; do not call all earthen walls adobe brick |

- Terminological basis: Correa-Trigoso & Soberón Winchonlóng (2020), especially “Chan Chan y el conjunto amurallado Chol An,” “Discusión,” and “Ambientes”; U-shaped-room/access method: Moore (1992). These are research terms, not an invented indigenous dictionary.
- **Access-graph method:** make rooms nodes and doorways edges; compare the route from entrance to ceremony, storage, water, and burial. Moore's graph-theory approach supplies a testable alternative to calling a building “mysterious” or “labyrinthine.”
- Chol An's excavated sequence includes blocked and newly opened entrances, altered decoration, and later domestic structures. A single final plan cannot represent every occupation phase (Correa-Trigoso & Soberón 2020).
- Fictional coupling: assign different address formulas, garment visibility, and speaking rights at successive thresholds. Mark the resulting protocol as your invention; archaeology does not supply the actual court speeches.

## Mythic core: partial coastal testimony, not a recovered sacred book
| Element | Evidence status | Usable mechanism without overclaiming |
|---|---|---|
| **Si**, Moon | Reported in north-coast religious traditions summarized by Moore (2002); colonial mediation | Celestial authority linked with weather/crops; reported precedence over the Sun is not a universal Andean hierarchy |
| **Ni**, Sea | Reported in the same regional synthesis; offerings and marine sustenance emphasized | A giver of food can also be dangerous; do not force one modern “god of X” job description |
| Moon Animal | Modern iconographic designation for a crested/fanged creature associated with celestial motifs across north-coast imagery | Visual continuity need not preserve a stable divine name or identical doctrine |
| Tacaynamo / Taycanamo | Legendary dynastic founder named in the historical synthesis | Founder genealogy can authorize hierarchy; legend does not establish an archaeological accession year |
| Stars / cosmic eggs and differentiated origins | Regional traditions used to explain noble/commoner distinctions | Treat as competing/local origin accounts, not one harmonized empire-wide creation narrative |
| Ancestors and burial ritual | Funerary architecture and grave goods, interpreted alongside testimony | Couple continuing obligations to dead rulers with living households; specific liturgies remain unknown |

- Source limit: Moore's (2002) religious synthesis offers an outline, not a complete pantheon. Divine gender, kinship, priestly ranks, ritual calendar, and original-language prayers are not established by the sources checked here; treat the particulars as open research needs.
- Keep Lambayeque/Sicán's Ñaimlap/Naylamp traditions distinct from Tacaynamo's Chimor lineage. Neighboring sea-origin stories are not licenses to merge founders or transfer every named attendant to Chan Chan.
- Reject the shortcut “Moon = silver = female = fertility everywhere.” It combines objects, modern associations, and theology without demonstrating the local links.
- For fiction, use a **charter-myth comparison**: whose ancestry authorizes access to which water, workshop, or courtyard? This is an analytical question, not proof that every excavated room enacted one particular myth.

## Spondylus: object biography, exchange network, and symbol
| Link in the chain | Grounded reference | What remains inference |
|---|---|---|
| Acquisition | Exotic shell brought from warmer waters to the north, especially the Ecuadorian coast (Scher 2017) | Specific suppliers, route monopolies, and expedition organization need contextual evidence |
| Labor and representation | Pillsbury's “Los Buceadores” relief is interpreted as Spondylus-diving imagery | The relief is not a written commercial contract or literal map |
| Political circulation | Pillsbury (1996) argues that ruler-sponsored long-distance exchange helped build expanding state power | An influential model, not proof that the state controlled every shell transaction |
| Transformation | Shell becomes beads, ornaments, and other worked materials (Scher 2017; Moore 2002) | Whole shell, fragment, bead, and powder need not have identical recipients or value |
| Deposition | Ritual shell offerings/caches occur in the regional synthesis (Moore 2002) | A find's precise deity, spoken dedication, and intention are often unknown |
| Water association | Marine/rainfall meanings are proposed in that synthesis | Not a universally decoded emblem; climatic symbolism is not a weather-forecasting technology |
| Display on bodies and buildings | Shell adornment and shell-related architectural imagery join elite appearance to distant procurement | No automatic equivalence between a motif and a legally exclusive rank badge |

- **Object-biography method:** distinguish extraction → transport → working → circulation → wearing/offering → deposition. At each step ask who names it, owns it, transforms it, and can authorize its movement.
- **Political-economy model:** ritual indispensability can make distant procurement politically valuable. Keep the direction of explanation explicit: exchange may support sovereignty; a shell image alone cannot demonstrate an imperial founding cause.
- Do not call all red beads Spondylus, treat all Spondylus as one species, or claim its entire natural range is confined to Ecuador. Species identification, source attribution, and archaeological context require separate checks.
- Avoid “Spondylus was Chimú coinage” without transaction evidence. Prestige valuables, offerings, labor obligations, and standardized currency are different institutions.
- Fictional coupling: give divers, bead makers, court custodians, and ritual recipients different vocabularies for the same material; let an import interruption affect both regalia and ceremonial credibility. These roles/phrases are design options, not recovered historical dialogue.

## Era staging: parallel clocks rather than one costume per dynasty
| Horizon | Historical reference | Staging instruction |
|---|---|---|
| Before Chimor's ascendancy | Earlier Moche and overlapping Sicán/Lambayeque traditions | Do not turn every predecessor into one ethnic/language lineage or a simple Moche → Sicán → Chimú ladder |
| Approximately 900/950 onward | Conventional beginning range for Chimú development and Chan Chan's growth (Moore 2002; Scher 2017; Correa-Trigoso & Soberón 2020) | Separate city growth, state formation, and dynastic origin legend; their dates are not interchangeable |
| Later expansion | Regional incorporation; Sicán conquest conventionally placed around 1375 in Scher (2017) | Conquest can add languages and workshops without homogenizing dress or worship |
| Late fifteenth century, conventionally c. 1470 | Inca conquest of Chimor; precise campaign chronology is not established by an excavated year label | Reweight political authority and prestige, not every household language overnight |
| Chimú–Inca material phase | Chol An domestic reoccupation and hybrid-period ceramics; a tunic dated 1450–1550 | “Chimú–Inca” is an archaeological/art-historical label, not a proven mixed language |
| Sixteenth–seventeenth centuries | Spanish rule and the principal surviving early linguistic/chronicle descriptions | Treat documented words and customs as conquest-filtered witnesses, not pristine earlier snapshots |
| Nineteenth–twenty-first centuries | Language contraction, collection, and reclamation | Modern identity belongs to living communities; it is not merely a leftover costume phase |

- Keep **political time**, **building-phase time**, **garment-production time**, and **language-transmission time** on separate tracks; link them only where evidence warrants.
- **Split-inheritance / successive-palace model:** Scher (2017) presents the view that a ruler's compound became his mortuary establishment and a successor built anew, potentially encouraging expansion. Use as an interpretive model, not a securely recovered succession statute or a one-palace/one-named-king timetable.
- Chol An's reuse shows why ritual compounds can outlive their earlier access regime. A ruined court can become housing without proving that all former inhabitants or cultural knowledge vanished.

## Costume and material signature
| Feature | Checked anchor | Coupled design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Cotton plus dyed camelid yarn | Alegria (2024), *Tunic with Felines*: local cotton and highland camelid yarn | A coastal garment may embody highland procurement; textile composition is not a language-family marker |
| Multiple techniques in one garment | Same record: plain weave, brocade, embroidery, tapestry borders, fringe | Distinguish fiber, weave, embellishment, component, and finished garment in the lexicon |
| Separately produced components | Borders attached after weaving; differences between feline figures interpreted as multiple hands | Workshop collaboration is plausible; exact apprentice/senior allocation is curatorial inference |
| Bird and marine imagery | Scher (2017) illustrates patterned tunics and other media | Compare motif layout and medium; do not assign every bird one divine name |
| Featherwork and basketry-supported headgear | Scher (2017), with named museum examples | Exotic procurement, bodily silhouette, and rank display can reinforce each other |
| Gold/silver ornaments and vessels | Earspools, beakers, repoussé imagery in Scher (2017) | Moving, reflective regalia changes the visibility of the wearer; no unsupported written sumptuary code |
| Modeled blackware | Reducing firing and molded/three-dimensional forms in Scher (2017) | Process and surface differ from a deity/color code; “black” does not establish a death cult |

- The Met tunic is dated **1450–1550**, crossing conventional Inca conquest and reaching into Spanish rule. Its feline subject/color choices are interpreted as possibly late or postconquest; the date range does not prove an exact event of manufacture (Alegria 2024).
- Calibrate claims separately: fiber/technique = object observation; multiple makers = interpretation; protective feline meaning = possibility; a specific office entitled to wear it = not evidenced; treat as unknown.
- A high-status surviving tunic does not document everyday dress for all genders/classes. Looting, preservation, collecting, and elite tomb selection shape the sample (Scher 2017; Alegria 2024).
- Fictional use: stage change through materials, workshop organization, border designs, and political insignia independently; avoid copying an entire museum ensemble and calling it the uniform of a monolingual nation.

## Chimú–Inca language layer: concrete contact without a false transcript
- Eloranta (2020), pp. 355–356, reproduces Quechua-loan analyses from Cerrón-Palomino, with references to Carrera (1644). These are **Muchik attestations**, not a recovered Quingnam court wordlist.

| Recorded Muchik form | Proposed Quechua source / gloss | What the example couples |
|---|---|---|
| `<catu>` | `*qatu`, “market / plaza / square”; Carrera p. 127 | Space and exchange terminology; adaptation of a uvular stop to Muchik's velar inventory |
| `<llaftus>` | `*l̃awtu`, imperial insignia/diadem; Carrera p. 6 | Political regalia and speech: Eloranta discusses /w/ → /f/ adaptation |
| `<yanà>` | `*yana`, “servant”; Carrera p. 144 | Status vocabulary; its presence does not recover the entire labor institution |
| `<mæcha>` | `*muča`, “kiss / adore”; Carrera p. 164 | Ritual/social action vocabulary; a loan need not preserve an unchanged theology |

- Angle brackets preserve source spelling; asterisks reproduce reconstructed source forms in the cited analysis. Do not silently convert these into a confident modern pronunciation or invented name generator.
- **Chronological restraint:** attestation in 1644 establishes that a form was recorded by then; it does not date the borrowing. Earlier contact, Inca-period use, and colonial Quechua mediation must be distinguished rather than collapsed into “introduced in 1470.”
- Herrera Wassilowsky (2016) explicitly notes pre-Inca Quechua in Ancash and proposes multilingual contact scenarios. Applying this to a particular Chan Chan household remains an extrapolation beyond the cited evidence.
- **Model for fiction:** local coastal speech + an expansion-associated language + a highland prestige layer + later colonial language. Assign actual domains and interlocutors; do not assume everyone speaks every layer or that grammar switches when a banner changes.
- Conquest can displace craftspeople and redirect elite production (Scher 2017); language-use details for particular relocated workshops are not attested. A change in patronage is not itself proof of a specific conversational language.

## Naming practices: names as layered witnesses
| Name category | Evidence / caution | Application rule |
|---|---|---|
| Dynastic person | Tacaynamo/Taycanamo variants in Moore's synthesis; Minchançaman named in Correa-Trigoso & Soberón (2020) | Preserve witness spellings; no unsupported native morpheme breakdown or complete succession list |
| Political title | “Chimú Cápac” is described as an Inca designation in the excavation article | An outsider's title is not automatically the ruler's own Quingnam self-designation |
| Compound labels | Chol An / former Rivero; Nik An / former Tschudi in archaeological usage | Track present nomenclature and earlier scholarly names separately; do not assume either is an attested fifteenth-century name |
| River, spring, canal, mountain | Nepeña toponyms discussed by Herrera Wassilowsky (2016) | Names can preserve water claims and sacred geography across language shifts |
| Segmented place-name | Herrera discusses Muchik `-an` “house” and `-pon/-pong` “rock / rocky mountain,” with uncertain Quingnam sharing/compounding | Treat identifications as local hypotheses, not a universal decoder for all north-coast names |
| Repeated name | Repetition appears in discussed coastal toponyms | Repetition alone cannot determine language, date, or translation; the “sun-sun” reading traces to Middendorf's proposal and remains unconfirmed — later etymological work argues no such reading is verifiable from the surviving record (Urban 2017) |

- No comprehensive Chimú personal-name grammar is recovered by this entry. Avoid fabricated gender endings, inherited surnames, royal name taboos, or rank prefixes presented as attested.
- Build fictional names from an independently specified conlang, then record whether each is an endonym, conqueror's form, translation, colonial spelling, or later commemorative rename.
- A water-place name may survive when local households change language; a dynasty's founder story may claim an older monument. Neither automatically dates the name to the construction.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **Threshold society:** couple architectural access, address forms, costume visibility, and authorized speech; test the resulting protocol against a room/door graph.
- **Sovereignty through distant materials:** make one imported ritual material affect exchange vocabulary, workshop prestige, clothing, and claims to rain/water authority; retain multiple explanations and local variations.
- **Asynchronous conquest:** change patrons, political titles, and certain garments before assuming household-language replacement; give old ritual and water names different survival rates.
- **Regalia loans with phonological adaptation:** borrow an insignia term through the receiving language's own sound system, as the Muchik examples illustrate, instead of sprinkling untouched prestige words at random.
- **Language-loss/reclamation arc:** distinguish loss of everyday transmission from survival in lists, surnames, craft terms, ritual memory, and new teaching; portray revival communities as agents, not fossils.
- Deliver to lore-shokunin only a **proposal packet**: source/analogy → invented mechanism → affected language/myth/symbol/era/costume → alternatives → uncertainty. Acceptance into the world bible is lore's decision, not this file's.

## Provenance cautions and stop rules
- The weakest area here is detailed preconquest theology and spoken court practice: no indigenous sacred book or continuous Quingnam conversational corpus was checked. Do not fill those gaps with plausible-sounding chants or pantheon genealogies.
- Colonial missionaries translated and classified through evangelizing aims; Inca-centered accounts also filter conquered peoples. “Babel,” “idolatry,” and alleged linguistic corruption are observers' judgments, not neutral native categories.
- Muchik ≠ Quingnam ≠ Quechua; Moche archaeology ≠ automatic Mochica speech; coastal religion ≠ uniform Chimú state doctrine. These distinctions are load-bearing.
- Access, textiles, and shell exchange have different evidence bases. Do not let an excavated wall make an inferred god-name certain, or a readable colonial word date a preconquest borrowing.
- Full tables/figures of Moore (1992), the complete Pillsbury (1996) argument, original chronicle folios, exact huachaque functions, and precise last-speaker/campaign dates remain follow-up checks where needed; uncertainty is not permission to invent.
- Reject a draft that turns shell into unsupported money, makes all Moon symbolism female/silver, claims a deciphered Chimú script, merges Ñaimlap and Tacaynamo, or treats conquest as the instantaneous end of culture. Keep method and reference knowledge; write no canon.
