## Scope and calibration
- Unit of comparison: a named locality/community + period + social position + speech/performance setting, not a single timeless “Berber civilization.”
- Amazigh, plural Imazighen, is an important self-designation; historical sources also use Berber. Its local meanings and distribution are not uniform (Chaker 1986).
- Tamazight can denote the wider linguistic domain, a particular Moroccan variety, or a modern standardization project; declare which use is intended.
- Tuareg examples belong to Saharan/Sahelian societies; Kabyle examples to northern Algeria; Gourara and Ouargla examples to distinct oasis settings. Do not silently combine them.
- **Attested** below means documented in the cited setting, not universal or unchanged; **interpretation** means an author's explanation; **design** means a proposed fictional mechanism.
- Strongest coverage here: linguistic description, modern ethnography, and documented performance. Knowledge of ancient ritual sequences and continuous local chronologies is thin; those gaps are not invitations to invent.
- Matriliny, women's authority, men's face-veiling, and women's head-covering are separate variables. Their coexistence does not establish a single causal “inversion.”

## Language family: related systems, not an Arabic dialect
- Amazigh/Berber constitutes a branch of Afroasiatic; Arabic belongs to the Semitic branch. Contact and borrowing do not make Berber a descendant of Arabic (Chaker 1989, §12).
- Older French scholarship often says “chamito-sémitique”; never turn that terminology into a “Hamitic race,” phenotype, or civilizational ranking.
- Language-versus-dialect labels also carry identity and standardization arguments. A useful craft comparison does not require pretending that every variety is mutually intelligible.

| Comparator | Useful linguistic anchor | Coupling boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Moroccan varieties | Tachelhit/Tashlhiyt, Central Atlas Tamazight, Tarifit; distinguish the regional names | A poetic register or standardized orthography need not be ordinary village speech (Chaker 1986; Unicode 2025) |
| Kabylie | Kabyle speech and the locally named poetic genres below | Do not rename all Amazigh verse asefru (Bounfour 2015) |
| Tuareg domains | Tamahaq, Tamajaq, Tamasheq reflect regional forms; Chaker records regular correspondences involving h/j/š and northern z | A sound correspondence is not permission to exchange entire vocabularies or social institutions (Chaker 1986) |
| Oasis cases | Gourara's Zenati-speaking performance communities; Ouargli agriculture in the calendar source | “Oasis” is an ecological comparison, not a language name (UNESCO 2008; E.B. et al. 1992) |

- **Root-and-pattern framework:** roots, nominal patterns, and obligatory morphology interact; do not approximate the family with Arabic-sounding syllables (Chaker 2012, §§2–10).
- Chaker's example *amɣar*, “old man/chief,” connects an age/size root MƔR to a nominal pattern; that age → authority relation is useful for designing related vocabulary, not a universal political office.
- Gender/number/state are distinct grammatical dimensions: compare *a-mɣar*, *ta-mɣar-t*, *i-mɣar-n*, *ti-mɣar-in* in Chaker's analysis. They are cited examples, not ready-made fictional names.
- Feminine morphology can distinguish sex, size, or individuation; it is not evidence that every t…t word names a goddess or an object owned by women.
- Free/annexed state is conditioned by syntactic relations; initial a-/ta- are not a productive modern definite article (Chaker 2012, §§11–15).
- Historical derivations can become opaque: a writer's root analysis need not be available to speakers. Preserve lexical irregularity and semantic drift instead of making every word a transparent cultural manifesto.
- **Design:** choose one phonological comparator and one contact history before deriving clothing, kinship, cultivation, and ritual vocabulary; exact local sound inventory remains `(to-verify)` until a suitable grammar is selected.

## Writing systems: stage the practice, not just the glyph
| Writing regime | Attested surface/function | Era-sensitive constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient Libyco-Berber | Regional inscriptions, including funerary and bilingual evidence | Sign readings, language interpretation, and dating have unequal certainty; a known alphabet is not a recovered mythology |
| Traditional Tuareg Tifinagh | Predominantly consonantal notation; rock messages, correspondence, accounting on perishable supports | Regional variants and variable historical direction; do not reduce it to decorative graffiti |
| Arabic-script Berber | Religious/legal and literary uses; notably the Tashlhiyt manuscript tradition | Arabic script does not imply that the text's language is Arabic |
| Modern Latin notation | Scholarship, publication, and community writing, especially documented in Kabyle settings | Not simply “foreign letters without local agency”; usage has its own history |
| Neo-Tifinagh, including IRCAM forms | Modern expanded alphabetic repertoires, vowels, educational and digital implementation | Modern left-to-right conventions cannot be projected unchanged onto ancient or traditional Tuareg texts |

- Historical basis: Camps et al. (1996), especially §§19–23, 35–51, 52–71; modern encoding: Unicode (2025), §19.3.
- Keep **script**, **orthography**, **language**, and **literacy domain** separate: a caravan account, a school primer, and a devotional manuscript can belong to different writing networks.
- Claudot-Hawad's section attributes the invention of Tifinagh in Tuareg oral mythology to Aniguran/Amamellen. That is a culture-hero attribution, not an archaeological author identification.
- Her account also stresses ephemeral accounting and correspondence: lack of surviving archives does not prove lack of practical literacy.
- Alphabet origin and the etymology of *tifinagh* remain matters for specialist comparison; a “Phoenician” gloss alone does not establish a direct descent model (Camps et al. 1996, §23).
- **Design:** give each writing network a substrate, reading direction, training route, audience, and authority claim; a script reform can alter who may name, teach, or certify without changing the spoken family.

## Mythic and religious core: stratigraphy, not a composite pantheon
| Stratum / named figure | What is supported | What cannot safely be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Ammon at Siwa; Zeus-Ammon in Mediterranean circulation | An ancient oracular cult and transformations of its imagery; Camps discusses uneven regional evidence | A supreme god shared by every ancient North African population, or an identity with every ram image (Camps 1986) |
| Anẓar and the “bride of rain” | Rain terminology, personification, songs, and locally recorded rain-solicitation rites | A fully reconstructed ancient deity with a standardized genealogy or priesthood (Camps and Chaker 1989) |
| Aniguran/Amamellen | Tuareg culture-hero association with script invention | A pan-Amazigh creator deity, or proof that myth dates an inscription (Camps et al. 1996) |
| Saints, sacred descent, and pilgrimage | Sbuâ commemorates the Prophet's birth through local saintly mausoleums, group journeys, standards, and collective rites | Saints as merely renamed pagan gods; the ritual is embedded in Islam (UNESCO 2015) |
| Vulnerability and nonhuman danger | Southern Moroccan naming accounts include protection from jnûn and an ogress, tamẓa | One universally ordered “Berber demonology” (Gélard 2012) |

- Chaker explicitly says that the old pantheon is too poorly known to affirm Anẓar's status as an ancient deity; preserve that reservation alongside the vivid ritual description.
- The Anẓar entry itself contrasts Kabyle ritual archaism with ordinary rain vocabulary elsewhere and notes the term's absence in Tuareg: even a widespread word does not guarantee a shared cult.
- Camps's “Ammon” rejects automatic equations between prehistoric ram engravings and Amon-Ra. Iconographic resemblance is not sufficient chronological or theological evidence.
- Ancient inscriptions, external Greek/Latin accounts, medieval reporting, and recent oral collections are different evidence strata; a recent narrative is not automatically an ancient transcript.
- **Design:** couple drought risk → an authorized petition genre → a specially dressed ritual object → collective food collection; invent the fictional theology separately and label that invention.

## Symbol and material: distinguish mechanism from interpretation
| Surface | Coupling that can be used | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Kel Ewey men's tagelmust / women's afar | Headdress, reserve, respect, gendered personhood, and life transitions | Relational practice, not a static universal icon (Rasmussen 1991) |
| Great Kabylie silver, filigree enamel, coral | Regional workshop technique, visible finish, mounting, repair, and material substitution | Not a uniform costume for every Amazigh population (Camps-Fabrer 1991) |
| Hand-shaped pendants, afus / khamsa terminology | A protective interpretation documented alongside locally differentiated jewelry | Not an exclusively Amazigh symbol or a key to every geometric motif (Camps-Fabrer 1991, §§28–29) |
| Tifinagh letters | Actual reading, personal messages, learned notation, and possible identity display | First establish that a mark is writing; modern symbolic reuse requires its own date and context |
| Saint's standard at Sbuâ | Cloth, bearer, lineage narrative, pilgrimage route, and temporary gathering | Not generic heraldry or evidence of a universal color code (UNESCO 2015) |
| Djanet Sebeïba warrior dress and swords | Men's dance, women's song, craftsmen's maintenance, and staged rivalry | Ceremonial transformation of conflict, not everyday battle uniform (UNESCO 2014) |

- **Material signature is regional and technological:** the jewelry source differentiates Great Kabylie enamel/coral, other regional cast or chained forms, and Tuareg pendants; it also records coin silver and replacement materials.
- Do not turn descriptions of protective use into claims of uninterrupted prehistoric continuity; the older jewelry scholarship sometimes makes precisely that leap.
- “Diamond = womb,” “triangle = goddess,” and a universal tattoo-to-Tifinagh dictionary are not established by these sources; any proposed local equivalence is `(to-verify)`.
- **Design:** specify who manufactures, buys, inherits, repairs, lends, and recognizes an ornament before assigning it mythic meaning; trade and repair can explain visual change without a religious revolution.

## Tuareg matriliny and the apparent dress inversion
- Attested comparator: Rasmussen's Kel Ewey setting distinguishes men's face-veils, *tagelmust*, from women's headscarves, *afar*. “Women unveiled” must not be misread as “women never cover the head.”
- Her opening argument relates both forms to reserve/respect and transitions in social personhood and property responsibilities. It does not establish that matriliny mechanically causes men's veiling.
- Abrous (2015, §6) distinguishes matrilineal groups, including Kel Ayr, from patrilineal groups, including Ifoghas and suzerain Iwellemmeden; even the latter retain important maternal reference points.
- **Matrilineal ≠ matriarchal:** tracing membership or certain rights through women is not equivalent to women holding every political office, controlling every asset, or abolishing gender hierarchy.

| Variable to keep independent | Ask of the historical comparator | Fictional coupling to test, not a historical assertion |
|---|---|---|
| Descent | Which rights follow which maternal/paternal relationship? | Lineage terms may govern legitimacy without determining everyday address |
| Residence | Where does a couple live, and when can that change? | The household that hosts a speaker can alter respectful avoidance |
| Property | Which objects are individually held, transferred, or kept undivided? | A ceremonial garment can change custodian without changing lineage membership |
| Political authority | Who qualifies, who exercises office, and under what historical regime? | An office can pass through a maternal line but be performed by a man |
| Veiling and speech | Which encounter, life stage, audience, or status makes reserve necessary? | Concealment and verbal indirectness can covary without making all men silent |

- Include rank and coercion in the research brief; noble ideals cannot stand in for every Tuareg person's experience. Kinship scholarship also documents colonial recoding of social categories (Claudot-Hawad 2015).
- “Blue people” imagery is not a complete costume specification: exact indigo cloth provenance, price, construction, and age/status distribution in the selected setting are `(to-verify)`.
- **Design:** write a dress rule as condition → action → audience interpretation → exception; do not write “this culture reverses all male and female roles.”

## Oasis calendars: ecological work and several kinds of time
- E.B., Gast and Delheure (1992) document concurrent calendars; their regional examples are not interchangeable. Seasonal names, exact boundaries, and local observation can disagree.

| Timekeeping layer | Source-attested role | Coupling opportunity |
|---|---|---|
| Julian agricultural reckoning | Seasonal cultivation and locally adapted month names | Older month vocabulary can coexist with a different civil calendar |
| Islamic lunar reckoning | Religious duties and festivals | A religious gathering moves relative to a solar harvest season |
| Gregorian civil reckoning | Administration and cross-calendar reference in the source's modern setting | Officials and farmers can use different dates for the same obligation |
| Stellar markers | Agricultural sayings and observations; the Ahaggar example includes Sirius | A remembered sky cue can authorize a seasonal warning without a written almanac |
| Phenological/work periods | Ouargla's cereal, channel, and date sequence; subdivisions track date development | An activity name can also be a time expression |

- Do not universalize the source's modern thirteen-day Julian/Gregorian offset across all historical centuries; do not turn its Ahaggar 1965 example into a perpetual conversion table.
- Its Ouargla subsection (§§30–40) describes a “dead” winter season and a “living” season subdivided by work; use this local contrast rather than assuming four equally meaningful temperate seasons.

| Ouargla worked sequence | Practical anchor in Delheure's account | Design inference |
|---|---|---|
| Winter / tažrest | Reduced vegetation and agricultural activity | Clothing storage and repair may use the lower-work season; this is a design choice |
| Period of barley/wheat | Springtime cereals; date development is already used to subdivide time | A cereal festival need not imply a cereal-only economy |
| Period of irrigation channels | Channel restoration and summer vegetables | Maintenance vocabulary can become the ordinary answer to “when?” |
| Period of dates | Ripening and concentrated harvest, extending toward mid-November in the account | A short harvest interval can support annual provisioning and assembly |

- The listed subperiods called “weeks” include named decades and a final much longer interval; do not translate their terminology into uniform seven-day units.
- *Asili*, pollination, is glossed through climbing the palms; this directly couples calendar terminology to skilled bodily work, rather than to an abstract numbered month.
- Ennayer/Yennayer belongs to the January/new-year complex; the source records Kabyle “door of the year” language and auspicious meals. Local modern celebration dates and year-numbering histories are `(to-verify)`, not evidence of one unchanged ancient founding epoch.
- Djanet's Sebeïba falls in the first Islamic lunar month, while Sbuâ commemorates the Prophet's birth; neither is established as a fixed solar harvest festival by its UNESCO record.
- **Design:** choose the food bottleneck, water-work schedule, sky observation, and ritual authority first; then test what happens when a lunar festival and harvest labor coincide.

## Oral poetry: asefru is one register, not the whole repertoire
| Genre / locality | Form and performance reported | Authority or register boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Asəfru / asefru, Kabyle; plural isəfra/isefra | Usually three tercets, 7/5/7 syllables per tercet, rhyme aab/aab/aab; shorter variants also reported | Prestigious form for material treated as serious, not a pan-Amazigh meter (Bounfour 2015, §§20–21) |
| Izli, Kabyle | Variable short forms, including autonomous distichs; women's lyric associated with weaving and wedding settings in the survey | Restricted public expression and gendered performance; do not generalize these restrictions to every region (§§22–24) |
| Adəkkəṛ, Kabyle | Religious/mystical choral recitation; God, afterlife, and saints; funerary vigils an important setting | Ritual authorization and participation differ from personal lyric (§§32–33) |
| Ahellil, Gourara | Poetry, polyphonic chant, instrumental music, movement, and chorus; religious and secular collective occasions | Oasis community cohesion and graded performance expertise, not a synonym for asefru (UNESCO 2008) |
| Imzad-associated song, Tuareg | Women play the single-string bowed instrument; the heritage record describes men composing/reciting/singing heroic songs | Gendered collaboration, oral transmission, and therapeutic functions; not a universal rule for every Tuareg genre (UNESCO 2013) |

- Ahellil's documented night sequence moves from **lemserreh**, open participation, through **aougrout**, experienced performers, to **tra**, the most accomplished at daybreak; time, competence, and access are coupled.
- Bounfour explicitly warns that missing documentation for an area does not mean an absence of poetry. His genre distinctions are a comparison framework, not a complete census.
- A translated text does not retain the original syllable count automatically; count the source-language performance before asserting a meter. An English 7/5/7 imitation is an adaptation.
- **Register matrix:** record speaker, addressee, permitted topic, setting, bodily action, musical accompaniment, audience response, and sanction; “formal versus informal” is too coarse.
- **Design:** a speaker may use household language in an elder's verse, while an apparently “high” script belongs to administration; prestige, literacy, and archaism need not align.
- Test the mismatch: a work chant at a funeral, a personal lyric in a public petition, or an expert-only dawn performance entered by a novice should have distinct consequences, chosen for the fictional system.

## Era model: parallel histories, not an evolutionary ladder
| Evidence horizon | Systems to stage together | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Antiquity, varying by region | Libyco-Berber inscriptions, Punic/Greek/Latin contact, localized cults and oracle imagery | Do not supply modern Tuareg costume or Neo-Tifinagh to every ancient North African scene |
| Medieval Islamic formations | Arabic-script Berber and Arabic learned networks, political change, locally mediated Islam | Surviving fragments and reports are not a complete archive of everyday speech (Camps et al. 1996) |
| Early modern / precolonial local cases | Tashlhiyt religious manuscripts, oral repertoires, community-specific material production | A manuscript tradition need not replace performance or match another region's institutions |
| Colonial nineteenth/twentieth centuries | New transcription, collecting, photography, administrative naming and kinship classification | Records are evidence of encounters and power as well as of local practice (Claudot-Hawad 2015) |
| Modern revival and standardization | New publications, orthographic choices, heritage framing, schooling/digital fonts, changing naming politics | Date each institutional claim; “traditional” can also be a present-day selection and argument |

- Stage language contact, religious authority, materials, and scripts separately; a change of ruling dynasty does not synchronize every subsystem.
- **Design:** make an era card with date range, locality, evidence type, competing languages/scripts, textile supply, cultivation cycle, and authorized performance settings before designing the silhouette.

## Naming practices: an act, a relationship, and a history
- Chaker's *Amazigh / Imazighen / Tamazight* examples show how person, group, and language labels can be morphologically related without being identical categories (1986).
- *Kel-Tamajeq*, “people of the Tamajeq language,” illustrates a collective identification; it is not a personal-name prefix to scatter indiscriminately over characters (Chaker 1986).
- Gélard's southern Moroccan account differentiates religious reference, family destiny, and identity assertion in personal-name choices; it is not a universal naming code.
- In her Aït Atta discussion, seventh-day nomination is a social transition: women often select the name, paternal affiliation is publicly established, and infant clothing/body presentation changes with recognition.
- *Idir*, glossed “[may] he live,” and feminine *Tidir* are documented protective choices after child deaths; those contextual uses do not make every bearer a record of a deceased sibling (Gélard 2012, §6).
- Islamic personal names and Amazigh speech are compatible. A name's Arabic linguistic origin alone does not establish the bearer's ethnicity or everyday language.
- Revived or newly coined names can provoke intergenerational disagreement; do not move a modern identity-driven name into an earlier era without dated evidence.
- **Design:** separate provisional infant reference, publicly conferred personal name, parentage, collective affiliation, epithet, and administrative spelling; decide who may utter or change each, rather than making a decorative name list.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **Take coupling mechanisms, not a costume collage:** relational reserve + headdress + speech etiquette; crop stage + time expression + labor duty; script training + authority; poetic form + audience access.
- Select one locality/period as the principal comparator; name any borrowed secondary comparator and explain the fictional institution that makes the combination possible.
- Build a small design sheet: linguistic rule → kinship/address consequence → ritual occasion → object/garment → seasonal timing → performance register. Mark every fictional link as proposed, not ethnographic fact.
- Exercise it with a naming ceremony, harvest dispute, and public recitation: ask who can speak, in which form, with which visible signs, on whose calendar, and who may object.
- If the fictional society changes descent rules, do not automatically reverse veiling, inheritance, residence, or political offices; change each deliberately and test the resulting interfaces.
- Hand lore-shokunin alternatives with source, locality, date/evidence horizon, confidence, translation choices, and unresolved dependencies. Lore owns acceptance and world-bible canon; this entry establishes none.

## Provenance cautions
- The signed EB articles contain disagreements, dated theories, and broad generalizations; their authorship does not make every interpretation equally secure. Keep observed practice separate from inferred “survivals.”
- Camps's Anẓar discussion uses “sympathetic magic” and degeneration language; Chaker's accompanying caution is load-bearing. Neither licenses a universal fertility cult or an evolutionary hierarchy.
- Colonial racial typologies, the “Hamitic” racial hypothesis, a timeless “Berber matriarchy,” and script/ornament shape alone as proof of prehistoric religion are not valid design authorities.
- Claudot-Hawad critiques colonial “tribalization” and the overuse of kinship as explanation; Abrous documents regional descent differences. Do not flatten their analyses into one kinship slogan.
- UNESCO records are community/state safeguarding presentations, not demonstrations of unchanged antiquity, exhaustive local variation, or settled historical origins.
- The 1996 writing survey is a dated snapshot; its statements about undecided official alphabet policy are not current law. Unicode's historical narrative and demographic figures are not substitutes for specialist history or current language counts.
- Thin areas in this entry: pre-Islamic liturgical words, ancient costume reconstruction, Siwi and eastern Libyan ritual histories, and group-specific textile supply chains. Claims requiring those details remain `(to-verify)`; do not fill them from generalized “desert people” imagery.
