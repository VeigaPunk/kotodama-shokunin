## 1. Scope and calibration

- “Japan” here primarily means court, warrior, temple, and urban networks in central Japan, with Kyūshū included for Christian contact; it does not erase regional languages, Ainu communities, or the distinct Ryūkyū kingdom.
- Use a locality, decade, institution, social position, and evidence genre before choosing vocabulary or clothing. A Kyoto merchant, a provincial mounted retainer, and a Kyūshū catechist do not share one complete cultural package.
- **Established:** institutions, scripts, and armor forms overlap; **contested:** explanatory models and period boundaries; **thin:** exact ordinary speech, local distributions, and many personal dress biographies.
- Named frameworks below are analytic tools: Frellesvig’s language stages, Kuroda’s **kenmitsu** model, **honji suijaku**, and institutional diffusion. None is a timeless national psychology.
- Coupling is conditional, not deterministic: armor does not reveal a warrior’s sect, a crest does not establish ancestry, and a Portuguese loan does not establish conversion.

## 2. Era staging: several clocks, not one costume switch

| Historical stage | Working dates | Coupled changes to track | Anachronism guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late Heian / warrior ascendancy | Twelfth century | Court prestige, estate institutions, mounted military elites; kana and sinographic literacies already coexist | Do not begin with a fully autonomous, culturally uniform “samurai nation” |
| Kamakura | 1185/1192–1333 | Warrior government alongside court and temple authority; older Buddhist institutions and new movements coexist | Different foundation dates answer different political questions |
| Nanbokuchō / early Muromachi | Fourteenth century; rival courts 1336–1392 | Contested legitimacy, mobile coalitions, wider elite use of close-fitting armor | Military equipment does not change on a dynasty’s opening day |
| Muromachi / early Sengoku | Fifteenth century; Ōnin War 1467–1477 as a common hinge | Regional power, urban associations, temple and warrior patronage networks | Sengoku onset varies by region and historian |
| Later Sengoku / unification | Sixteenth century; 1568/1573, 1600/1603 are alternative hinges | Castle-town expansion, larger armies, newer armor construction, Christian missions and printing | “Sengoku” and “Momoyama” are overlapping political/art-historical labels |
| Early Tokugawa boundary | After 1600/1603 | Sources printed in 1603–1608 preserve late-sixteenth-century material but also belong to a changing polity | Mature Edo status rules, dress conventions, and persecution apparatus are not default Sengoku facts |

- Frellesvig’s **Late Middle Japanese, approximately 1200–1600**, crosses several political periods; a change of shogunate is not a language-family split.
- “Feudal” is a comparative convenience with contested scope; identify land rights, service obligations, and jurisdiction instead of importing a European vassal diagram unchanged (Friday 2004; Berry 1994).

## 3. Language family, writing, and the evidence ladder

- Japanese belongs to **Japonic**, alongside the Ryukyuan languages; classification does not make Ryukyuan merely a provincial register. A demonstrated “Ural-Altaic” family must not be assumed (Frellesvig 2010).
- **Kanji** represent lexical and grammatical material with multiple reading traditions; **kana** supply phonographic resources. These are interacting systems, not a progression from primitive pictures to a finished alphabet.
- **Kanbun** and Japanese reading practices mediate Chinese textual authority; mixed Japanese writing and kana genres support other uses. Do not equate kanbun literacy with conversational Chinese fluency.
- Buddhist Sino-Japanese vocabulary is an accumulated transregional layer, not a lexical invention of Kamakura sect founders. New institutions redistribute and reinterpret older terms.
- Jesuit romanization offers unusually valuable phonological and register evidence near 1600; it is neither modern Hepburn nor a transparent recording of every region and class.

| Evidence channel | What it can support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Dated letter, petition, grant, oath | Formulae, titles, address relations, institutional authority | A verbatim conversation or every writer’s personal speech |
| Monastic text, sermon, foundation narrative | Authorized terms, doctrinal mappings, claims to legitimacy | Equal comprehension or observance by all laypeople |
| War tale or performed narrative | Heroic self-presentation, remembered names, religious framing | Battlefield frequency, reliable speeches, or a universal warrior ethic |
| *Vocabulario* / Rodrigues | Recorded lexical distinctions and grammatical commentary around 1600 | Unchanging usage since 1200; unmediated access to speakers |
| Object inscription plus material examination | Label, ownership claim, construction, component chronology | Original meaning of every motif or an untouched original ensemble |

## 4. Warrior registers: write relationships, not “samurai voice”

- Separate **field** (service, petition, challenge), **tenor** (superior, equal, dependent), and **mode** (spoken, dictated, written, performed); this is a design application of ../linguistics/sociolinguistics-registers.md, not a newly discovered medieval native taxonomy.
- A warrior could employ clerks, religious specialists, court titles, and literary models. “Warrior language” was not an isolated dialect opposed to all court culture (Friday 2004).

| Register problem | Historical anchor | Reusable coupling | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Submission and requests | **Sōrō** and sōrō-style epistolary conventions | Formulae, spacing, signature, and delivery personnel can signal rank together | Formula and recipient relation require a dated document; this entry gives no template to copy verbatim |
| Reverence and deference | Honorific/humble resources, titles, and indirect reference | Who is named, whose action is elevated, and which garment is worn should agree | Do not paste modern textbook keigo unchanged into 1450 |
| Military service | **Bushi**, **samurai**, **hōkō** (service), **chigyō** (holdings/income rights) | A vocabulary of obligations and reward can carry the politics of a household | These labels are not perfectly interchangeable ranks across all centuries |
| Martial self-presentation | Names, affiliations, and deeds in war narratives | A performed identity can align speech, crest, and claimed patron | Stock challenges and speeches are literary evidence, not routine transcripts |
| Contact translation | Rodrigues’ grammatical descriptions and missionary dictionaries | Interpreters must learn social relations as well as word substitutions | Recorded labels reflect missionary purposes and selected informants |

- **Gozaru**, **sessha**, **kisama**, and theatrical sentence endings are not a ready-made pan-medieval dialogue kit; their dated meanings and distributions need individual checks against identified texts, not assumption.
- Avoid a single immutable “bushidō code”: medieval obligations and ideals are real, but modern nationalist synthesis is not their unchanged source (Benesch 2014).
- For fiction, specify an address matrix and a letter template before writing dialogue; record invented forms as invented rather than attributing them to historical speakers.

## 5. Buddhist sect vocabulary spreads through networks

- Kuroda’s **kenmitsu** framework foregrounds the institutional power of exoteric–esoteric Buddhism; it corrects an older textbook tendency to make a few “new Kamakura schools” the whole medieval religious world (Kuroda 1996).
- Treat the framework as influential and debated, not proof that older institutions were uniform or that newer communities lacked agency.

| Institutional network | Vocabulary / practice anchors | Likely carriers and material interfaces | Do not flatten into |
|---|---|---|---|
| Tendai and established temple complexes | **Hokke** / Lotus discourse, esoteric practice, **mappō** (latter age of the Dharma) | Clerical training, texts, patronage, ritual commissions | An obsolete institution displaced everywhere by “new Buddhism” |
| Shingon / esoteric specialists | **Dainichi** (Mahāvairocana), mantra, **bonji** | Ritual specialists; icons, talismans, Sanskrit-derived Siddham characters | A Japanese creator god plus decorative “magic runes” |
| Pure Land traditions | **Amida**, **nenbutsu**, **Namu Amida Butsu** | Teachers, lay gatherings, recitation, images and devotional objects | One centralized sect with one social constituency |
| Jōdo Shinshū / Honganji networks | Shinran’s tradition, **Honganji**, lay religious association | Congregations, correspondence, patrons, fortified institutional centers | Every Pure Land adherent being a Honganji partisan |
| Rinzai Zen / Five Mountains | **Zen**, **Gozan**; Chinese literary learning | Monasteries, warrior patronage, diplomacy, printing and literary exchange | “All samurai were Zen” or Zen having no ritual/material life |
| Sōtō Zen | **Zazen**, temple and lineage vocabulary | Developing regional temple networks as well as monastic training | “Peasant Zen” as an exhaustive social label |
| Nichiren / Lotus adherents | **Daimoku**, **Namu Myōhō Renge Kyō**, **Hokke** | Preaching and urban lay communities; Kyoto neighborhood politics | A peaceful, apolitical “chanting sect” or a uniform merchant religion |

- **Nenbutsu** and **daimoku** are not interchangeable “Buddhist prayers”: their scriptural referents, institutional affiliations, and polemical contexts differ.
- **Kō** can designate an association; its purpose and local constitution must be specified. An **ikki** is an alliance/uprising category, not by itself a doctrine or a synonym for “peasants.”
- **Ikkō-ikki** requires actor- and source-specific treatment: hostile naming, Honganji connections, local politics, and participant diversity matter; do not infer every member’s theology from a label.
- These distribution sketches synthesize institutional scholarship, not a measured map of village vocabulary; locality-specific reach and exact household usage are not established in this entry.
- Fictional application: let a recitation, a school’s writing practice, and a patron’s donated robe travel on different networks; shared vocabulary need not produce shared allegiance (Kuroda 1996; Berry 1994).

## 6. Mythic core and symbolic systems: combinatory, not a fixed pantheon chart

- **Honji suijaku** relates Buddhist “original grounds” to local kami “traces/manifestations”; actual identifications vary by institution and period. It is not one nationally standardized god-equivalence table (Teeuwen and Rambelli 2003).
- Kami cults, Buddhist cosmologies, ancestral concerns, court ritual, and local foundation narratives overlap. “Shinto versus Buddhism” as two clean, mutually exclusive memberships is a bad default for this period.
- Kuroda’s critique of timeless independent Shinto is influential; a claim that medieval people had *no* distinctive kami discourse would overcorrect it. Separate local practice, priestly theories, and later state classification (Kuroda 1981).

| Reference cluster | Language / symbol / institution coupling | Craft caution |
|---|---|---|
| Amaterasu and Ise traditions | Divine genealogy can authorize institutions and claims of imperial legitimacy | Court genealogy is not the only religious map used by every community |
| Hachiman | Warrior patronage and kami–bodhisattva identifications | A “war god” gloss omits Buddhist status and shrine-specific history |
| Amida, Kannon, Dainichi, Fudō | Salvation, compassion, cosmic Buddhahood, and protective/esoteric efficacy have distinct vocabularies | Not four interchangeable patron “gods”; iconography requires identification |
| **Engi**, foundation narratives | Place, relic, deity, founder, and patronage can be narrated together | A legitimizing story is evidence of a claim, not proof of the miracle |
| **Mon**, crests and devices | Recognized affiliation can be repeated on garments, armor, and banners | A device is not a unique biological lineage identifier or a universal word-for-word code |
| **Bonji**, inscriptions, sacred emblems | Script can index ritual authority on objects as well as transmit readable language | Display does not prove the wearer can read it; claimed protection is not verified physical efficacy |

- Read a motif with its accompanying inscription, institutional owner, dated use, and reception history; avoid universal “red = courage” or “three swirls = one fixed theology” dictionaries.
- The productive design pattern is **multiple authoritative readings of one surface**, including disagreement between patron, artisan, priest, and ordinary viewer.

## 7. Costume and armor: diffusion, refitting, and status lag

| Form / material | Era staging and construction | Social/linguistic coupling | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Ō-yoroi**, “great armor” | Prominent in late Heian/Kamakura elite mounted warfare; box-like lamellar construction and large shoulder defenses | Mounted-archery prestige, costly lacing, inherited display | Not what every medieval fighter wore; survival does not prove contemporary battlefield dominance |
| **Dō-maru**, “body wrap” | Close-fitting wraparound cuirass, conventionally fastening at the right; long coexistence with other forms | A form associated with less exalted users could be elaborated for elite wear | Not invented only when the sixteenth century began; component fastening needs object-level checking |
| **Haramaki** | Conventionally distinguished by a rear opening; also elaborated beyond humble early associations | Similar mobility needs can yield distinct technical vocabularies | Historical labels and later museum/connoisseur classifications need matching object by object; no object is matched here |
| **Tōsei gusoku**, “contemporary/modern equipment” | Later-sixteenth-century developments include larger iron plates and varied cuirass constructions | Larger armies, craft supply, command display, and contact adaptation | Not one uniform suit; not all earlier armor vanished after firearms appeared |
| **Kosode**, textiles and sashes | Small-sleeved garment’s expansion into principal outer dress provides a different clock from armor changes | Textile expense, pattern, occasion, and wearer shape social readings | Do not import a complete nineteenth-century kimono/obi ensemble into 1500 |

- The **ō-yoroi → dō-maru** shorthand names changing prestige and practical use, not a clean evolutionary replacement. Wealthy users could adopt and embellish a previously less prestigious form.
- Direct anchor: the Met’s dō-maru, **14.100.120a–g**, has an armor date of **ca. 1550** and an **eighteenth-century helmet**. It demonstrates elite sixteenth-century dō-maru use and the danger of dating an assembled suit from one component.
- The museum’s account connects this armor with Date Masamune’s reward to Shiraishi Bungo in 1594; distinguish that recorded provenance narrative from independently established original manufacture.
- **Kozane**, lacing, lacquer, iron/leather, and silk imply specialist labor and maintenance; record materials and repairs before assigning a mythic interpretation to the finish.
- **Mon**, banners, helmet ornaments, and robes can make rank legible, but precise permission rules and meanings vary. No fixed heraldic color grammar is assumed here.
- **Nanban** equipment belongs to a contact history, not proof that every wearer was Christian; European-style cuirasses were a selective material adoption, not wholesale European dress.
- V&A’s overview supports the broad sixteenth-century dress transition; exact kosode cut, sash width, textile accessibility, and regional ensembles require dated garments or images; this entry collates none.

## 8. Naming people and castle-town space

- Separate a person’s **house/territorial designation**, **adult personal name**, **childhood name**, **office/title**, and **Buddhist name**; the slots are not all spoken together or available to every person.
- **Genpuku** can couple coming-of-age, adult naming, patronage, and dress. A religious name can belong to a living person; it is not automatically a posthumous label.
- Address by office or relationship can be more appropriate than a personal name; exact household convention, gender distribution, and chronology require person-specific records, which are not collated here. Do not infer that women lacked names because records often identify them relationally.
- A shared name element can signal patronage or affiliation as well as genealogy. Use ../linguistics/naming-systems.md as a slot-design method, not as proof of a particular historical name’s reading.

| Settlement term / element | Working distinction | Naming-system application |
|---|---|---|
| **Jōkamachi** 城下町 | Castle town: settlement organized in relation to a castle and its authority | Distinguish castle designation, older settlement name, and official administrative name |
| **Jō** 城 / **jōka** 城下 | Castle / “below the castle” | A fortification term is not an automatic suffix for every settlement |
| **Monzenmachi** 門前町 | Temple/shrine approach or gate-front town | A sacred destination can organize commerce and geographic reference |
| **Jinaimachi** 寺内町 | Temple-precinct town, with institution-specific organization | Religious affiliation and jurisdiction may shape the settlement before warrior incorporation |
| **Machi / chō** 町 | Town, neighborhood, or administrative locality, according to context | Preserve local readings; do not alternate readings merely for variety |
| **Teramachi** 寺町 / occupational elements such as **kaji** 鍛冶 | Temple district / smith-related naming | A name may preserve allocation, concentration, relocation, or older land use; it does not prove one present population |
| Older terrain and village names | Watercourses, elevations, fields, and earlier communities predate a new urban regime | Let old oral names survive official renaming; an etymology requires records, not kanji guessing |

- Castle towns are not all born on empty ground. Temple, market, village, and warrior jurisdictions can be overlaid or reorganized; the label describes a relation to power, not one identical plan.
- Berry’s Kyoto is an urban comparison, **not itself a representative castle town**; Satoh supplies morphological comparison, much of it involving later development. Neither warrants a universal 1550 street grid.
- Defensive approaches, commercial districts, temples, and residences can influence place naming, but a neat Edo cadastral map must not be treated as a direct Sengoku map.
- Fictional method: retain old name, new official label, assigning authority, dated reason, script form, pronunciation, and dissenting local usage as separate fields. Historical local etymologies remain unestablished until documented in records.

## 9. The Kirishitan layer: translate concepts, not just nouns

- Xavier’s arrival in **1549** anchors the mission’s sixteenth-century expansion; influence was regional and uneven, particularly strong in parts of Kyūshū, not a national language replacement.
- Distinguish Portuguese trade/contact vocabulary, Latin liturgical or learned vocabulary, and Japanese Christian usage. An imported commodity name does not establish a Christian speaker.
- **Kirishitan** is historically tied to Portuguese *cristão*; **bateren** to *padre* and **iruman** to *irmão*. These distinguish Christian identity and missionary roles; they are not interchangeable labels for “foreigner.”

| Translation strategy | Attested or securely documented anchor | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Retain a marked foreign term | **Deus** / conventional Japanese **Deusu**; **anima** in catechetical discourse | A contested theological referent can remain visibly foreign rather than equated with a local deity |
| Reuse an established religious word | **Goshō** for afterlife; **gedatsu** in Christian explanation | Familiar vocabulary offers intelligibility but carries earlier Buddhist associations |
| Add Japanese honorific morphology | **Go-Passiom** in the 1592 *Fides no dǒxi*, discussed by Lough §II after Ward | A borrowed sacred concept enters local deference grammar; not merely a foreign token dropped into unchanged syntax |
| Reassign a Buddhist semantic resource | **Go-nyūmetsu** for Christ’s death in *Dochirina Kirishitan*, discussed after Higashibaba | Reuse can imply analogies translators must manage without making Buddhist and Christian doctrines identical |
| Address different literacies | Catechisms in Japanese script and romanized editions; dictionary and grammar production | Script, audience, pedagogy, and authority become linked design decisions |
| Dispute an apparent equivalence | The reported early **Dainichi–Deus** identification and its rejection | Translation failure can generate explicit boundary-making, not just a permanent synonym |

- These anchors follow Lough (2023), §II, citing Kaiser (1996), Higashibaba (2001), and Ward; spelling is reproduced or normalized as stated, not a diplomatic transcription of inspected primary copies.
- The Dainichi episode is a missionary-mediated report of a translation problem, not evidence that Buddhists “really worshipped the Christian God”; the standard sequence — Dainichi adopted for the Trinity, then rejected once its *śūnyatā* sense emerged — is documented in scholarship (Farge 2012:67–68, cited in Lough 2023), while the primary Jesuit wording remains uncollated here.
- Japanese converts, assistants, women catechists, and readers participated in translation and transmission. A European-authored publication is not evidence that all conceptual work was European.
- Record the trade route, teaching institution, translating actor, text, script, audience, and local adoption for each lexical layer. A mission dictionary’s inclusion of a word does not establish mass everyday use.
- Hideyoshi’s **1587** expulsion order, the **1597** Nagasaki executions, and Tokugawa repression belong to successive political contexts; prohibit a simple “toleration until an overnight 1600 ban” narrative.
- Routine **fumi-e** testing belongs to seventeenth-century Tokugawa surveillance — *fumie*/*efumi* ceremonies were gradually introduced on a wide scale under Iemitsu, and the *shūmon aratame* system followed in 1640 (Morris 2018:415) — so neither it nor the well-documented hidden-Christian practice is automatic Sengoku scenery.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Choose several era clocks:** political regime, language stage, religious network, equipment cohort, and textile fashion; let them overlap rather than assigning one date to all.
2. **Build vocabulary by carrier:** inherited vernacular, sinographic administration, Buddhist learning/recitation, household service, imported doctrine. Specify who acquires each layer and through which institutions.
3. **Pair word with surface:** a service petition with title and seal; a recitation with congregation and devotional object; a sacred character with a commissioned piece of armor. These are design options, not automatic historical correlations.
4. **Design prestige reversal:** a mobile armor form can climb socially while older equipment survives as heirloom or ceremony. Keep technical name, construction, ownership, and symbolic claim separate.
5. **Model rival maps:** castle jurisdiction, temple affiliation, market neighborhood, and old village identity can name the same space differently. Renaming is an act with an author and an audience.
6. **Make contact selective:** translators may retain a foreign deity-name, reuse an afterlife term, and attach local honorific morphology; let contemporaries argue about the implications.
7. **Hand off options, not canon:** supply constraints, alternatives, dated reference anchors, uncertainties, and proposed coupling checks through ../synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md; lore-shokunin decides world-bible acceptance.

## 11. Provenance cautions and limits

- **Inspection boundary:** source metadata and the linked museum records, V&A overview, Berry publisher description, Satoh abstract, Kishimoto paper, and Lough article were checked on 2026-10-17; the Satoh volume year, Morris (2018), and Lough's citation of Farge (2012:67–68) were re-inspected in the same pass. Bibliographic confirmation is not full-text verification; the armor PDF did not yield usable article text, and primary Jesuit editions were not inspected.
- **Mediated citations:** Higashibaba and Kaiser examples are reported via Lough and should be cited at Lough's page references; their original passages and primary spellings were not collated, so use them for concepts, not for quotation, dialogue reconstruction, or a philological claim. Lough is a modern interpretation, not a substitute for source criticism.
- **Mission/colonial-source bias:** Jesuit accounts served conversion, fundraising, and controversy within Portuguese imperial networks. Japan was not thereby a Portuguese colony; European reports are neither neutral ethnography nor the only available Japanese voices.
- **Chronicle scarcity:** exact ordinary speech and costume use among nonelite rural people, many women, and particular regional communities are thinly represented in the sources used here. Literary dialogue, elite archives, and surviving luxury objects cannot silently fill those gaps.
- **Object bias:** wealthy patrons, temple/shrine treasuries, later refitting, and collecting history shape survivals. Date each component; an elaborate surviving dō-maru does not establish the average infantry kit.
- **Contested models:** kenmitsu, honji suijaku analysis, “feudalism,” and Sengoku boundaries are useful but debated; modern bushidō essentialism and a demonstrated “Ural-Altaic” genealogy are not accepted defaults. Costume labels and sect labels require historical context, not national stereotypes.
- **Release rule:** the limits above are now stated as calibration rather than pending flags; unresolved items — primary Jesuit editions, original ordinances, object-level label matches, local etymologies — must still be checked against dated records or inspected editions before quotation. This entry offers reference constraints and generative method; it certifies neither a reconstruction of every medieval Japanese life nor any fictional canon.
