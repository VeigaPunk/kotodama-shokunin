## Scope and calibration

- **Austronesian is a language-family designation**, not a race, one state, one mythology, or one costume repertoire. A homeland hypothesis does not identify an unchanged ancestral society.
- **Formosan** conventionally groups Taiwan's Indigenous Austronesian languages geographically; it must not automatically become a single genetic subgroup opposed to Malayo-Polynesian (Blust 2013).
- Taiwan's Atayal, Amis, Paiwan, and other peoples are not interchangeable; neither are Tagalog, Visayan, Ifugao, and other Philippine traditions. Modern national borders are not linguistic boundaries.
- This entry samples Atayal testimony, Tagalog colonial descriptions, Visayan ritual terminology, Tuwali-Ifugao landscape categories, and Philippine fibre industries; each sample retains its own locality and evidence date.
- **Calibration labels:** established = well-supported at the stated scale; model = an explicit scholarly interpretation; source-bound = testimony or description that cannot safely be generalized; `(to-verify)` = unresolved detail, not material ready for historical assertion.
- Living cultures are not survivals waiting to be mined. Separate publicly described knowledge from permission to reproduce a restricted ritual, sacred design, community-owned narrative, or identifiable person's work.

## Language family, typology, and the homeland argument

| Reference scale | Linguistic distinction | Coupling implication |
|---|---|---|
| Taiwan's Formosan languages | Several early-diverging branches of Austronesian, not varieties of one “Taiwanese tribal language” | Give neighboring communities separate sound histories and ritual vocabularies |
| Atayal and Seediq | Related Atayalic languages, not permission to collapse distinct peoples | Shared name morphology can coexist with different identities and histories |
| Tao/Yami of Orchid Island | Malayo-Polynesian; Blust places it with Batanic/Bashiic languages | A politically Taiwanese community need not occupy a Formosan branch |
| Philippine languages | Tagalog, Cebuano, Ifugao languages, and others belong within Malayo-Polynesian | “Philippine” is not one language or a sufficient fine-grained genealogical label |
| Modern Taiwan as a whole | Indigenous Austronesian languages coexist with Sinitic and other languages | Do not classify Mandarin or Taiwanese Hokkien as Austronesian |

1. **Comparative method:** establish regular sound correspondences and shared innovations before assigning branches; vocabulary resemblance alone can reflect borrowing (Blust 2013).
2. **Diversity/homeland inference:** Blust's ten-primary-branch model places nine branches exclusively in Taiwan and the remaining Malayo-Polynesian branch across a much wider area. Deep diversity concentrated in Taiwan supports a Taiwan homeland for the dispersal, not merely “many languages means oldest people.”
3. **Calibration:** the exact high-level subgrouping is debated; extinction, contact, sampling, and alternative branching proposals affect the argument. State the classification used instead of presenting the branch count as universally settled.
4. **Triangulation:** linguistic dispersal, archaeological assemblages, population movement, and present identity are different evidential objects. No shared pot, gene, or textile automatically supplies a shared language or deity.
5. **Typological use:** Austronesian voice systems, affixation, and reduplication offer design comparisons, but their distributions vary. Do not replace an actual grammar with a universal “Austronesian template” or assume a simple English active/passive mapping.

**Fictional application:** propose related languages with regular innovations, then model maritime borrowing separately from inheritance. A prestige garment name can travel across unrelated political borders without bringing its source culture's entire religion.

## Writing: baybayin is an orthographic system, not an alphabet-shaped emblem

The following mechanics are established for the relevant script conventions, not all historical or contemporary Philippine writing (Unicode Consortium 2025, §17.1).

| Feature | Checked distinction | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Script type | Baybayin is an abugida; a consonant letter has inherent /a/ | Specify how a reader supplies vowels before drawing glyphs |
| Vowel signs | A mark above supplies i/e; below supplies u/o in the described convention | Graphic distinctions need not map one-to-one to modern phonemes |
| Traditional coda treatment | Traditional orthography often leaves syllable-final consonants unwritten | Missing consonants in writing do not imply their absence in speech |
| Vowel cancellation | López's seventeenth-century cross-shaped virama is a reform; later pamudpod conventions require their own history | Mark reform layers rather than projecting a modern spelling backward |
| Attestation | Spanish descriptions date from the sixteenth century; a Manila book was printed in 1593 | A first surviving print is not the invention date of the script |
| Related scripts | Hanunóo, Buhid, and Tagbanwa are related traditions, not securely direct descendants of Tagalog baybayin | Do not rename living script communities as mere remnants of one national script |
| Genealogy | Brahmi-derived affinities are clear; the exact transmission route is uncertain | A precise island-by-island origin story remains `(to-verify)` |
| Medium | Bamboo inscription is documented; layout interacts with how material is held and cut | Design tools, surfaces, and reading orientation together |

- Traditional underrepresentation can work with shared oral context; it is not proof that users were unable to express complex thought.
- Distinguish language, script, font, spelling reform, and current cultural revival. A modern Unicode repertoire is not an inventory of marks available in every historical period.
- **Application:** stage an old community spelling alongside a mission/administrative reform and later heritage typography; test the same spoken name in each, without declaring a fictional history canonical.

## Mythic and pantheon core: begin with relations, not a merged god list

| Located evidence | Core available for comparison | Boundary on inference |
|---|---|---|
| Tagalog account, Plasencia 1589/1903 | Bathala/Badhala appears as a powerful maker; ancestor observance, offerings, and ritual specialists also appear | Missionary “creator,” “idol,” “heaven,” and “devil” translations are interpretations, not neutral equivalents |
| Visayan material synthesized by Scott 1994 | Diwata language and spirit-directed ritual belong to a locally specific religious field | Do not attach every popular Philippine deity list to every Visayan island or century |
| Tuwali-Ifugao, Findley et al. 2022 | Old Kiyyangan has place-specific origin significance for terrace agriculture and associated rituals | Mythic origin, earliest occupation, first terracing, and wet-rice intensification are not the same date |
| Ifugao museum account | Mumbaki conduct baki; bulul figures and offerings belong within particular ritual assemblages | A carved figure is not self-explanatory evidence for one archipelago-wide “rice god” |
| Atayal testimony, Cultural Survival 2020 | Gaga concerns ancestral teachings and social obligations; tattooing is linked to competence, recognition, and afterlife access | No warrant to make gaga a deity or every Atayal account identical |
| Taru's Atayal account, 2022 | A girl-to-bird legend, named P'jit in her telling, informs contemporary woven artworks | The named artist's interpretation is neither an all-Taiwan myth nor an ancient garment prescription |

- **Analytical framework:** record participant → place → obligation → offering/performance → expected response. This reveals social organization without requiring a Greek-style bounded pantheon.
- Treat *anito* and *diwata* as context-sensitive vocabulary, not fixed translations of “ghost” and “fairy.” Verify the meaning in the language, period, and sentence being used.
- Detailed Amis, Paiwan, Tao, and other Taiwanese cosmologies are thinly covered here; complete deity inventories, restricted narratives, and ritual sequences remain `(to-verify)`. Do not fill these gaps with Atayal material.

## Babaylan and adjacent ritual specialists

- **Located labels:** Visayan *baylan/babaylan* and Tagalog *catalonan/katalonan* are comparison points, not interchangeable job titles for the whole archipelago (Scott 1994; Brewer 2004; Fluckiger 2017).
- Healing, spirit mediation, divination, and communal ceremony can intersect. Record which functions a particular source actually assigns instead of making every practitioner a universal magician.
- Women held substantial ritual authority in the documented settings. Sources also describe specialists using gendered roles or presentation that missionaries classified as male, effeminate, or deviant.
- *Asog* in Visayan discussion and *bayog* in Tagalog discussion require local glosses; neither automatically names all ritual specialists or maps exactly onto a present-day sexual orientation or gender identity.
- Conversely, do not erase gender variation because colonial descriptions are biased. Preserve the reported practice while distinguishing description, hostile judgment, and modern interpretation.
- “Shamanism” is a comparative scholarly category in Brewer's title, not evidence of one uniform institution or a necessary Indigenous self-name.
- Caquenga's Cagayan case shows how ritual authority could mobilize followers independently of a local political leader; its surviving narrative is principally Dominican and adversarial (Fluckiger 2017).
- Colonial suppression, conversion, accommodation, and continued practice are historical processes. “The babaylan disappeared at contact” is not a usable default.

| Coupling question | Useful design variable | Error to reject |
|---|---|---|
| Who recognizes competence? | Spirit calling, apprenticeship, lineage, or community acceptance—select and substantiate | “All priesthoods are hereditary” |
| How is authority audible? | Distinct vocabulary, song, invocation, or controlled speaking occasion | Inventing sacred vocabulary and presenting it as historical quotation |
| How is authority visible? | Source-specific accessories and performance setting | One generic feathered “tribal shaman” costume |
| What happens under mission rule? | Rival legitimacy, concealment, translation, conversion, and changing patrons | A single before/after switch that erases Indigenous agency |

## Ifugao terrace vocabulary as an ethnoecological system

**Named framework:** the **muyong–uma–payo complex**, not “rice terraces” as isolated scenery. Land-use terms below follow Findley et al. (2022), Table 1, explicitly identified there as Tuwali-Ifugao usage in the Kiangan region; the final ritual rows follow the National Museum's exhibition description.

| Term | Scoped working gloss | System coupled to vocabulary |
|---|---|---|
| *payo* | Wet-field/rice terrace | Irrigation, maintenance labor, tenure, and differential access to rice |
| *uma / habal* | Swidden field | Root crops and other cultivation alongside terraces, not a superseded “primitive stage” |
| *muyong* | Privately held managed woodlot/forest | Water regulation, timber, fuel, food, and inherited resource management |
| *inalahan* | Public forest in the cited table | Distinguish land categories and access rules; “public” is not automatically unrestricted |
| *mabilau* | Fallow swidden/caneland | Regrowth and time since cultivation become part of the lexical landscape |
| *magulun* | Pasture/grassland | Land-cover categories should not be collapsed into unworked wasteland |
| *latangan / boble / kubu* | Settlement-terrace labels grouped in the cited table | Verify their individual semantic ranges before treating them as freely substitutable synonyms `(to-verify)` |
| *mumbaki / baki* | Ritual specialist / ritual in the museum's description | Person, action, occasion, and paraphernalia need separate lexical entries |
| *bulul* | Carved figures included in that ritual assemblage | Object form, ritual activation/use, and tourist reproduction require separate records |

- **Chronology, model with substantial evidence:** work at Old Kiyyangan supports major wet-rice intensification in the colonial-contact period rather than a blanket multi-millennial rice-terrace history (Findley et al. 2022; Acabado and Martin 2022).
- Do not replace “all terraces are 2,000 years old” with “every terrace was invented in 1600.” Settlement, taro cultivation, earlier terraces, wet-rice adoption, and later construction phases require different archaeological questions.
- **Pericolonialism:** communities outside direct colonial control could still transform through nearby conquest, migration, exchange, and political pressure. “Unconquered” does not mean untouched.
- Chronicle evidence is sparse and late relative to many proposed changes. Findley et al. explicitly distinguish archaeological evidence, oral histories, retrospective ethnography, and model assumptions; their population and land-area estimates are not censuses.
- Hudhud narrative chanting is associated with agricultural work and mortuary occasions; narrator/chorus relations and formulaic language matter (UNESCO heritage record). Do not equate all Hudhud performers with mumbaki or all narrative chanting with baki.
- **Application:** draft a coupled lexicon for water source → managed woodland → field type → labor obligation → ritual occasion → performance genre; specify who owns, works, and speaks about each component.

## Hibiscus, buri, and ramie: different material chains

**Named craft framework:** use a **chaîne opératoire**, a sequence from resource access through extraction, preparation, fabrication, use, and repair. Do not identify a garment's material from its color or a plant's ornamental flower.

| Material and located evidence | What is supported | Costume/material constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Sea hibiscus, *Hibiscus tiliaceus* | Brown (1919), p. 79, records bast for rope, string, tying cattle, and hog traps; vernacular forms include malubago/malibago | Inner-bark fibre and cordage are supported; a named fine-cloth garment tradition is not established by this entry `(to-verify)` |
| Buri palm, *Corypha* | The 1913 manual calls it *C. elata* and documents prepared leaf strips and regional mat-making | Retain that as the historical botanical label; check current taxonomic usage separately |
| Buri mat work | Sleeping, drying, wrapping, and packing uses appear in the manual | A floor or sleeping mat is not automatically clothing; a coarse packing weave need not signify poverty in dress |
| Bohol raffia weaving | Centillas et al. document buri-resource dependence and preparation/weaving in Buenavista | Palm-derived woven cloth and plaited leaf-strip mats require distinct process entries; the abstract does not settle detailed fibre anatomy `(to-verify)` |
| Atayal ramie | Taru (2022) describes growing ramie, hand processing, dyeing, weaving, and garment reconstruction | Do not replace this nettle-family bast with hibiscus merely because both yield fibre |
| Abacá, *Musa textilis* | Brown (1919), pp. 56–57, describes a Philippine fibre plant and textile/cordage uses | It is a banana relative, not hemp in the botanical sense and not a palm |

- **Hibiscus/buri correction:** these are not one botanical material or an evidenced uniform “Austronesian textile tradition.” The strongest checked hibiscus evidence here concerns cordage; buri has independently documented mat and contemporary cloth contexts.
- A plant name can vary by language, island, and source orthography. Brown's place-indexed names are lexical evidence, not proof that each community made the same product from the plant.
- Taru names ramie *K'gi* in her account and links successful weaving to growing the material and knowing land, wind, and water. Preserve this situated testimony rather than declaring a word or philosophy universal.
- Her modern installations deliberately combine ramie with other materials. A contemporary artist's work can express inherited memory without being a reconstruction of an ancient ritual costume.
- The 1913 manual explicitly promotes foreign market preferences and criticizes local color combinations. Read those passages as colonial industrial pedagogy, not objective laws of good design or evidence of Indigenous color meanings.
- **Application:** give each proposed fibre its own harvesting rights, season, processing verbs, tool lexicon, waste streams, fabric behavior, and exchange route; keep unverified species-to-garment connections open.

## Costume and symbolic systems: make every sign conditional

| Evidence anchor | Coupled systems | Safe abstraction; unsafe shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Atayal weaving and tattoo testimony | Skill → social recognition → body marking → ancestral obligations | Competence can authorize a visible sign; do not copy facial designs as generic decoration |
| Taru's ramie revival | Language and place names → cultivation → museum research → reconstructed garments → contemporary art | Revival can be inventive and evidence-based; not “unchanged since prehistory” |
| Ifugao museum assemblage | Mumbaki → particular baki → *maamlag* accessories → offerings and objects | Ritual dress is an assemblage, not just a robe silhouette |
| Museum-listed *bayah*, *moma*, *hapid* | Rice wine, areca nuts, and betel leaves respectively | Consumables can signal preparation and obligation; verify use in the specific occasion |
| Buri mats and cloth | Household use, packaging, craft labor, and markets | One resource crosses use classes; this does not make every product a sacred textile |
| Plasencia's festival description | Chief's house, temporary shelter, lighting, palm-leaf decoration, sound, and gathering | Sacred space can be temporarily produced; the palm species cannot be inferred as buri `(to-verify)` |

**Symbol worksheet:** record form → local name → material/technique → authorized maker → wearer/user → occasion → interpretation source → date → restrictions.
A repeated diamond, red stripe, feather, or floral form does not possess a stable family-wide meaning. Similarity can arise through technique, trade, revival, or unrelated interpretation.
**Application:** alter a fictional production or recognition rule before inventing a visual motif; document how the change affects naming, obligations, and who may display the result.

## Era model: asynchronous histories, not one civilization ladder

| Staging layer | Historical anchor | Worldbuilding question |
|---|---|---|
| Linguistic prehistory | Homeland and dispersal hypotheses from comparative linguistics | Which features are inherited, and which later innovations distinguish branches? |
| Local histories before surviving descriptions | Evidence comes unevenly from archaeology, oral traditions, and retrospective records | What can be inferred without projecting a later costume or religion backward? |
| Philippine maritime contact worlds | Scott's sixteenth-century synthesis describes multiple societies and regional exchanges | Can a loanword or prestige object move without complete religious conversion? |
| Philippine mission/colonial encounters from the sixteenth century | Plasencia and later religious histories are interested witnesses | Which Indigenous voices become invisible or misclassified in official records? |
| Ifugao pericolonial transformation | Old Kiyyangan wet-rice intensification modeled across 1570–1800 | How can refuge, migration, status, and labor create a landscape that later looks timeless? |
| Japanese rule in Taiwan, 1895–1945 | Taru and tattoo testimony discuss cultural disruption and suppression | Which skills persist privately, and which objects become museum-held evidence? |
| Later language and identity pressures | Cultural Survival describes further assimilation and language hierarchy | How do official schooling and naming records diverge from community practice? |
| Contemporary reclamation | Taru's cultivation, workshop, and art; modern Bohol craft economies | Who innovates, teaches, earns income, and decides what continuity means? |

- These are comparison layers, not synchronous stages reached by every community. Do not export Taiwan's colonial dates to Philippine history or call all Indigenous material “precolonial.”
- Distinguish a source's observation date, claimed remembered past, object's manufacture date, and present reinterpretation. A late record can preserve older knowledge without precisely dating its origin.
- A useful era label may name a change in language policy, crop, weaving input, or authority rather than an externally imposed dynasty.

## Naming practices: relational grammar before exotic sound

- **Atayalic patronymy:** Ochiai (2021) analyzes a person's own name followed by the father's personal name; the paper's example *Kumu Watan* means Kumu, child/daughter of Watan. This is not evidence for a fixed inherited European-style surname.
- **Morphology matters:** the analysis distinguishes name roots, fossilized personal *y-*, and possessive *na*; citation forms, direct address, and patronymic contexts can differ. Do not attach the same prefix mechanically to every invented name.
- **Genealogy matters:** a name form may preserve older grammatical material. Record the relationship between speakers and generations rather than selecting disconnected syllables from different languages.
- **Philippine scope limit:** ritual titles, personal names, place names, baptismal names, and administratively recorded family names belong in different fields. A comprehensive precolonial Philippine naming template is not established here `(to-verify)`.
- **No surname shortcut:** do not infer ancestry, religious affiliation, or social status solely from a modern person's Spanish- or Chinese-looking registered name; determine the community's naming and administrative history first.
- **Material/place names:** use source-local plant and landscape terms as examples of semantic fields, not a ready-made fictional gazetteer. A place named for a plant does not prove continuous cultivation or an ancient etymology.
- **Application:** propose schemas for community address, genealogy, ritual office, and outside registration; generate forms only after choosing phonology and morphology. Keep the actual Atayal example as attributed reference, not fictional canon.

## What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Branching without essentialism:** deep linguistic diversity can motivate related but distinct cultures; regular innovations are more useful than a single “island people” aesthetic.
2. **Orthography with social history:** an underrepresenting script, a reform, and a revival can encode competing literacy practices without implying an inferior spoken language.
3. **Authority as a coupled institution:** ritual vocabulary, gendered access, skill recognition, material assemblages, and political patronage can change together or at different speeds.
4. **Land-use vocabulary as world structure:** woodlot, swidden, wet field, fallow, and settlement terms make labor, tenure, ritual, and memory legible; mixed agriculture need not form an evolutionary ladder.
5. **Material specificity over a tropical mood board:** distinguish hibiscus cordage, buri mat plaiting, Bohol raffia cloth, Atayal ramie, and abacá; derive forms from process rather than borrowing a silhouette.
6. **A handoff, not a declaration:** provide lore-shokunin alternative coupling rules, historical analogies, evidence grades, and unresolved choices. Only the canon-owning sibling records accepted world-bible facts.

## Provenance cautions and stop conditions

- **Colonial-source bias:** Plasencia and missionary histories aimed to identify, classify, and replace religious practices. Their hostile categories and reported conversion totals require criticism, not literal reuse.
- **Translation layers:** the 1589 report, 1903 English edition, modern historical analysis, and this craft abstraction are separate layers. Check the Spanish text before arguing from an exact translated term `(to-verify)`.
- **Heritage summaries are not chronometric proof:** do not adopt UNESCO's proposed early Hudhud origin, its blanket kinship description, or popular terrace-antiquity formulas without specialist corroboration `(to-verify)`.
- **Model versus measurement:** the Ifugao interpretation has archaeological support, but not every demographic parameter or causal link is directly observed; preserve site scope and uncertainty.
- **Practitioner testimony has scope:** Taru's workshop history and quoted elders are valuable evidence precisely because they are situated, not because one speaker represents all Indigenous Taiwan.
- **Thin coverage remains:** region-specific babaylan regalia, individual Taiwanese pantheons, historical Philippine name grammars, and hibiscus-to-garment traditions need further community-specific sources `(to-verify)`.
- **Reject discredited shortcuts:** racial civilization ladders, “primitive animism” as an inferior stage, a uniform Austronesian pantheon, and universal motif/color dictionaries are not historical frameworks. New local pedagogical models in a craft article are likewise not established laws of culture.
- **Stop rule:** if a proposed coupling rests only on a tourism image, unlocated myth list, uncertain botanical identification, or an unmarked leap across people and centuries, keep it an explicitly fictional option or leave it unresolved; never upgrade it into sourced tradition or canon.
