## Scope and calibration
- **Geography:** emphasize the southern Tigris–Euphrates alluvium: Uruk, Ur, Lagash/Girsu, Nippur, Eridu, Babylon, and Sippar; Assyria is related, not interchangeable.
- **Taxonomy:** Sumerian and Akkadian name languages; Akkadian also labels a dynasty/period; Babylonian names regional dialects and several political/cultural periods. These categories do not map neatly onto ancestry.
- **Attested:** an identifiable composition, grammatical description, inscription, object, or archive supports the statement; attestation does not make the source an impartial description of society.
- **Interpretive:** an inference or scholarly model; **craft:** a proposed design procedure, not an ancient institution; **(to-verify):** unresolved detail requiring a more specific witness.
- The strongest coupling evidence joins a dated text to its institution and material context. A first-millennium copy can preserve older tradition without documenting every earlier performance or costume.
- Sources are uneven: urban institutions, elite burial, royal display, and learned scribes survive disproportionately; rural speech, ordinary dress, and some dynastic intervals are much less visible. [Postgate 1992; Van De Mieroop 2016]

## Language family and typological contrast
| System | Supported description | Coupling use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Sumerian | Language isolate: no demonstrated genealogical relationship to another language; heavily agglutinative morphology, generally verb-final, with ergative patterns and important alignment qualifications. | Design productive affix chains and noun/verb morphology before surface names; “isolate” does not mean culturally isolated or morphologically simple. |
| Akkadian | East Semitic within Afroasiatic; consonantal roots with vocalic patterns, affixation, grammatical gender, and historically changing case morphology. | Use root-and-pattern derivation alongside inflection; do not build names by replacing vowels arbitrarily. |
| Akkadian syntax | Generally verb-final in much attested prose, despite familiar verb-initial patterns in some other Semitic languages. | Do not infer word order from family membership; contact and register must be modeled separately. |
| Babylonian / Assyrian | Major regional dialect traditions of Akkadian, each internally periodized; Standard Babylonian is a learned literary register. | Keep literary, bureaucratic, regional, and conversational forms on different layers. |
| Contact ecology | Sumerian and Akkadian coexisted and influenced one another; Sumerian remained a learned and cultic language after ordinary spoken use declined. | Separate home language, state administration, schooling, and ritual competence; a script's survival does not prove a speech community's survival. |

- Grammatical anchors: Jagersma (2010), especially for earlier Sumerian; Huehnergard (2011), especially for Old Babylonian Akkadian. Exact pronunciation, Sumerian dialect geography, and the timing of vernacular loss are incompletely recoverable.
- Reject proposed Sumerian–Turkic, Sumerian–Japanese, or other macro-family equations as established facts: resemblance and typological similarity are not demonstrated descent.
- **Craft:** let a later vernacular borrow prestigious ritual lexemes from an older language without copying that language's complete grammar or equating speakers with a biological “race.”

## Cuneiform: shared technology, differentiated access
- Cuneiform is a writing tradition, not a language or a single alphabet. Developed sign inventories combine word-signs, syllabic values, and determinatives; signs may have several readings. [Spar 2004; Jagersma 2010; Huehnergard 2011]
- Late-fourth-millennium proto-cuneiform includes accounting and lexical lists; assigning every earliest record securely to Sumerian overstates decipherment. Do not collapse the development into one inventor's alphabetic breakthrough.
- Wet clay and a stylus support counting, receipts, lists, contracts, letters, and learned compositions; stone inscriptions and seals serve different display and durability needs.
- A divine determinative, conventionally transliterated as superscript `d`, classifies a name; it is not an extra syllable to pronounce. Modern subscripts distinguish sign values, not ancient tone marks.
- Transliteration records sign readings; normalization reconstructs language; translation interprets meaning. Label which layer a generated inscription imitates.
- Sumerian word-signs can be used inside Akkadian writing; their appearance alone does not identify the spoken language. Shared literacy permits different pronunciation conventions and archives.
- **Craft:** distinguish an owner's seal, a clerk's tablet, a monumental royal text, and a memorized hymn; give each its own material, audience, register, and authentication mechanism.

## Era model: stage bundles, not a timeless aesthetic
Dates below are conventional orientation in BCE; third-millennium boundaries are approximate and second-millennium dates use the conventional Middle Chronology where applicable. Alternatives remain scholarly questions, not fictional errors. [Postgate 1992; Van De Mieroop 2016]

| Stage | Approximate span / anchor | Language–institution–material bundle | Do not back-project |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late Uruk | Later fourth millennium, roughly 3500–3100 | Expanding urban institutions; proto-cuneiform near the millennium's end; administrative imagery and seals. | Later Babylonian creation epic or a fully transparent spoken-language record. |
| Jemdet Nasr | Roughly 3100–2900 | Further administrative writing and regionally differentiated material assemblages. | A securely narrated unified dynasty inferred solely from a modern archaeological label. |
| Early Dynastic | Roughly 2900–2350 | Rival city-states; Sumerian and Semitic-language evidence; votive figures, temple estates, elite Ur burials. | All inhabitants dressed like court burials; a single centralized “Sumerian empire.” |
| Akkadian imperial period | Roughly 2350–2150 | Sargonic kingship; expanded Akkadian royal writing alongside Sumerian; imperial visual rhetoric. | “Akkadian” as exclusively the name of this period, or cultural replacement in one generation. |
| Post-Akkadian / Gudea of Lagash | Late third millennium, especially twenty-second century | Regional polities and temple-building claims; Gudea's Sumerian inscriptions and dedicated statues. | A uniform “dark age”; one exact date for every Gudea object or all neighboring polities. |
| Ur III | Conventionally 2112–2004 | Centralized administration, extensive Sumerian documentation, textile labor, and royal cult. | Surviving administrative language as proof of every worker's mother tongue. |
| Isin–Larsa / Old Babylonian | Early second millennium; Babylon's first dynasty ends conventionally 1595 | Competing courts, Akkadian letters/contracts, Sumerian scribal literature; Hammurabi's reign conventionally 1792–1750. | *Enūma Eliš* as securely authored by Hammurabi, or every law collection as a modern statute book. |
| Kassite / Middle Babylonian | Broadly after Old Babylonian to 1155; early transitions unevenly documented | Continued Babylonian scribal culture under new dynasts; land grants and inscribed monuments with divine emblems. | A clean total replacement of language, gods, or costume by dynastic ethnicity. |
| Post-Kassite and earlier first millennium | Twelfth–seventh centuries | Multiple dynasties, regional competition, continuity and reworking of learned traditions. | A chronicle-complete sequence of everyday cultural change. |
| Neo-Babylonian empire | 626–539 | Babylon-centered monarchy, monumental building, large temple and private archives; later divine textile evidence at Sippar. | Neo-Assyrian palace costume as interchangeable Babylonian evidence. |
| Achaemenid and later continuities | From 539; Seleucid rule follows Alexander's conquest in 331 | Babylonian scholarly and temple traditions persist within new imperial settings; Akkadian and Aramaic have differentiated uses. | Political conquest as an instant termination of cuneiform or local religion. |

- Period labels combine excavation sequences, dynasties, dialects, and modern convenience; boundaries will not synchronize across all cities or systems.
- **Stage separately:** spoken language → scribal register → royal ideology → temple organization → garment production → public display; allow inheritance and lag between them.
- **Thin coverage here:** early Kassite/Sealand transitions, provincial garment variation, and later local continuities require specialist follow-up; no precise reconstruction is claimed.

## Era lists and the politics of memory
- The *Sumerian King List* makes kingship descend from heaven and pass between cities; the flood separates its primeval and subsequent sequence. Its antediluvian reign lengths are not usable human lifespans. [ETCSL 2.1.1, lines 1–94]
- Manuscripts disagree about names, order, totals, and reign lengths. The ETCSL composite exposes variants rather than concealing them; it is not a single untouched tablet.
- Its sequential model of legitimate kingship can obscure contemporaneous rival rulers; compare inscriptions and administrative synchronisms before turning its succession into absolute chronology. [Postgate 1992; Van De Mieroop 2016]
- Year names, regnal-year dates, king lists, and chronicles are different genres. A year named for a building or victory records a dating/commemorative choice, not an impartial account of that reign.
- **Craft:** maintain separate tables for archaeology, political succession, temple calendars, and the court's sacred history. Disagreement can be institutional rather than an authorial continuity mistake.
- Do not fill poorly documented reigns with invented historical events; attach `(to-verify)` to an asserted synchronism until an identifiable source supports it.

## Pantheon: local institutions before universal portfolios
The following are recurring associations, not exhaustive job descriptions or perfectly stable bilingual identities. [Black and Green 1992; Lambert 2013]

| Name pairing / deity | Important association | Coupling question |
|---|---|---|
| An / Anu | Heaven and high divine authority; especially important at Uruk in some periods. | Does remoteness in myth correspond to, or differ from, local cult prominence? |
| Enlil | Nippur, divine authority and decisions about kingship; major role in *Atrahasis*. | Which sanctuary legitimates a king whose political capital is elsewhere? |
| Enki / Ea | Eridu, subterranean fresh water, wisdom, crafts, and resourceful intervention. | Which technologies, specialists, and ritual registers share a divine patron? |
| Inana / Ištar | Desire, war, Venus, and city-specific cults; Uruk is a major center. | Can an apparent contradiction in domains be maintained rather than reduced to “fertility goddess”? |
| Nanna / Suen / Sîn | Moon; Ur is a principal cult center. | How do lunar observation, monthly rites, and personal names reinforce one another? |
| Utu / Šamaš | Sun and justice; Sippar and Larsa are important centers. | How does visible daily order authorize legal or royal imagery? |
| Marduk | Babylon's patron and supreme god in *Enūma Eliš*. | How does a city's ascendancy reorder inherited divine genealogies and offices? |
| Nabû | Writing and learned expertise; strongly associated with Borsippa. | Which professionals reproduce the authority of a god through their own documentary work? |

- Equating names across Sumerian and Akkadian traditions is a historical learned practice, not evidence that every local cult was identical. Genealogies and rankings vary by text, city, and period.
- The human–divine relationship includes feeding, dressing, housing, praising, petitioning, and consulting gods: a temple cult is a service institution, not merely a collection of myths.

## Mythic core: compare mechanisms, not one “Mesopotamian Genesis”
| Composition / witness situation | Core narrative mechanism | Coupling lever | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Enūma Eliš*, seven-tablet Akkadian composition; Lambert (2013). | Primordial Apsû and Tiāmat; conflict culminates in Marduk's victory over Tiāmat, organization of the cosmos, humanity from Qingu's blood, and exaltation of Babylon/Marduk. | Divine sovereignty authorizes cosmic ordering; human service, city prestige, and the enumeration of divine names interlock. | Composition date is debated; many surviving witnesses are first-millennium. Not a generic third-millennium Sumerian creed. |
| *Atra-ḫasīs*, Old Babylonian and later witnesses; Lambert and Millard (1969). | Gods' labor crisis leads to humans made from clay with a slain god's flesh/blood; human noise prompts successive disasters and a flood; Ea preserves Atrahasis; post-flood restrictions regulate reproduction. | Labor, provisioning, divine disagreement, population, and human vulnerability form one explanatory system. | Manuscripts are damaged and variant; “noise” is not automatically a modern ecological theory or a moral indictment equivalent to later flood traditions. |
| *Inana's descent*, Sumerian composite; ETCSL 1.4.1. | Inana loses adornments and insignia at successive gates, dies, is restored, and cannot leave without a substitute. | Costume and objects enact jurisdiction: authority effective above does not exempt her from the underworld's order. | Do not merge its details silently with Akkadian *Ištar's Descent* or force a universal seasonal allegory. |
| *Sumerian King List*, ETCSL 2.1.1. | Kingship moves from city to city across primeval time, flood, and later dynasties. | Sacred history organizes political legitimacy through names, totals, and succession. | Ideological historiography with textual variants, not a transparent chronology. |

- *Enūma Eliš* gives humanity the gods' work; *Atrahasis* makes the labor problem a narrative starting point. Shared motifs do not make the compositions identical or prove one simple line of borrowing.
- In *Enūma Eliš*, Marduk's fifty names culminate his elevation: names can enumerate authority rather than merely label an individual. [Lambert 2013, tablets VI–VII]
- In *Inana's descent*, compare the dressing sequence with its removal: ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 14–25 and 129–163; manuscript variation even affects the placement of an insignia's removal.
- **Craft:** derive an institution, social obligation, ritual action, and prestige vocabulary from a mythic mechanism; do not assume its narrative supplies literal physics or uniform popular belief.

## Kingship and temple economy: coupled, not synonymous
- Distinguish **divine authorization**, a ruler's ritual service, and **the ruler's own divinization**. Naram-Sin and Šulgi are important examples of royal divinization; this was not the permanent status of every Mesopotamian king. [Van De Mieroop 2016; Black and Green 1992]
- Titles such as `lugal`, `ensi`, and `en` are city- and period-sensitive; translating them once as “king,” “governor,” or “priest” does not establish one immutable constitution. Gudea's `ensi` title coexists with substantial local ruling power.
- Gudea's dedicatory statues join inscription, worshipful posture, garment surface, and temple emplacement: the ruler is represented as a pious builder. The statue is not a candid portrait of ordinary dress. [Met 59.2; Cleveland 1963.154]
- Temples could control land, flocks, stores, workshops, dependents, and ritual provisioning; palaces, households, merchants, and other property relationships also mattered. A totalizing “temple-state owns everything” model is not a safe default. [Postgate 1992]
- Wool and textile labor connect animal management, organized work, allotments, prestige goods, and cult. Archives can classify women, men, children, and dependents differently; an allotment alone does not establish that every recipient was enslaved.
- **Craft pipeline:** identify resource → labor organization → textile grade → recipient/status → ritual occasion → accounting vocabulary; specify who authorizes each transfer.
- A temple's divine wardrobe and human personnel's dress are separate evidence classes. Neither a god's inventory nor a royal statue automatically supplies a universal priestly uniform.
- **Design test:** who can commission a garment, who produces it, who owns it after use, who records it, and what happens when the ruler or patron deity changes?

## Symbolic grammar: identify the bearer and dated medium
Recurring associations below are documented in Black and Green (1992); apply them to an identified object and period, not as a universal decoding key.

| Form / medium | Recurring association | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Horned divine headdress | Divinity in much Mesopotamian imagery; royal adoption can make an exceptional status claim. | Do not give every king horns or infer a zoological ancestry from the crown. |
| Crescent | Moon deity Sîn/Nanna in appropriate contexts. | Check neighboring signs, inscription, and date; a shape alone does not identify every bearer. |
| Solar disk / rays | Šamaš/Utu in appropriate iconographic contexts. | Do not collapse every circular motif into the same solar meaning. |
| Star, often eight-pointed | Ištar/Inana in many contexts. | Not every star or rosette is unambiguously this goddess; counts and forms vary. |
| Spade / stylus | Marduk / Nabû respectively in established emblem traditions. | Tool form, orientation, and assemblage matter; a generic craft tool is not automatically a divine emblem. |
| Cylinder-seal impression | Authentication, ownership/office, and selected visual relations with divine or human authority. | The scene's imagery and the transaction's legal function are related but not identical. |
| Lapis lazuli, gold, fine textile | Costly material, distance, patronage, and adornment; specific myths assign further value. | “Blue means divinity everywhere” exceeds the evidence; cost and meaning require context. |

- Sumerian `me`, often rendered “divine powers,” concerns culturally constitutive powers, offices, practices, or capacities in particular compositions; scholarly interpretation is not exhausted by one English gloss. It is not a universal mana currency. [Black and Green 1992; ETCSL 1.4.1]
- **Craft:** annotate each motif as bearer → material → medium → setting → permitted interpreter → possible competing interpretation; copying a symbol without its institution loses the coupling.

## Costume and material signatures: evidence-to-inference table
| Evidence / stage | Defensible design reference | Limit or verification task |
|---|---|---|
| Early Dynastic votive and elite imagery | Wrapped or skirt-like garments, conspicuously textured/flounced surfaces, formal posture, and differential adornment. | Carved texture does not uniquely establish fleece, woven pile, tufting, or construction; state any reconstruction as a proposal and check it object by object rather than settling on one reading. |
| Elite burial at Ur | Rich adornment and costly imported materials can communicate courtly hierarchy and ceremonial roles. | Burial assemblage is not the average wardrobe or proof of an unchanged kingdom-wide dress code. |
| Gudea statues | Draped garment, exposed areas of the body, clasped hands, inscription, and stone form create a deliberately pious royal image. | Compare individual objects; damaged or restored heads are unreliable costume evidence. Cleveland explicitly records a mistaken past head association. |
| Institutional textile records | Wool processing, named workers/recipients, quantities, and grades support a production-and-distribution model. | A textile term or weight is not automatically a recoverable sewing pattern. [Postgate 1992] |
| Neo-Babylonian Ebabbar archive, Sippar | Gods' and goddesses' garments have recorded shapes, weights, colors, and functions; wardrobe work is an organized cultic activity. | Zawadzki (2006) is a local, late archive; garment assignments and colours by deity are document-level checkable through Zawadzki's text volume (2013) and Quillien (2019), while per-year change schedules still need document-by-document collation. |
| Literary divine dress | Inana's headgear, beads, pectoral, ring, dress, and measuring equipment articulate authority and vulnerability. | A mythic dressing sequence is not a census of contemporary women's outfits. [ETCSL 1.4.1, lines 14–25] |

- **Code versus law:** distinguish customary display, cult prescription, workshop allocation, royal representation, and enforceable clothing law. Do not invent a Mesopotamia-wide sumptuary statute from an image.
- Middle Assyrian veiling regulations belong to a different regional/legal context; do not project them onto Early Dynastic Sumer or every Babylonian woman. [Westenholz 1989, reference 6]
- Do not infer “temple prostitute” from a woman's ornaments, goddess affiliation, or a loosely translated cultic title. The blanket institution is a deeply challenged reconstruction, not an explanatory default. [Westenholz 1989]
- **Craft:** specify garment shape, fiber, finish, fastening, maintenance, access, and occasion independently; an expensive border can signal institution or patronage without a fabricated color-to-caste code.

## Naming practices: grammar, devotion, and documentary identity
- Sumerian and Akkadian names draw on different grammars; people can bear names outside their everyday language. Name language alone does not securely identify ethnicity, origin, or ancestry. [Jagersma 2010; Huehnergard 2011; Postgate 1992]
- Theophoric names incorporate a deity; verbal or nominal clauses can declare divine favor, kinship, protection, or a gift. Shortened names and variant spellings make a single-token identity matcher unsafe.
- Attested anchors include **Narām-Sîn**, “beloved of Sîn,” and **Amar-Suena**, “calf of Suen”; the first is Akkadian, the second Sumerian. Parse the appropriate language rather than copying syllable shapes. [Huehnergard 2011; Jagersma 2010; King List, lines 266–296, 341–354]
- **Iddin-Dagān**, “Dagan has given,” illustrates a verbal theophoric structure; the king is attested in the King List, lines 355–377. The grammatical template is reusable; a particular deity's cult distribution still requires historical control.
- The historical English forms Sargon, Ur, and Inanna coexist with scholarly normalizations such as Šarru-kīn, Urim, and Inana; source conventions are not automatically diachronic sound changes.
- Administrative identity may add parentage, occupation/title, affiliation, and seal ownership; do not automatically append a modern inherited surname to every person.
- **Craft schema:** record normalized name → language → morphological parse → literal sense → social use → chronological distribution → attested spelling/source; keep proposed fictional forms explicitly unapproved.
- Separate personal names, throne names, divine epithets, temple names, and city names. Avoid generating all categories from one undifferentiated “ancient-sounding” syllable list.

## Applying the reference: an archive-stratified coupling grid
This is a modern craft procedure, not a named ancient framework or a claim that institutions followed one universal development path.

| Design decision | Pair the evidence | Reject the shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Ritual language survives vernacular change | Language history + schooling/cult + writing conventions. | “The conquered population vanished because its speech declined.” |
| Patron deity gains preeminence | City/political stage + literary theology + dated cult evidence. | “A new ruler instantly replaced the whole pantheon.” |
| Costume signals sacred office | Garment/inventory + wearer + occasion + local institution. | “A pictured crown proves a kingdom-wide clothing law.” |
| Catastrophe reorganizes history | Flood composition + king-list ideology + independent chronology. | “A shared flood motif proves one global historical event.” |
| Name style changes across eras | Attested corpus + grammar + family/document context. | “More apostrophes and longer names make an older language.” |

- **Minimal workflow:** select city and period → separate speech from script/register → choose one institutional relationship → trace a resource and its dress consequence → identify a mythic legitimation → derive naming grammar → check for anachronisms.
- For each proposed coupling retain **observation / source locator / interpretation / fictional transformation / uncertainty / approval status**. Lack of a local witness stays visible; do not launder analogy into attestation.
- Use `language-contact.md` and `writing-systems.md` for register inheritance; `flood-traditions.md` for comparison without false identity; `material-culture-inference-method.md` for image/text/object triangulation.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **Layered continuity:** a living vernacular, inherited scholarly language, and shared script can change at different rates while sustaining an institution's authority.
- **Institutional polytheism:** cities, workshops, families, courts, and scribal professions can privilege different divine relationships without requiring mutually exclusive religions.
- **Myth as social explanation:** divine labor, service, population, sovereignty, and naming offer mechanisms for constructing obligations, not just a list of supernatural beings.
- **Dress as enacted jurisdiction:** authority can reside in removable objects and in the institution authorized to issue, maintain, or confiscate them.
- **Era as a bundle with lag:** archive form, royal titulature, temple wardrobe, and popular speech need not switch on the same accession date.
- Deliver these as alternatives and constraints, with historical models labeled; lore-shokunin alone selects, records, and verifies fictional canon.

## Provenance cautions and explicit gaps
- **Verification boundary:** bibliographic records, publisher descriptions, the cited museum records, and the two ETCSL translations were checked on 2026-10-17; this is not a fresh collation of tablets or a claim to have inspected every cited book in full.
- Zawadzki's repository record supplies metadata and abstract only; its garment terminology and colours by deity are checkable at document level through Quillien (2019), which cites Sippar tablets (e.g. BM 64798) and Zawadzki's page-level treatments. Per-year change schedules remain `(to-verify)` pending collation of the text volume (Zawadzki 2013).
- Met 59.2 and Cleveland 1963.154 carry different Gudea dating estimates. Do not hide such variation behind an invented exact date, and do not mistake catalogue estimates for independent reign chronology.
- **Source bias:** royal inscriptions legitimate rulers; learned composites select traditions; institutional accounts make labor visible through administrative categories. None alone reconstructs every person's belief, status, or clothing.
- **Colonial collection history:** excavation, division of finds, acquisition, restoration, and modern museum categories shape the surviving sample. An accession record is an object locator, not proof of complete archaeological provenance or an attribution/rights ruling.
- **Rejected shortcuts:** nineteenth-century racial equations of “Sumerian” and “Semitic,” civilization-as-single-mind models, Anunnaki-as-aliens claims, and universal symbol dictionaries are not sound historical foundations.
- **Contested, not settled:** the dating of *Enūma Eliš*, extent of early institutional control, precise end of spoken Sumerian, and some divine/name readings require explicit models and witnesses rather than confident synthesis.
- **Thin knowledge here:** everyday lower-status wardrobes, regional pronunciation, early Sealand/Kassite cultural staging, and poorly preserved chronicle intervals need targeted specialist research. Mark a needed detail `(to-verify)` instead of supplying picturesque filler.
