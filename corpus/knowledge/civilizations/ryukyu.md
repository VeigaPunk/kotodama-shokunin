## Scope: an island network, not one cultural template

- **Established:** Ryukyuan belongs to Japonic; language genealogy does not make its languages dialects mutually intelligible with Japanese. Internal diversity is substantial (L, pp. 1–4).
- Northern Ryukyuan comprises the Amami–Okinawa grouping; Southern Ryukyuan comprises the Miyako–Yaeyama grouping, including Yonaguni. Lower-level boundaries need a named classification, not a tourism map (L, pp. 2–3).
- “Okinawan” here is not shorthand for every Ryukyuan language. Shuri is a particular court-associated reference variety; Tsuken, Amami, Miyako, and Yaeyama evidence must retain their own labels.
- Within Okinawa, D records Shuri `?aigwaamee` and Naha `?utugamaa` as corresponding respectful terms for a gentry family’s unmarried daughter. Compare lexical choices and social conditions even between neighboring urban varieties (D, entry `?aigwaamee`).
- “Continuum” is a prompt to inspect local variation, not permission to assume uninterrupted mutual comprehension across the archipelago.
- Modern administrative boundaries cut across the language region: Amami is in Kagoshima Prefecture, while Okinawa, Miyako, and Yaeyama are in Okinawa Prefecture (L, p. 1).
- **Calibration:** distinguish established documentation, localized ethnography, debated reconstruction, and design analogy. A coupling proposed for fiction belongs to the last category until lore-shokunin accepts it.

## Era staging: select a slice before combining systems

| Stage | Attested historical anchor | Coupling consequence | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formation before conventional unification in 1429 | Maritime networks and multiple power centers; H challenges retrospective court narratives | Ports, ritual places, and political centers need not coincide | Do not back-project the mature Shuri bureaucracy into every early settlement |
| Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries | Court consolidation; W places the priestess hierarchy’s heyday in the sixteenth century | Royal ritual, status-sensitive speech, and overseas missions can reinforce one another | Unification of Okinawa is not instantaneous integration of every island |
| 1609–1879 | Satsuma invasion; kingdom continues Chinese tributary relations under indirect Japanese control (RH, pp. 9–10) | External dependence and multiple diplomatic presentations coexist | Neither complete independence nor immediate cultural replacement by Japan |
| 1879–1945 | End of kingdom; changes in ritual support; W identifies loss of priestess income through the 1903 land reform | Court vocabulary can outlive its institutions; office and land rights separate | Institutional rupture does not mean every local practice stops in 1879 |
| Post-1945 to 1972 | War devastation, US administration, and textile reconstruction (C) | Replanting, cooperative labor, and new markets become part of craft history | Revival is historical work, not uninterrupted survival |
| Post-1972 contexts | Kijoka designation in 1974; later heritage and tourism transformations (T; R) | Cloth, sacred sites, and language become differently managed cultural resources | A 2019 observation or 2024 exhibition is not evidence of timeless practice |

- **Design rule:** give each institution its own clock: speech change, royal succession, land reform, textile production, and heritage designation need not advance together.
- For earlier settlement and language dispersal, historical dates remain debated; linguistic divergence, migration, and archaeological occupation are different events (L, p. 2).

## Language family, structure, and writing

| Dimension | Documented pattern | Fictional-system use | Do not infer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descent | Ryukyuan and Japanese are related Japonic branches (L) | Build related languages through regular correspondences | Chinese trade makes Ryukyuan genetically Sinitic |
| Grammar | Generally verb-final, modifier-before-head, with frequent omitted arguments (L, p. 8) | Make context and shared participants do work in dialogue | Every sentence must contain a pronoun |
| Information structure | Topic/focus particles; kakari-musubi involves focus and verb-form restrictions, varying by language (L, pp. 11–12) | Distinguish assertion, contrast, and questioning structurally | One particle/ending pair works across all islands |
| Sound | Moraic organization, often pitch distinctions; glottalization and syllabic consonants vary greatly (L, pp. 4–7) | Specify length, timing, and allowable syllables before naming | A “soft tropical” sound stereotype describes the family |
| Regional counterexample | Ōgami Miyako has syllabic fricatives, including /kss/ ‘breast’ (L, p. 5) | Related languages can develop sharply different phonotactics | This is a Shuri pronunciation model |

- Keep **speech variety**, **writing system**, and **documentary register** as separate fields.
- *Omoro sōshi*: court collection of songs from Amami and Okinawa, with compilation stages in 1531, 1613, and 1623; use the kana-based textual tradition through an annotated edition, not as transparent recording of ordinary conversation (O; W).
- Compilation dates date collection, not necessarily composition of every song. Royal praise and ritual formula are genres, not neutral demographic surveys.
- The original collection was lost in the 1709 Shuri fire; reconstruction followed in 1710 from another witness. Specify manuscript/edition when making fine linguistic claims (O, §2).
- *Rekidai Hōan*: Chinese-language diplomatic records covering 1424–1867, subsequently collected and copied; written Chinese is a transregional documentary medium, not proof of everyone’s spoken language (RH, pp. 1–6).
- Distinguish literary Chinese composition from learning spoken Chinese: D’s `kwaNhwaa` 官話 records Chinese study in preparation for study abroad or official service. Exact historical pronunciation and teaching variety require separate evidence.
- D’s electronic transcription substitutes `?` for the glottal-stop symbol and uses special conventions such as capital `N`; dictionary forms below retain its notation, not a proposed modern orthography.

## Shuri court registers: deference is multidimensional

Evidence: D, especially entries `-abi=juN`, `-ganasii`, `-ganasiimee`, `-NSee]eN`, `?aNmaa`, `?ajaa`, and `ciN`.

| Relation being encoded | Attested Shuri material | What it couples |
|---|---|---|
| Speaker → listener | Frequent polite surface form `-abiiN`, under lemma `-abi=juN`, marks politeness toward the interlocutor | Interactional stance, not simply the rank of the person discussed |
| Speaker → honored referent | `-ganasii` is an honorific suffix; `-ganasiimee` adds further respect; examples include lordly and royal reference | Titles, sacred/person references, and social hierarchy |
| Verbal respect and construction | D orders reading forms `'jumiNSeeN`, `'jumimiSeeN`, `?ujumimiSeeN`, `?ujumiNSeeN` by increasing respect in that entry | Prefixes and verbal morphology interact; longer is not automatically higher |
| Socially differentiated kin vocabulary | D contrasts commoner mother `?aNmaa` with gentry mother `?ajaa` | Kinship, household identity, and status reproduction |
| Respectful material vocabulary | `ciN` ‘clothing’ has respectful counterpart `'Nsu` | Costume can be linguistically elevated before its fabric is even described |

- **Operational framework: referent × addressee × setting.** Record who speaks, to whom, about whom, in which ceremony or household, and whether the usage is quoted, sung, or conversational.
- Do not turn the table into one universal ladder of “peasant / noble / royal language.” Lexical class distinctions and listener politeness are different mechanisms.
- D documents remembered distinctions long after the kingdom ended. Exact fifteenth-century distribution of these forms is not established by D, whose data record twentieth-century Shuri usage; do not present its examples as a reconstructed court scene.
- For fiction, establish permission to use a title, a respectful verb, and a ceremonial costume independently; a diplomatic outsider may know one but misuse another.

## Maritime multilingualism: specialized brokers, not universal fluency

- **Established:** Kume-mura/Kuninda elites handled navigation, interpretation, and Chinese diplomatic drafting; these are institutional skills, not an ethnic essence (RH, p. 2).
- The 1436 letter to Siam, document **1-40-20**, names Tei Chi 鄭智 and Ryō Tokuchū 梁徳仲 as interpreters. This establishes named brokerage, not the complete spoken repertoire of either interpreter or crew (RH, pp. 7–8).
- That document links porcelain, pepper, and sappanwood to onward Chinese trade. Cloth, dyestuffs, and diplomatic language can therefore be studied in the same route network without inventing a single shared tongue.

| Surface | Documented basis | Design variable |
|---|---|---|
| Court ↔ Kume | Chinese drafts sent to Shuri; copies filed for reuse (RH, p. 6) | Who can authorize, compose, interpret, seal, and archive? |
| Ship ↔ destination officials | Interpreters and travel authorizations occur in RH | Where does a broker change code, and who checks the broker? |
| Port ↔ household | Naha hosts representatives of multiple origins in RH’s account | Separate public transaction competence from home-language fluency |
| Cloth ↔ import network | X records imported colorants; RH lists sappanwood among traded goods | Separate local fiber, imported dye, imported pattern, and garment ownership |

- Treat written Chinese and spoken Chinese competences separately; neither entails command of every Chinese regional variety.
- Japanese connections and later Satsuma domination belong in the model alongside Chinese relations, not as an either/or identity test.
- Claims of one kingdom-wide port pidgin, the exact languages spoken on a particular wharf, or universal bilingualism are not established by the sources consulted here; RH describes specialist Kume brokers and Chinese as the medium of official regional communication, not general fluency.
- “Thirty-six families” is a transmitted Kume founding designation; do not use it as an independently verified census or exact account of a single migration event without specialist checking.

## Mythic core: distributed sacred relations, not a fixed deity roster

| Locus/concept | Attested association | Coupling to track | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Amamikiyo / Amamikyo | Origin figure in royal sacred geography; linked with prominent utaki (R, §§2–3) | Origin narrative, particular places, and dynastic legitimacy | Do not replace with Amaterasu or claim one uniform island-wide account |
| Nirai kanai; obotsu kagura | Otherworld beyond the sea; otherworld in the skies in W’s discussion of sacred power | Blessing, navigation, and the direction from which power arrives | Not a ready-made heaven/hell opposition |
| Hearth deity, W’s *fii nu kan* | Household protection and family rites; the deity’s gender varies locally (W, p. 343) | Household continuity, senior women, marriage, and death | Do not freeze one village’s arrangement into a universal pantheon |
| Ancestors and kin groups | Domestic and tomb rituals; historical changes in kin organization (W; R) | Genealogy, residence, obligation, and memorial practice | Patrilineal organization is not an unchanged prehistoric constant |
| Utaki and ibi | Sacred groves/sites and their ritual centers (R, §1) | Access, locality, prayer, and authority over space | Neither generic public park nor automatically a Shinto shrine |

- **Onarigami:** especially sisters’ spiritual protection of brothers, with local substitutions and wider female-kin roles; blessing and curse are both attested in the narratives W discusses (pp. 346–350).
- **Seji/shiji:** sacred efficacy/power in this scholarship; track whose power, how transmitted, and for whose benefit rather than translating it automatically as free-floating “mana.”
- Royal mythology, household religion, Buddhist institutions, and later heritage narratives need not form one internally standardized theological textbook.

## Noro system: office, kinship, and changing jurisdiction

- In the consolidated kingdom, a royal female relative headed the hierarchy: **kikoe-ōgimi**, rendered *chifijin* in W. She was not invariably the reigning king’s sister (W, pp. 341–342; R, §3).
- W links consolidation to Shō Shin’s reign, beginning in 1477; the existence of local religious specialists does not establish an equally old centralized bureaucracy (W, p. 352).
- Noro/nuru held community/state ritual roles; eligibility could depend on particular lineages. Some positions passed matrilineally, but this was not a universal inheritance rule (W, pp. 342–343).
- Regional organizations differed: W records *chikasa* for village priestesses in Miyako/Yaeyama. Do not simply rename every local specialist “noro.”
- Stipends, land-derived support, appointments, and relations with officials matter as much as sacred charisma. Their loss or redirection changes how an office can continue (W, pp. 342, 350–351).
- **Noro versus yuta:** community/office-linked ritual service is analytically distinct from client-oriented spirit-medium practice; trance is not a defining requirement of every priestess (W, p. 344).
- Contemporary *kaminchu*, *noro*, and *yuta* labels can overlap or be strategically chosen. R explicitly warns against treating them as protected, perfectly bounded professional titles (§3).
- Historical male exclusions at many utaki are documented; present restrictions vary by site. Modern heritage access is not evidence that earlier ritual space was public (R, §§1–4).
- **Design extraction:** connect ritual jurisdiction to descent, residence, resources, and appointment; do not infer general matriarchy or gender equality from female ritual authority.

## Symbolic system: identify the bearer, audience, and occasion

| Symbolic vehicle | Documented reading | Coupled use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| 1458 *Bankoku shinryō* bell | Court inscription presents Ryukyu as a maritime bridge among neighboring regions (RH, p. 1) | Script, political self-presentation, shipping, and prestige; not proof of an invariably peaceful polity |
| Woven *tisaji* | W discusses cloth given by sisters as protection for traveling brothers, and related narrative traditions (p. 347); X distinguishes protective cloth-gift contexts | Weaving labor, kinship, voyage risk, and efficacy; the cited passage does **not** identify all such cloth as bashōfu |
| Restricted grove | Access and ritual action distinguish sacred space from ordinary land (R) | A boundary can signify more than monumental architecture |
| Fine or elaborately processed cloth | Court and everyday bashōfu examples coexist (T; X) | Fiber grade, labor, finish, and authorized use can mark rank; “banana fiber = low status” fails |

- A motif needs an attested local interpretation; a modern guide’s explanation is not automatically a medieval codebook.
- X records uncertainty about when the famous five/four minsa pattern developed. Do not project the present “forever” reading into every era or every Ryukyuan textile.
- Compare **symbol as protective gift**, **symbol as court claim**, and **symbol as heritage brand**; the same material need not mean the same thing in each economy.

## Bashōfu and costume: fiber is not a whole dress system

- **Material:** bashōfu 芭蕉布 is cloth from cultivated fiber banana, *ito-bashō*. Do not describe it as woven edible fruit or as a synonym for all Okinawan cloth (T; C).
- **Production chain:** cultivate plants → harvest and prepare fibers → split and join yarn → dye/resist where applicable → weave → make and maintain garments (C).
- C describes **u-umi**, fine splitting and joining with small knots: yarn preparation is skilled labor, not an interchangeable “spin cotton” step.
- **Nigashi:** alkali processing softens/refines yarn and facilitates bright dyeing; court-associated precedents and modern revival are both reported (C). Do not assign every surviving vivid piece a royal date.
- T explicitly includes wear across age, gender, and status, plus court robes and ritual garments. X distinguishes finer inner fibers for elite cloth; prestige depends on workmanship and context, not fiber name alone.
- Kijoka in northern Okinawa is a major postwar revival center, not the sole historical production site. T emphasizes women’s collective transmission; C discusses Taira Toshiko’s organizing and innovation.
- Distinguish the **1974 collective designation** of Kijoka bashōfu from **Taira’s individual designation in 2000** (T; C).

| Term | What kind of category? | Contrast to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Bashōfu | Fiber/material-based textile | Does not specify one silhouette, rank, or pattern |
| Kasuri / ikat | Yarn-resist patterning before weaving | Not the same process as surface stencil dyeing |
| Bingata | Dyeing tradition, notably paste-resist stencil work, with other methods also recorded | Can occur on different cloth bases; not itself a fiber |
| Miyako/Yaeyama jōfu | Regional fine textiles using ramie in X’s account | Do not relabel them banana cloth because the islands share political history |
| Kumejima tsumugi | Regional silk textile in X’s account | Material supply and finishing differ from bashōfu |

- **Costume specification:** record fiber, yarn grade, weave, coloring process, garment construction, wearer, occasion, island, and date separately.
- A shared material can span everyday wear, office, ritual, and gifts; a change in setting may require another register and title without any change of fiber.
- Textile taxation and compelled labor, especially in Miyako/Yaeyama accounts, must not be aestheticized as merely an efficient way to produce beautiful patterns (X; W).

## Naming practices: one person can occupy several naming systems

Evidence: D, entries `naa`, `'warabinaa`, `nanui`, and `karana`; these are socially differentiated historical records, not instructions for naming contemporary Okinawans.

| Layer | Documented function | Application constraint |
|---|---|---|
| *Warabi-naa*, D’s `'warabinaa` | Childhood-name layer, contrasted with adult formal names | Do not assume every class receives the same later naming layers |
| *Nanui* / Japanese *nanori* | Adult personal name for men of gentry/noble status in D | Age, gender, and status affect access to the category |
| *Kara-naa*, D’s `karana` | Chinese-style name possessed by nobles/gentry | It is not evidence that the bearer’s home language was Chinese |
| Title and honorific | Titles such as *aji* can take honorific reference forms such as `?aziganasii` | Office/title, personal name, and respectful address are not interchangeable |

- D’s concrete cross-reference: **Haneji Chōshū** 羽地朝秀 has Chinese-style name **Shō Shōken** 向象賢. Treat them as linked forms for one person, not two historical agents.
- D’s `naa` records different name forms by status and gender. Do not extract a list and randomly attach aristocratic prefixes to every villager.
- **Fictional naming schema:** persistent person identifier → local/family name → adult/formal name if applicable → external diplomatic form → current title; annotate script, pronunciation, authorized user, and date.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **A prestige center without linguistic uniformity:** a court-associated variety, locally divergent languages, and specialist written codes can coexist without universal comprehension.
- **A broker-centered maritime polity:** make language learning, interpreters, drafting, seals, and document reuse consequential; foreign words should travel along identifiable routes and social roles.
- **A kin–voyage–cloth coupling:** weave labor, protective gifts, and obligations to traveling kin into one system; change the invented names, theology, and institutions rather than copying a living ritual.
- **A gendered but non-simplistic distribution of authority:** ritual offices, household property, civil administration, and client healing can allocate power differently and change at different times.
- **A material ladder within one fiber:** differentiate ordinary and prestigious cloth through preparation, fineness, coloring, and authorized occasions, not only imported silk versus “humble” local fiber.
- Hand lore-shokunin a **proposal**, with time/place scope, source-backed precedents, invented departures, uncertainty, and predicted consequences for language, myth, symbol, and costume. No world-bible fact is asserted here.

## Provenance cautions

- Court chronicles are late and politically interested: H specifically challenges reliance on histories composed in 1650–1750 for much earlier events. Their silences are not evidence that peripheral communities lacked history.
- Documentary survival is uneven. RH explains loss, copying errors, speculative repairs, and collation of different witnesses; the Chinese diplomatic archive also omits correspondence with Japan (pp. 1–4).
- **Chronology cross-check:** use O’s 1531/1613/1623 compilation stages; W’s passing description of *Omoro sōshi* as eighteenth-century on p. 346 is not a reliable date for its compilation.
- **Contested:** interpreting Okinawan religion as primordial Japanese Shinto, a prehistoric “living fossil,” or automatically conservationist nature worship is explicitly criticized by R. These are not neutral comparative baselines.
- **Contested:** strong readings of Susan Sered’s *Women of the Sacred Groves* (1999) as evidence for absent gender hierarchy are challenged by W and R; female sacred authority neither proves matriarchy nor cancels patriarchal institutions.
- X’s diffusion stories, lexical etymologies, pattern explanations, and sumptuary summaries differ in certainty. Check a dated object, regulation, or specialist study before assigning an exact origin or mandatory color/rank rule.
- **Thin coverage:** exact premodern Shuri speech performances, individual noro regalia and succession rules, village-specific divine names, and precise historical port-language distributions are not established by the sources consulted here. No prayer text, initiation script, or invented ancient genealogy is supplied.
- These are living traditions and changing heritage institutions. Respect site restrictions and distinguish community testimony, court prescription, ethnographic observation, and tourist interpretation; none can silently stand in for all the others.
