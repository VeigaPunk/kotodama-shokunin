## Language family and writing: separate the clocks
- Tamil is Dravidian, not an Indo-Aryan language or a derivative of Sanskrit; it belongs to the southern part of the family (Krishnamurti 2003).
- Proto-Dravidian is a comparative reconstruction; Old Tamil is an attested historical language. An asterisked reconstructed form is not something copied from a Sangam manuscript.
- Useful structural bundle: predominantly suffixing morphology, case-marked nominals, verb-final syntax, and extensive participial constructions; these are interacting tendencies, not a template imposed identically on every Dravidian language (Krishnamurti 2003).
- Phonological design handles include vowel length, dental/retroflex contrasts, and contrasts among liquids and nasals. Retroflection alone does not identify a language as Dravidian: it also participates in South Asian contact patterns (Krishnamurti 2003; Southworth 2005).
- Tamil-Brahmi adapts Brahmi to Tamil phonology; Mahadevan's corpus anchors Archaic Tamil inscriptional evidence approximately from the second century BCE through later local script developments to the sixth century CE.
- Cave donations and pottery inscriptions are different communicative surfaces; a donation formula is not evidence that ordinary conversation sounded like literary poetry (Mahadevan 2003).
- Prakrit loans and Jain-associated inscriptions already show contact in early written Tamil. Neither the language nor the cultural horizon should be modeled as a sealed, Sanskrit-free island (Mahadevan 2003).
- Writing-system ancestry does not determine language-family ancestry: Brahmi-derived scripts can write unrelated languages. Modern Tamil letter shapes must not be drawn onto a Tamil-Brahmi-era object without a dated model.
- **Design transfer:** assign separate histories to spoken varieties, prestige poetry, donor formulae, and script. Contact may change vocabulary, orthography, and patronage at different speeds.

## Dravidian evidence and the “pre-Vedic layer” question
“Pre-Vedic” is relative to a Vedic textual horizon, not a single South Asian archaeological period; Sangam poetry is much later than the earliest Vedic material.
The useful evidence concerns languages and contact before or during Indo-Aryan expansion, not proof that the Sangam social system already existed then (Krishnamurti 2003; Southworth 2005).

| Evidence class | What it can support | What it cannot establish | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regular correspondences across Dravidian languages | Shared inheritance and relative linguistic relationships; cautious reconstruction of vocabulary | A complete Proto-Dravidian pantheon, a dated Tamil kingdom, or an unchanged costume | Reconstructed; semantic histories need checking |
| Proposed Dravidian loans or structural influence in Old Indo-Aryan | Arguments for contact with Dravidian-speaking communities | That every non-Indo-European word is Dravidian, or that its donor was specifically Tamil | Individual etymologies and contact chronology contested |
| Retroflection and other areal convergence | Long-term interaction among language communities | A racial boundary or an exclusively Dravidian origin for every shared feature | Areal evidence; multiple mechanisms possible |
| Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions | Early historic written Tamil, named donors, and contact vocabulary in specific contexts | Direct evidence for a Tamil-speaking population in the Rigvedic period | Attested but chronologically later |
| Indus signs interpreted through Dravidian hypotheses | A research hypothesis to test against the full sign corpus and archaeological context | A secure decipherment, or identification of the language as Old Tamil | Contested; particular decipherment proposals `(to-verify)` |
| Sangam myths or ecology read backward into prehistory | Later testimony useful for comparison and reception history | An unbroken, independently dated pre-Vedic cultural layer | Retrospective inference, not direct attestation |

- Shared agricultural vocabulary can inform prehistory, but crop identification, semantic change, borrowing direction, and association with archaeological finds each require a separate argument (Southworth 2005).
- A northwestern contact hypothesis does not make later southern Tamil poetry a chronicle of that contact. Language, archaeological assemblage, biological ancestry, and polity are different variables.
- Reject “Tamil is the mother of every language” and visual sign-resemblance proofs as methods: comparative linguistics requires systematic correspondences, not a handful of similar words or symbols.
- **Design transfer:** build inherited vocabulary, contact loans, and later learned reinterpretations as distinct layers; let different institutions disagree about their antiquity. Mark the invented disagreement as design, not recovered Tamil history.

## Akam/puram: a social organization of speech
The core distinction is between **akam**, the interior field of love relationships, and **puṟam**, the exterior field of public action, reputation, generosity, conflict, death, and counsel (Ramanujan 1985; Hart and Heifetz 1999).

| Dimension | Akam convention | Puram convention | Coupling use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Persons | Lovers and intermediaries usually identified by roles, not personal names | Named rulers, patrons, poets, places, and public reputations are prominent | Separate intimate anonymity from public name-bearing |
| Speech situation | Lover, friend, mother, foster mother, messenger, or observer addressing someone | Praise, appeal, admonition, lament, reflection, and other public interventions | Specify who may say what, to whom, and with what risk |
| Landscape | An inventory of place/time and local life helps imply a relationship situation | Places, flowers, martial contexts, and patronage also organize public meaning | Reuse materials across genres without identical meanings |
| Evidential risk | A persona's utterance is not the poet's autobiography | Praise is interested speech, not an unbiased royal chronicle | Preserve genre before extracting history |

- This is not “women write love; men write war.” Literary voice, poet attribution, gender, and genre must be kept separate; female-attributed poets also occur in public poetry (Hart and Heifetz 1999).
- **Kaḷavu** and **kaṟpu** distinguish clandestine/premarital and established marital situations in received love-poetic analysis; translation as simple “sin versus virtue” imports an alien moral binary (Ramanujan 1985; Herbert n.d., Akam Conventions).
- The five central landscape categories do not exhaust the tradition: **kaikkiḷai** and **peruntiṇai** designate additional problematic love situations in the grammatical tradition. Their detailed application varies; exact taxonomy `(to-verify)` against a named edition.
- Puram has its own classifications, not merely the five love landscapes with battle substituted. Avoid silently collapsing the two taxonomies (Ramanujan 1985).
- Speaker/context labels attached to poems can belong to later interpretation. Record the poem body separately from its **tuṟai** or colophon and commentary (Wilden 2014; Herbert n.d., Akam Conventions).
- **Design transfer:** define genre permissions before drafting verses. A friend's indirect warning, a ruler's praise poem, and a donor inscription need different naming rules and different truth incentives.

## Tiṇai: geography–language–emotion coupling
Use the **mutal–karu–uri** framework of the *Tolkāppiyam*, *Poruḷatikāram*, *Akattiṇaiyiyal*: setting; locally appropriate beings/materials; characteristic human situation.
The threefold formula is identified as verse 3 within that section in Herbert's online presentation; global sūtra numbering and text dates vary by edition `(to-verify)`.
- **Mutal:** place and time, including season and time of day; the coordinate frame, not merely a scenery adjective.
- **Karu:** flora, fauna, livelihoods, inhabitants, deities, food, implements, and other local inventory; the culturally selected setting materials.
- **Uri / uripporuḷ:** the distinctive relational action or situation; reducing it to a universal one-word “mood” loses its social logic.
- These are received literary conventions, not five isolated peoples, five exhaustive climate zones, or a deterministic theory that landscape causes personality (Ramanujan 1985; Herbert n.d., Akam Conventions).

| Tiṇai | Mutal: place / salient time | Karu: selected local inventory | Uri: central situation | Received sacred association |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Kuṟiñci** | Mountain slopes; night, especially midnight | Millet fields, honey, springs, mountain animals; guarding crops creates meeting opportunities | Union / lovers' meeting | Cēyōṉ / Murukaṉ |
| **Mullai** | Pastoral woodland; rainy season and evening | Cattle, herding, returning paths, jasmine; return schedules become promises | Patient waiting | Māyōṉ, connected with Tirumāl/Viṣṇu |
| **Marutam** | Cultivated river plain; dawn/day | Paddy, irrigation, settlements, water birds; public proximity allows gossip and confrontation | Quarrel, especially over infidelity | Vēntaṉ, conventionally identified with Indra |
| **Neytal** | Coast; late day/evening | Sea, boats, fish, salt, shore flowers and birds; uncertain return permits anxiety | Pining / lamenting separation | Varuṇaṉ in poetic classification |
| **Pālai** | Desiccated, dangerous journey-space; hot season and midday | Scarce water, difficult paths, predators and raiders; travel for wealth or elopement | Separation / departure | Koṟṟavai in received wilderness/war associations |

- **Pālai calibration:** do not draw a permanent Sahara-like fifth province. Drying landscapes and dangerous travel are central to its literary interpretation; the elaborated explanation and deity mapping have textual/commentarial histories, not necessarily one simultaneous origin (Zvelebil 1973; Ramanujan 1985).
- The table is a convention map, not a census of exclusive divine territories. A god assigned to a landscape by poetics need not be its only worshipped deity.
- Landscape vocabularies become indexes of socially meaningful actions: millet guarding enables a tryst; evening herds make absence noticeable; roads join separation, danger, and earning wealth.
- **Uḷḷuṟai**, an implied inner comparison, lets a local scene convey a relationship indirectly; **iṟaicci** is another category of suggestion, not an automatically interchangeable synonym. Detailed distinctions `(to-verify)` against a specific commentary.
- *Kuṟuntokai* 8 juxtaposes fish taking fallen mangoes with a socially charged utterance about a lover's conduct. The analogy works through the speaker and situation, not through a universal “fish means desire” dictionary (Herbert n.d., Akam Conventions).
- *Porunarāṟṟuppaṭai* 214–221 describes exchange and cross-landscape songs/garlands: fishermen sing mountain music and mountain dwellers wear shore flowers. The text itself complicates sealed-landscape models (Herbert n.d., Porunarāṟṟuppaṭai).

## Mythic/pantheon core: preserve contact and textual layers
- **Murukaṉ/Cēyōṉ:** mountain association, youthful power, and ritual interventions in love-poetic situations; the **vēlaṉ** and **veṟiyāṭṭam** supply a ritual explanation competing with the lover's explanation of distress (Zvelebil 1973; Herbert n.d., Akam Conventions).
- The craft mechanism is competing diagnoses: family interprets bodily change ritually, a confidante recognizes a relationship. Do not flatten every mention into a detachable “war-god archetype.”
- **Māyōṉ/Tirumāl:** pastoral associations coexist with identifications and narrative connections to the wider Viṣṇu tradition. “Dark one” is an epithet, not a race category (Zvelebil 1973; Ramanujan 1985).
- **Koṟṟavai:** associated with victory and fierce divine power; a single universal mother-goddess lineage reaching unchanged into the Neolithic is not established by this association. Prehistoric continuity claims `(to-verify)`.
- **Vēntaṉ/Indra and Varuṇaṉ:** retain the distinction between received landscape assignments and the ritual testimony of an individual poem; an elegant taxonomy need not describe all lived worship.
- *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 55 evokes the three-fort destruction of the blue-throated god; poem 56 invokes Śiva, Balarāma, Tirumāl, and Murukaṉ in royal comparison. Wider Indic mythic reference is present within the corpus, not only in a much later replacement layer (Hart and Heifetz 1999, poems 55–56; Herbert n.d.).
- Conversely, anthology invocations can be later additions: do not date every Śiva description by the supposed date of the collection's earliest poems (Wilden 2014; Herbert n.d., Puṟanāṉūṟu 1).
- **Design transfer:** distinguish landscape epithet, ritual practice, courtly comparison, and later theological identification. They can share a name without having identical institutional histories.

## Symbolic system: meanings travel with contexts
| Carrier | Attested/conventional function | Safe design transfer | Overreach to reject |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local flowers and garlands | Place associations, adornment, poetic classification, and martial/patronal contexts | Give materials several genre-dependent meanings | One flower = one eternal ethnic identity |
| Bangles on a changing body | Slipping bangles can signal lovesick wasting in akam convention | Make a wearable object register absence and bodily change | Diagnose an actual historical person's health from the trope |
| White royal umbrella | Public sovereignty and protective shade in praise/ethical comparison | Make rank visible through portable ceremonial objects | Import later rules for every official's entitlement |
| Fine cloth replacing rags | Patronal generosity and the bard's changed condition | Couple textile quality to reciprocal obligations | Treat panegyric abundance as measured economic output |
| Divine flags/attributes | Mythic identities become resources for royal comparison in poem 56 | Let court language appropriate sacred signs | Read the poem as a uniform heraldic manual |

- The umbrella and common bodily needs coexist in *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 189: hierarchy is visible while the poem argues for giving wealth away (Hart and Heifetz 1999; Herbert n.d.).
- Color translations need lexical and contextual checks: a term rendered dark, bright, red, or white can concern appearance and evaluative force together. Do not infer a complete cosmological color code from an epithet.
- **Design transfer:** maintain a symbol record as `material → setting → speaker → genre → social action → later reinterpretation`; meanings may diverge between lovers, bards, ritual specialists, and rulers.

## Era model: stage evidence rather than a legendary chronology
| Layer | Working temporal frame | What belongs here | What must not be silently backdated |
|---|---|---|---|
| Reconstructed Dravidian prehistory | Relative linguistic history; absolute placement contested | Reconstructed forms, possible contact strata | Sangam institutions, named gods, garments, or script shapes |
| Early historic inscriptional horizon | Mahadevan's evidence approximately second century BCE–sixth century CE | Tamil-Brahmi and later local scripts; situated donations and pottery writing | A pan-Tamil literacy rate or Rigvedic-era Tamil attestation |
| Sangam poetic horizons | Broadly late centuries BCE / early centuries CE; boundaries and individual texts debated | Anthology poetry, bardic praise, love conventions, varied patrons | A single exact year or unbroken dynasty list for the whole corpus |
| Grammatical, anthology, and commentary layers | Different composition, compilation, and interpretation dates | *Tolkāppiyam* strata, poem ordering, contextual labels, scholastic elaboration | Treating every later category as the original poet's own explanation |
| Later reception and material cultures | Date each epic, devotional text, image, manuscript, and edition separately | New theologies, court institutions, dress conventions, and recovery narratives | Projecting later temple/court imagery onto every early poem |

- “Sangam” refers to the received literary complex; the legendary successive academies are not a securely documented event-by-event contemporary chronicle (Zvelebil 1973; Wilden 2014).
- Composition date, the date of a mentioned event, anthology formation, commentary, surviving copy, and modern print edition are separate fields. A late copy can preserve early poetry without proving every attached attribution early.
- Cēra, Cōḻa, and Pāṇḍya names provide important political reference points, but royal praise and colophons do not supply a complete synchronized regnal chronology (Hart and Heifetz 1999; Mahadevan 2003).
- Earlier dates proposed for particular archaeological writing finds require the actual excavation report: stratigraphy, association of the sample with the inscribed object, and the reading `(to-verify)`. A carbon date does not automatically date the act of lettering.
- **Design transfer:** stage change independently across language, script, literary canonization, ritual institutions, and textiles. An old poem can be reinterpreted under a new court without every inherited system changing at once.

## Sangam dress description system: an extraction grid, not a uniform
The following is a **modern analytical method** for reading poetic clothing evidence, not the name of an ancient formal costume taxonomy.
The strongest entry points are contrasts and actions: worn versus gifted cloth, ornament retained versus removed, body filling versus losing bangles, and local flowers versus costly substitutes.

| Textual anchor | Description operation | What it supports | What remains uncertain |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 189, especially line 5 | Reduces ruler and hunter to common food/clothing needs; specifies two worn items | Clothing as a rhetorical measure of bodily sufficiency | Exact cut, drape, gender distribution, or compulsory two-piece dress |
| *Porunarāṟṟuppaṭai* 74–83 | Sweat-soaked, infested, patched rags are replaced by exceptionally fine, floral cloth compared with snake skin | Textile quality and replacement enact hospitality/patronage | The simile does not prove snakeskin clothing, a fiber, or embroidery technique |
| *Neṭunalvāṭai* 136–147 | Separation changes earrings, bangles, protective threads, and the condition/type of clothing | Adornment and grooming can narrate an absent partner and distress | A queen's literary condition is not a universal widowhood or caste dress code |
| *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 125 | Cotton worked by a woman supplies a comparison for a food description | Cotton working is intelligible to the poem's audience | Scale of manufacture, universal division of labor, or a loom specification |
| Akam slipping-bangle convention | Fit changes as the lover's body wastes during separation | An accessory joins body, time, and relationship | A statistically representative medical or dietary account |

- Anchors: Hart and Heifetz (1999), poems 125 and 189; Herbert (n.d.), the named poems/line spans and Akam Conventions. Paraphrases above are not claims that the English translators agree on every lexeme.
- Material signature: fine textiles, conspicuous repair/soiling, flowers and fragrance, metal and shell adornments, pearls, and portable rank display; each needs its own situated textual anchor, not a single “Sangam outfit.”
- Small working lexicon: **aṟuvai** denotes clothing/cloth in *Porunarāṟṟuppaṭai* 83; **tukil** and **kaliṅkam** occur in *Neṭunalvāṭai* 145–146; **toṭi** and **vaḷai** enter its arm-adornment contrast; **kaṇṇi** denotes garland/wreath in contextual descriptions (Herbert n.d.).
- “Silk” versus “cotton” in translations of the queen's clothing can involve lexical/commentarial interpretation. Fiber assignment and the precise rendering of these terms require comparison with the Tamil and a named scholarly commentary `(to-verify)`.
- A cloth term is not automatically a garment pattern or an origin certificate: do not infer a Kalinga import solely because a translator renders **kaliṅkam** as a textile.
- Patching supports knowledge of sewing/mending, not a universal tailored blouse; lack of a seam description does not prove tailoring absent. Neither “everyone wore modern saris” nor “nobody wore upper cloth” follows from these passages.

**Extraction fields for a usable costume brief:**
1. `surface/lexeme`: retain the Tamil word, poem number, line, translation, and commentary separately.
2. `physical description`: fineness, sheen, color, floral appearance, thickness, knots, repairs, dirt, moisture; mark which qualities are explicit.
3. `body and attachment`: where it is worn, how it moves, and whether fit is described; missing cuts and fastenings stay unknown.
4. `adornment`: shell/metal/pearl/flower, worn location, retention/removal, fragrance, and relation to the surrounding scene.
5. `occasion and relation`: court audience, journey, work, waiting, ritual, war; giver, wearer, observer, and intended audience.
6. `poetic job`: praise, pity, desire, rank, generosity, bodily change, or comparison; do not turn a rhetorical function into a law.
7. `historical layer`: poem body, later colophon/commentary, modern translation, archaeological parallel, or declared design inference.
8. `unknowns`: fiber, weave, dye, seam, drape, entitlement, and prevalence; attach `(to-verify)` to any proposed historical completion.

## Naming practices: attribution is not a modern name generator
- Akam role-anonymity and puram public naming are genre choices; an anonymous lover is not evidence that a society lacked personal names (Ramanujan 1985).
- Poet attributions can combine personal element, locality, kinship, honorific endings, occupation-associated designations, or an epithet; attribution history needs its own confidence field (Hart and Heifetz 1999; Wilden 2014).
- The attribution to *Puṟanāṉūṟu* 189 contains Madurai + Kanakkāyaṉār's son (**makaṉār**) + Nakkīraṉār. This is a specific place/kinship/name construction, not a compulsory surname formula (Herbert n.d.).
- Treat **-ār** as an honorific/plural formation in relevant usage, not a caste surname detached from its language; exact segmentation of any name `(to-verify)` against its attested form.
- Dynastic designations, regnal epithets, personal names, and later identifiers recording where a ruler died should remain separate database fields. A long colophon label need not be the name spoken during that ruler's life.
- **Design transfer:** build contextual name registers: inscriptional donor identity, intimate role-reference, bardic epithet, public ruler title, and retrospective chronicle label. Do not recombine sacred names and historical caste/community terms indiscriminately.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **A generative grammar of setting:** `place + season + livelihood + speaking role + relationship situation`; choose the social mechanism connecting them, not merely a flower palette.
- **Indirect speech as cultural technique:** a local animal, crop, or weather scene can let a confidante warn without an explicit accusation; make the implied meaning recoverable from the interaction.
- **Several truth registers:** public praise, intimate utterance, ritual diagnosis, donor formula, and later commentary may describe the same event differently without being interchangeable evidence.
- **Dress as a verb:** giving, replacing, removing, repairing, loosening, wetting, or withholding an ornament can carry more world information than listing garments.
- **An era with uneven change:** inherited morphology, borrowed cult titles, conservative verse, a newly adapted script, and changing luxury fabrics can coexist; date them separately.
- **A non-canon handoff to lore-shokunin:** supply a proposed coupling table, source anchors, confidence labels, alternative interpretations, and missing material details. Lore decides whether any proposal enters the world bible.

## Provenance cautions and thin areas
- **Thin here:** regional archaeological textile survival, dye identification, exact weaving technology, lower-status everyday dress, local ritual variation, and the chronology of individual *Tolkāppiyam* strata. Do not fill these with attractive illustrations or reconstructed certainty; targeted specialist work is `(to-verify)`.
- **Chronicle evidence is scarce:** poems, inscriptions, archaeology, and later literary histories complement one another but do not constitute a continuous neutral narrative of every early Tamil dynasty or community.
- **Colonial-source calibration:** older philology can use racial “Aryan/Dravidian,” “primitive,” or evolutionary-stage categories. Retain checkable textual observations, reject language-family-as-race reasoning, and test developmental stories independently (Krishnamurti 2003; Southworth 2005).
- **Modern political/purity calibration:** neither a contact-free primordial Tamil civilization nor the reduction of Tamil tradition to Sanskrit derivation is an adequate model. The inscriptional and poetic evidence includes both distinct language history and interaction.
- **Ecological calibration:** tiṇai offers a powerful comparison framework, not proof of timeless ecological harmony, five biological peoples, or five successive stages of civilization.
- **Edition calibration:** an online gloss may resolve a difficult word through a later commentator. Cite the commentator/edition before using that gloss to specify fiber, dress construction, marriage marks, or religious chronology `(to-verify)`.
- **Reuse calibration:** modern translations remain authored works; use brief attributed quotations only when needed and prefer new analytical paraphrase. Check rights before reproducing translations or illustrations in a distributed world bible.
- Keep method, attested reference, contested reconstruction, and fictional choice visibly separate; this entry authorizes none of the latter as canon.
