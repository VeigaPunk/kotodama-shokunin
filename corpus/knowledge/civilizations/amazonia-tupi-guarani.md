## Scope and calibration

- **Not one civilization:** Tupi–Guarani is a linguistic grouping within the Tupian/Tupi stock, not a single polity, religion, costume, or ethnic identity.
- **Geographic correction:** coastal Tupinambá and the Guarani worlds of the Paraná–Paraguay basins and Atlantic Forest are not interchangeable with Amazon-basin societies. This entry uses an Amazonian comparison without relocating its other cases into Amazonia.
- Keep four evidence labels: **CT** = sixteenth-century coastal Tupinambá; **MG** = colonial mission Guarani; **MB** = particular Mbya communities/texts; **EA** = separately identified eastern Amazonian language communities.
- ISA's Ñandeva profile also discusses Kaiowá; a statement on that page is not automatically specific to Ñandeva. Modern Paraguayan Guarani is not a substitute for MB, MG, or CT.
- **Attested** below means supported for the named source/community, not universal. **Design inference** identifies a transferable construction method, not a claim about Indigenous history.
- **Contested:** reconstructing a timeless Tupi–Guarani religion by combining colonial chronicles and much later ethnography. Pompa (2004) directly critiques this evidentiary shortcut.
- **Thin coverage:** exact CT ritual utterances, fine-grained local coastal register variation, and period-specific wardrobe distributions. Do not fill those gaps with MB chants, generic Amazonian imagery, or invented translations.

## Language family, writing, and historical separation

| Layer | What is warranted | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| CT / coastal Old Tupi documentation | Related coastal varieties are represented through grammars, colloquies, and religious texts; “Old Tupi” is a scholarly cover label, not evidence that every village spoke identically | Assign each fictional textual witness a place, date, speaker, and recorder |
| MG / mission Guarani | Guarani became a privileged mission medium within previously and persistently multilingual settings; grammatical codification helped create a standard | Standardization is an institution-building process, not discovery of a pre-existing homogeneous nation |
| MB / living Guarani varieties | Mbya, Ñandeva, and Kaiowá differ linguistically and religiously; names and classifications themselves require local context | Choose a reference variety before selecting phonology, ritual terminology, or names |
| EA / Amazonian comparison | Araweté, Asurini, Ka'apor, Tembé, and Wayãpi have comparable but nonidentical plant vocabularies | Related languages may conserve crop terms while innovating forest names |
| Línguas gerais / Nheengatu | Portuguese-colonial supra-local language histories and present-day Nheengatu must be distinguished from mission Guarani and sixteenth-century coastal texts | A trade/mission lingua franca can acquire its own history; do not treat Nheengatu as unchanged CT |

- **Writing:** alphabetic documentation used Latin-derived orthographies introduced through colonial encounters; missionaries recorded and reshaped languages that already had grammar. They did not “give grammar” to speech (Anchieta; Boidin).
- Do not call body designs or feather arrangements an alphabet without evidence of systematic language encoding. Symbolic communication and glottographic writing are different analytical categories.
- Historical spellings, modern community orthographies, and Portuguese loanword spellings are not interchangeable. Preserve source forms in quotations; label any normalization.
- **Named framework — grammatisation and territorialisation (Boidin 2020):** grammars, dictionaries, and repeated textual practice help select and spread a privileged variety while organizing a mission's social space; description is also an intervention in language politics.
- **Design inference:** build separate rules for spoken inheritance, scribal spelling, and contact vocabulary; a missionary spelling reform need not represent a sound change.

## CT: coastal registers as encounter domains

| Encounter domain | Source anchor / confidence | What to model rather than copy |
|---|---|---|
| Arrival and reception | Léry XVIII describes lamentation and welcoming speeches; attested through a European narrator | A welcome can perform kinship/history and authorize hospitality, not merely exchange greetings |
| Negotiation with outsiders | Léry XX stages coastal-language/French conversation; textual witness, not a recording | Interpreters select equivalent terms and suppress distinctions; preserve misunderstanding as a mechanism |
| Captivity, warfare, remembered enemies | Léry XV; highly charged narrative setting | Speech can establish persons and obligations through remembered relations; do not reduce all social life to warfare |
| Religious performance | Léry XVI discusses figures he calls *caraibes* in openly polemical language | Separate a performer's authority, audience response, and an observer's accusation of deception |
| Catechetical language | Anchieta 1595 and the mission-text history discussed by Boidin | Translation creates a new register with prescribed meanings; it does not simply transcribe an older one |

- These are analytical register domains, not a recovered Indigenous list of named speech styles. Exact CT pronunciation, formula inventories, and speaker-specific restrictions remain (to-verify).
- Léry's colloquy can support a design about translation scenes; it cannot by itself establish normal conversation frequency or a village-wide grammar of politeness.
- **Coupling move:** record `{occasion, speaker-role, addressee, genre, medium, claimed authority, material setting}` before inventing any high/low-language contrast.

## MG: redução bilingualism without a false two-language binary

- *Redução* / Spanish *reducción* names a colonial mission-settlement institution, not an Indigenous evolutionary stage. Indigenous participation included coercion, negotiation, protection-seeking, and resistance; conditions varied (Boidin; Neumann).
- Use **asymmetric multilingualism plus uneven literacy**, not “everyone bilingual in Spanish and Guarani.” Guarani could dominate local communication while selected intermediaries handled Spanish; other Indigenous languages and Latin also belonged to the wider setting.

| Channel | Historical mechanism | Fictional design variable |
|---|---|---|
| Guarani catechism, sermon, prayer | Repeated translation and performance stabilized Christian senses | Which old words acquire official new referents? |
| Spanish–Guarani mediation | Letters and oral translations crossed colonial authority boundaries | Who can inspect the original, and who hears only an authorized paraphrase? |
| Guarani municipal writing | Indigenous officeholders wrote correspondence and administrative records | Literacy may increase a local elite's independent bargaining power |
| Latin religious/textual channel | Part of the clerical and learned environment, not evidence of general community fluency | Keep liturgical prestige distinct from everyday comprehension |
| Multilingual Indigenous arrivals | A privileged Guarani medium could also marginalize other Indigenous languages | A retained Indigenous language can itself become a standardizing power |

- **Concrete semantic change:** Boidin documents *Tupã* as Christian “God,” *Tupã óga* as “church,” and *ñemombe'u* redirected toward “confession”; these are historically situated Christian-register correspondences, not timeless dictionary identities.
- **Agency check:** Neumann's evidence includes Guarani and Spanish writing, sometimes bilingual, by Indigenous authors; political letters were not merely clerical copies. Literacy remained concentrated, especially among administrative men.
- Nicolás Yapuguay's Guarani *Explicacion del catecismo* (1724) and *Sermones y exemplos* (1727), discussed by Boidin, make Indigenous authorship concrete without erasing Jesuit supervision.
- Mission-era printing and post-expulsion correspondence are different stages. Do not make the Jesuits' departure in 1767–1768 an automatic end to Guarani literacy.
- **Design inference:** connect the new register to buildings, gestures, offices, paper access, and textiles supplied through institutions; do not model contact as loanwords alone.

## Pajé, beautiful speech, and mythic authority

- **Terminology firewall:** Portuguese *pajé* is not a universal job title or a sufficient description of every Tupi–Guarani ritual specialist. Do not equate CT *caraibe*, MB leadership terms, and the divine name Karaí solely by resemblance.
- CT ritual speech survives largely through interested observers. MB *ayvu porã* / beautiful speech and Cadogan's restricted *ñe'ẽ porã tenonde* material are much better evidenced in their own settings, not substitutes for missing coastal transcripts.
- **MB language–person coupling:** Cadogan II links the foundation of human speech, sacred song, divine participation, and relations among beings; IV links incoming word-souls, infancy, and naming. “Word,” “soul,” and “name” are translation problems, not three universally interchangeable English concepts.
- ISA's MB “Language” section distinguishes elaborate ritual vocabulary from ordinary speech; “House of prayer” links song, dance, speeches, and immediate community concerns. Sacred register can interpret a present crisis rather than merely recite distant origins.

| MB case / source location | Mythic or performative core | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Ñamandu, Cadogan II | Foundation of speech and other divine parents precedes the terrestrial order | A particular recorded Mbya theology, not a family-wide supreme-god diagram |
| Karaí, Jakaira, Tupã and their consorts, II–III | Relations among flame, vivifying mist, rain/cooling, and word-soul parentage | Keep relations and paired figures; “fire god / water god” alone discards the word/person system |
| First earth, destruction, renewed earth, III, VI–VII | The present world has a prior world and transformation history | Do not align a mythic destruction with an archaeological disaster without independent evidence |
| Solar/lunar narratives, VIII | Pa'i Rete Kuaray and a younger lunar companion; the recorded MB account complicates the label “twins” | Even a familiar comparative motif may have locally incompatible genealogies |
| Ritual leadership, ISA MB | Speech, prayer-house practice, and interpretation of events; women can also exercise spiritual leadership | Avoid defaulting to an exclusively male “shaman caste” |

- **Contested framework:** “Land without evil” and pan-family prophetism have a major scholarly history, not a single uncontested explanation for migrations. Pompa warns against forced colonial/ethnographic continuity; land loss, kin relations, and political histories cannot be replaced by one mythic motive.
- **Design inference:** distinguish everyday reference, restricted sacred terminology, and a colonial devotional register; specify who learns each, who may hear it, and how authority is challenged.
- Do not copy restricted chants, healing instructions, or sacred names as a fictional spell list merely because a compiler published them.

## Symbolic system: relations before an emblem dictionary

| Attested relationship | Systems coupled | Safe analytical extraction |
|---|---|---|
| MB *jeguaka* / feather head adornment and religious *jeguakáva*, Cadogan “Al lector” | Bodily sign ↔ religious designation of male humanity | An object term can become a person-category in a particular register; not every head ornament means “chief” |
| MB smoke/mist and word-soul naming, Cadogan III–IV | Material action ↔ cosmology ↔ personhood ↔ specialist speech | Map what a substance does in a narrated/ritual relation, not a universal “smoke = spirit” equation |
| MB maize blessing, naming, and seasonal renewal, ISA “House of prayer” | Crop calendar ↔ birth cohort ↔ ceremonial speech ↔ settlement time | Food technology, religion, and naming can share a calendar; manioc need not monopolize the sacred crop role |
| MG *Tupã* and Christian buildings/practices, Boidin | Divine vocabulary ↔ architecture ↔ political era | A word's apparent continuity can conceal a change of referent and authorized practice |

- Body paint, feathers, smoke, and plants do not constitute a single pan-Amazonian symbolic code. Species, maker, occasion, wearer, and history matter more than a generic color/archetype list.
- **Design inference:** a symbol record should distinguish `{material referent, register, user, occasion, associated narrative, authorized interpretation, competing interpretation}`.

## Era model: parallel histories, not a civilization ladder

| Stage / geography | Language and authority | Material/visual staging rule |
|---|---|---|
| Before sustained colonial contact, locally dated | Later texts cannot directly inventory earlier registers | Archaeological objects alone cannot identify an ethnic language, deity, or exact costume rule |
| CT encounter record, sixteenth century; Léry printed 1578 | Interpreters, hospitality, alliance, warfare, rival religious explanations | Keep locality and observer visible; do not import eighteenth-century mission uniforms |
| MG reductions, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries | Guarani codification, Christian registers, selected literate offices | Churches, workshops, paper, and regulated routines belong to a specific colonial assemblage |
| Portuguese Amazonian lingua-geral trajectories | Supra-local language formation and later Nheengatu history | A separate contact route, not the northern stage of a Guarani mission chronology |
| MG post-expulsion, 1767–1768 onward | Indigenous correspondence and administrative change continue | Reassignment of offices/resources can change a garment's political meaning without changing its material |
| MB and other Guarani histories, twentieth–twenty-first centuries | Different communities maintain and remake speech, ritual, and territorial relations | Purchased clothing and contemporary institutions do not invalidate Indigenous identity |

- **Design inference:** stage local historical time, seasonal time, life-cycle time, and mythic-world time separately; then record where calendars and authorities intersect.
- Do not convert “oral → mission writing” into “primitive → advanced,” or portray surviving peoples as remnants outside history.

## Manioc technology vocabulary: build the process, not a food-name list

- Léry IX is a CT witness for processed roots and fermented drink; ISA's Ñandeva/Kaiowá productive-system discussion supplies modern Guarani evidence. Neither licenses a universal Tupi–Guarani recipe.
- Cassava/manioc processing addresses cyanogenic compounds through process-specific combinations of operations (Montagnac et al.). “Sweet/bitter” is not a reliable instruction that one category is always safe raw; this is reference structure, not preparation or safety guidance.

| Lexical/process node | Attested label or comparison prompt | Word–world coupling to design |
|---|---|---|
| Crop, root, named cultivar, planting stock | *mandi'o* in ISA's modern Guarani profile; *mandioca* in Brazilian Portuguese | Distinguish crop kind, cultivar name, edible part, and vegetative planting material |
| Peeling, grating, mashing | Process vocabulary to elicit from a chosen community; historical headwords (to-verify) | Derive tool, operator, processed mass, and completion/result terms systematically |
| Pressing and strained liquid | Regional Brazilian *tipiti* as a comparative device label; attribution to a chosen CT/MG/MB case (to-verify) | A press requires fiber knowledge, repair vocabulary, work space, and safe handling distinctions |
| Starch separation | Modern Brazilian *tapioca* can designate starch and prepared foods; exact historical form/date (to-verify) | Distinguish suspension, settled starch, pulp, and finished food rather than one English “flour” |
| Dry meal | Portuguese *farinha* is a Portuguese lexical label, not an Indigenous headword by default | Track drying/roasting, texture, storage, portability, and trade units separately |
| Flatcake | Brazilian *beiju*; *mbeju* in Cadogan VIII is glossed as maize cakes | Similar food names do not prove the same substrate or recipe across communities |
| Fermented drink | Léry's *caouin*, often rendered *cauim* in Brazilian usage | Record substrate, vessel, preparer, occasion, sharing obligations; not an all-purpose “manioc beer” |

- **Costume/material bridge, design inference:** plant-fiber baskets and presses, pottery, heat, washing, and work schedules create vocabulary for handling, wear, maintenance, and bodily technique—not just nouns for ingredients.
- Do not transfer every modern Amazonian culinary term or artifact into sixteenth-century coastal scenes. A Brazilian Portuguese borrowing is evidence of contact history, not proof of a reconstructed ancestral form.
- **Design exercise:** for a fictional staple, supply cultivar distinctions, an operational verb family, intermediate products, tools, storage categories, and hospitality rules; mark every invented form as invented, not “authentic Tupi.”

## Forest nomenclature depth: ecology plus linguistic history

- **MG evidence:** Noelli (1998), working from Montoya, identifies differentiation among grassland, wetland, forest communities, and transitional zones. “Forest vocabulary” therefore includes landscape mosaics, not only tree species.
- **EA comparison:** Balée and Moore (1991) find greater cross-language similarity in names for more intensively managed plants, interacting with literal versus metaphorical/descriptive naming. This is a dataset result, not a law that all crop words are ancient or all wild-plant names unstable.
- Their comparison concerns Araweté, Asurini, Ka'apor, Tembé, and Wayãpi. Do not silently relabel their plant names as Tupinambá or Guarani; genealogy, contact, and local ecology need separate evidence.
- Avoid the cliché “hundreds of words for trees” without a counted, identified lexicon. A vernacular category need not map one-to-one to a scientific species, and a descriptive compound is not automatically a separate taxonomic rank.

| Design-inference axis | Ask of the lexicon | Coupling payoff |
|---|---|---|
| Habitat and vegetation community | Is a patch named by moisture, dominant growth, management, or history? | Toponyms and travel directions encode inhabited landscapes |
| Managed versus less-managed plants | Are crop/encouraged-plant names conserved across related languages? | Diachrony can diverge by semantic field without random synonym generation |
| Life stage and usable part | Which distinctions concern bark, fiber, sap, fruit, timber, seedlings? | Basketry, pigment, medicine, tools, and clothing share material vocabulary |
| Literal versus descriptive names | Which are inherited labels and which remain interpretable compounds? | Folk explanation and etymological truth can disagree |
| Expertise and access | What would gatherers, cultivators, ritual specialists, or outsiders know differently? | Nomenclature depth belongs to a distribution of expertise, not an omniscient population |
| Ritual versus practical reference | Does a source actually attest a special designation? | Do not invent taboo avoidance and then present it as ethnographic fact |

- **Working record:** `{community, language/orthography, source/date, referent, local category, ecological setting, usable parts, management, practical term, ritual term if attested, uncertainty}`.
- **Falsification check:** removing the English gloss should not collapse every distinction into “tree”; adding Latin binomials should not erase distinctions the source speakers make.

| Case / evidence | Signature worth studying | Anti-flattening rule |
|---|---|---|
| CT, Léry VIII | Featherwork, bodily ornamentation, painting, and lip ornaments in a coastal encounter account | Sparse sewn clothing is not absence of material sophistication; exact local rank/age assignments (to-verify) |

| MB, Cadogan introduction and ritual texts | Feather head adornment can also be a religious person-designation; material and vocabulary reinforce one another | The register-specific meaning is not a universal dress code or a crown |
| MG, Boidin's mission assemblage | Workshops, Christian architecture, ritual gestures, and new offices form an institutional material context | Exact garment cuts, cloth allotments, and office insignia require dated inventories/images (to-verify) |

- **Design inference:** make a material chain for each display object: organism/material → harvesting/production → skilled maker → transfer → wear occasion → repair/disposal → relevant speech.
- Neither a generic feather bonnet nor an undated “tribal outfit” is a substitute for that chain. Coastal tropical scenes, subtropical Guarani seasons, and mission schedules need different constraints.

## Naming practices: persons, institutions, places

- **MB sacred naming:** Cadogan IV describes a name learned through a specialist's relationship with divine word-soul origins, not an arbitrary epithet chosen from an animal list. ISA connects naming and crop blessing to seasonal renewal.
- Cadogan's Spanish “patronímicos sagrados” must not be automatically read as inherited European-style family surnames; determine what relation and transmission the particular passage describes.
- **CT gap:** exact rules for name acquisition/change and their relations to warfare need passage-level checking before reuse; detailed onomastic reconstruction here is thin (Léry XV; specific rules are not established here) (to-verify).
- **MG layer:** Boidin's Indigenous correspondents bear names and colonial offices within Catholic letter conventions. A baptismal/administrative identifier and a ritual identifier need not answer the same social question; any specific coexistence rule is (to-verify).
- **Places:** ISA's historical discussion notes village identification through rivers or leaders; vegetation-community vocabulary supplies another investigable naming field, not automatic etymologies for existing Brazilian place names.
- **Design inference:** separate `{self-designation, outsider label, personal everyday name, ritual name if any, documentary name, office/title, place-name}` and specify who can use each. Do not compose “Tupi names” by freely mixing Guarani, Nheengatu, and Portuguese dictionary fragments.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **A coupling engine, not a canon package:** choose one reference case per system; label every cross-case combination as a fictional transformation rather than historical reconstruction.
- **Registers with institutions:** speech authority depends on occasion, audience, material setting, and access—not simply an ornate sacred vocabulary.
- **Contact without replacement:** a local-language Christian and political register can expand while its users resist colonial authorities in that same language.
- **Technology as semantics:** a staple-processing chain generates verbs, intermediate substances, tools, skill categories, obligations, and ritual occasions together.
- **Ecology as diachrony:** management, naming structure, and language history jointly shape lexical similarity; create patterned inheritance rather than random forest-word abundance.
- **Display as social action:** connect material production and bodily signs to actual roles, narratives, and periods; never assume “more feathers = higher rank.”
- **Handoff to lore-shokunin:** deliver proposed rules, alternate implementations, source/community/era labels, uncertainty, and contradictions. Lore alone selects and records fictional canon.

## Provenance cautions and recheck triggers

- Checked 2026-10-17: catalogue identities, accessible chapters/extracts, journal abstracts, and ISA dossiers. Bibliographic verification is not full-text or archival verification; access limits are named in the source ledger.
- Colonial religious classifications, staged dialogue, captivity narratives, and illustrations serve European rhetorical purposes; compare witnesses and Indigenous-authored documents rather than accepting or dismissing chronicles wholesale.
- Cadogan preserves named Indigenous intellectual work but also uses period language of racial essence, “primitive” thought, and religious “purity”; those are not this entry's premises. His published access does not establish contemporary community permission to reproduce restricted material.
- **Discredited shortcuts:** “Jesuits gave these languages grammar,” “all Amazonian peoples share one shamanic religion,” and lexical poverty as proof of intellectual poverty are not usable frameworks. The scope of historical prophetism is contested, not simply equivalent to those errors.
- Recheck exact historical headwords, CT ritual formulas, early attestations of regional food/device words, garment distributions, and contested pagination before treating any `(to-verify)` item as established.
- Thin knowledge remains about community-specific present-day restrictions and detailed CT onomastics/register phonetics. Seek identified community voices and precise editions; do not manufacture a fluent chant, a pantheon genealogy, or an archaeological date to cover the gap.
