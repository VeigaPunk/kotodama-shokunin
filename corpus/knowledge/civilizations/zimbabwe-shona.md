## Scope and calibration key
- Great Zimbabwe is an archaeological place and historical political center; Shona is a present-day linguistic-cultural umbrella, not the name of an unchanged medieval nation.
- African construction of Great Zimbabwe is established, not one side of an unresolved foreign-builder debate. Phoenician, biblical, and white-civilizer origin stories are discredited colonial explanations. [Metropolitan Museum 2001]
- Distinguish **A: material attestation**, **E: situated ethnography**, **I: interpretation**, **M: documented modern development**, and **D: fictional design inference**.
- A stone object can be A while its supposed meaning is I; E from a twentieth-century village does not establish a thirteenth-century court ritual.
- Shona-speaking communities differ by locality, dialect, lineage, religious affiliation, generation, and diaspora setting. Christian and ancestral commitments need not form mutually exclusive populations.
- Verification, 2026-10-17: accessible museum, UNESCO, and MBIRA texts checked; bibliographic identities also checked through catalogue or scholarly records. ## Language family, writing, and the tonal system
| Feature | Reference baseline | Coupling constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Bantu within Niger-Congo; Shonic/S.10 is a classificatory grouping. | Do not infer one polity, religion, or costume from a language-family label. |
| Internal variation | Karanga, Zezuru, Korekore, and Manyika are important reference varieties; linguistic and political boundaries do not coincide. | Name the variety behind a word or recorded performance. |
| Neighboring identities | Ndau and Kalanga must not automatically disappear into “standard Shona.” | Distinguish linguistic relationship from speakers' self-identification. |
| Morphology | Noun classes and agreement interact with a richly inflected verb. | Design agreement across names, titles, epithets, and place descriptions, not decorative prefixes alone. |
| Segmental shape | Five-vowel system and predominantly open syllables; distinctive sibilant contrasts occur. | Avoid English-style consonant clusters and treating spelling digraphs as sequences of independent English sounds. |
| Tone | High/low tonal contrasts participate in lexical and grammatical distinctions. | Record tonal forms separately from an unmarked display spelling. |
| Writing | Present standard orthography uses the Latin script and normally omits tone marks. | Orthographic homography does not establish identical pronunciation or meaning. |
| Standardization | Doke's 1931 project and subsequent revisions belong to missionary/colonial institutional history. | A standardized written language is neither an ancient script nor evidence of cultural homogeneity. |

- **Autosegmental phonology:** use Myers (1990) to distinguish segmental form, tonal association, morphological domain, and realized pronunciation; H/L are categories, not fixed musical frequencies.
- Theoretical descriptions can use high-tone spreading and restrictions on adjacent H tones; the precise domains, exceptions, and rule ordering for any chosen variety are `(to-verify)` before implementation.
- Do not invent a Shona minimal pair from two familiar spellings, or apply a generic “Bantu tone rule” to every verb form.
- **D workflow:** give each invented lexeme a segmental form, underlying tone specification, class, and source label; test inflection, possessives, compounds, and address forms before generating names.
- For an invented literacy, choose explicitly whether everyday writing suppresses tone while teaching, ritual, or lexicographic notation records it. This is a design option, not an attested medieval Shona convention.
- No recovered indigenous written chronicle is being used here to reconstruct Great Zimbabwe's own speech or theology; modern Shona forms cannot supply a verbatim medieval court register.
- Orthographic and ethnolinguistic classification deserve the historical critique in Chimhundu (1992), not just Doke's administrative categories.

## Mythic core: relationships and jurisdictions, not a tidy pantheon
| Category | Situated reference use | Common design error |
|---|---|---|
| Mwari | High/creator deity in Shona religious accounts; contemporary usage also participates in Christian discourse. | Inventing a fixed Olympian-style family tree of gods. |
| Ancestral spirits | Deceased kin can remain participants in family obligations, protection, and consultation. | Treating every dead person as an interchangeable “ghost monster.” |
| Mhondoro | Territorial/guardian spirits associated with chiefly ancestry in documented traditions. | Equating every occurrence with either a literal lion or the Shumba clan. |
| Svikiro | Spirit medium; authority involves relationships and recognition, not merely personal supernatural power. | Assuming a medium is automatically a hereditary king or priest of all rites. |
| Shave | Spirit categories associated in ethnographic accounts with acquired abilities and other affiliations. | Translating every non-ancestor spirit as an evil demon. |
| Ngozi | Avenging spirit discourse can concern injury, accountability, and restitution. | Removing the social obligation and retaining only a curse effect. |

- Baseline references: Bourdillon (1976), supplemented for mbira-associated ancestors and guardians by MBIRA (n.d.). Terminology and jurisdiction vary; exact local mappings are `(to-verify)`.
- Mwari/Mwali shrine traditions around Matobo/Matonjeni belong to a regional field including Kalanga and other communities; a specifically medieval Great Zimbabwe cult organization is `(to-verify)`, not supplied by present nomenclature.
- Separate origin narrative, territorial authorization, family remembrance, healing, and dispute settlement; they need not share one specialist or one sacred place.
- **D:** map which relationships authorize a voice: elder → lineage ancestor; medium → recognized guardian; musician → service to a gathering. Do not automatically copy these roles into a single supreme priesthood.
- Treat practitioners' explanations as religious claims in their own setting, not as laboratory proof of supernatural causation or as irrational errors to be “corrected.”

## Dzimbahwe: stone, enclosure, bird, and interpretive limits
- **Name:** Zimbabwe/dzimbahwe is conventionally connected with “houses of stone”; Huffman and Main document that the sixteenth-century sense was “the home, court, and grave of a leader” (De Barros 1551, in Theal), so today's gloss and early usage differ. The exact contraction, dialect form, and competing derivations remain `(to-verify)`; do not turn one into a recovered inscription. [Huffman and Main 2022]
- **A:** Great Zimbabwe's granite dry-stone walls use no mortar. Earthen/daga domestic structures and courtyards were integral to the inhabited settlement. [Metropolitan Museum 2001]
- **I:** Walls can distinguish access, visibility, status, and authority; they are not automatically military fortifications or roofs of stone palaces. Exact uses of particular enclosures remain debated. [Chirikure and Pikirayi 2008]
- **A/I:** Carved soapstone birds survive; ancestral/royal significance is an interpretation, not a translated label left by their makers. Their precise meaning and original arrangements are not securely recovered. [Metropolitan Museum 2001]
- **I:** Assigning a particular bird species or one universal message to every bird is too strong; retain morphology, find context, and alternative identifications separately.
- **I:** The conical tower's function and symbolism are debated. Granary, fertility, or kingship readings should be attributed as hypotheses rather than encoded as settled fact.
- **M:** The Zimbabwe Bird's national-emblem role belongs to a later political history; present flag use does not decode the medieval carving automatically.

| Sign layer | Useful question | Fictional transfer, explicitly D |
|---|---|---|
| Material index | What labor, quarry, skill, and maintenance does the stone imply? | Authority displayed through coordinated labor rather than enormous weapons. |
| Spatial distinction | Who can approach, see, speak, or remain behind a wall? | Differentiated public and restricted audiences without assuming every enclosure is a temple. |
| Conventional emblem | Which community recognizes the bird or wall motif, and when? | A politically situated sign with rival readings, not a universal symbol dictionary. |
| Later resemanticization | Who subsequently claims the ruin? | Shrine, archaeological monument, and national emblem can coexist and conflict. |

## Mutupo: clan emblems, taboo animals, and praise address
- **Mutupo** is singular; **mitupo** plural. “Totem” is a convenient comparative gloss, not evidence for a single global totemism stage of religion. [Bourdillon 1976; Hodza and Fortune 1979]
- Clan affiliation commonly involves patrilineal descent, recognition through an emblem, praise/address forms, marriage constraints, and food avoidance; none should be reduced to an animal mascot.
- **Chidawo** designates a praise/address designation that may distinguish a branch; do not equate it mechanically with mutupo, a modern surname, or a given name.
- Animal identification is only the beginning of an entry: attach lineage, locality, praise branch, speaker, occasion, and the particular prohibition.

| Reference emblem/name | Conventional association | What must not be inferred automatically |
|---|---|---|
| Shava / Mhofu | Eland | Every eland-associated branch has identical genealogy, praise wording, or rules. |
| Soko | Monkey/baboon association | One precise zoological species or the same translated praise title everywhere. |
| Shumba | Lion | All lion symbolism is clan symbolism, or all mhondoro belong to this clan. |
| Nzou | Elephant | Universal prohibition on ivory, images, touch, killing, and meat together. |
| Moyo | Heart | All mitupo refer to whole animals; an organ association can delimit avoidance differently. |

- These are orientation examples, not a complete or validated clan directory; branch assignments, exact translations, and sanctions for each are `(to-verify)` against a passage and a relevant community.
- A frequently described taboo is eating the associated animal or body part. Do not expand food avoidance into total species protection without evidence.
- Same-mutupo marriage avoidance can express shared descent; exact exceptions, subclan distinctions, adoption, and present legal practice are `(to-verify)` locally.
- Praise performance can make ancestry socially present through address and remembered relationships; copying a public English translation does not establish permission or competence to perform it.
- **D:** design `{descent account, emblem, permitted address, avoidance, marriage rule, exception authority}` together; then test a guest meal, marriage negotiation, and formal greeting.
- An emblem need not be visibly worn: speech, food refusal, and recognition can carry identity without a clan-animal costume.

## Mbira dzavadzimu: sound-making as ancestral relationship
- Use **mbira dzavadzimu**, “mbira of the ancestral spirits,” for the relevant tradition; mbira also names a broader instrument family. It is not a synonym for every commercial kalimba. [Berliner 1978; MBIRA n.d.]
- **A/E, living construction:** metal lamellae attached to a hardwood soundboard, often amplified by a calabash **deze**; buzzing attachments contribute deliberately to the sound. [MBIRA, “The Mbira Instrument”]
- Modern instruments can use recycled steel, bottle tops, and fiberglass resonators. These are dated material adaptations, not evidence that ritual practice has become inauthentic.
- **E:** At a **bira** gathering, music can participate in addressing ancestors or guardians and seeking guidance. **Mapira** is the plural; do not confuse the ceremony with a generic concert.
- Paired, interlocking **kushaura/kutsinhira** parts, cyclic variation, singing, dance, and **hosho** shakers make an ensemble and social process, not a lone “magic instrument.” [Berliner 1978]
- MBIRA describes the recognized **gwenyambira** as a musician in service to the spirits and community; do not collapse that role into the medium's role.
- Repetition can sustain attention and participation, but “a particular rhythm mechanically causes possession” is an unsupported universal law.
- Mbira also occurs in secular gatherings, teaching, stage performance, and international circulation; not all Shona people participate in ancestral ceremonies. [UNESCO 2020; MBIRA n.d.]
- Preserve local tuning relationships and instrument histories; a Western mode label is a comparison, not an exact indigenous tuning specification. [MBIRA, “The Mbira Instrument”]
- Do not backdate the current key layout, repertoire, bottle-top buzzers, or a particular bira protocol to Great Zimbabwe. Detailed medieval performance reconstruction is `(to-verify)`.

| Coupling | Practical question | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Kinship → repertoire | Who requests a remembered piece, and why does that relationship matter? | Do not invent an actual ancestor's preference and attribute it to a living lineage. |
| Material → sound | Which local metals, wood, resonator, and buzzing devices are available in this era? | Distinguish ironworking evidence from proof of a specific instrument. |
| Speech → song | How do lexical tone, poetic wording, and melody interact? | Shona-specific tone–melody constraints are `(to-verify)`; no one-to-one speech-tone-to-key mapping. |
| Authority → event | Who recognizes a successful consultation, and who can disagree? | Community interpretation matters; sound alone does not establish social legitimacy. |

## Stone sculpture: modern creation without denying cultural agency
- The internationally recognized stone-sculpture movement developed in the mid-twentieth century; medieval soapstone birds do not demonstrate an uninterrupted institutional lineage to it. [Winter-Irving 1991; Zilberg 1996]
- Key modern settings include Frank McEwen's National Gallery workshop associated with the gallery opened in 1957, and the Tengenenge community founded in 1966 under Tom Blomefield.
- Name artists, not an anonymous tribal genius: Joram Mariga, Henry Munyaradzi, Nicholas Mukomberanwa, and Bernard Matemera are useful starting points for object-specific study.
- “Shona sculpture” is a movement/market label; participants' identities and influences exceed an ethnically sealed category.
- Serpentinite varieties, including stone marketed as springstone, are important modern materials; do not equate them with the medieval soapstone birds or granite walls.
- Human–animal transformations, kinship, ancestral themes, and statements about a spirit within stone may be artist-specific positions, patron narratives, or both; they are not automatically an ancient communal catechism.
- For any claimed mythological meaning, collect `{artist, object title, date, material, attributed statement, interviewer/catalogue, translation}`; a seller's generic caption is insufficient.
- **Calibration:** “invented tradition” identifies historical production and authorization; it does not mean African artists lack authorship or their contemporary spiritual commitments are fraudulent.
- McEwen-centered discovery stories can marginalize artists and other training networks; purely commercial explanations can erase agency in the opposite direction. Hold both risks open.
- **D:** a new artistic school may selectively reinterpret older emblems, develop fresh mythic readings, and later be marketed as ancient. Date each layer instead of “exposing” all modern meaning as false.

## Era staging: overlapping regional histories, not a single tech ladder
| Stage | Safe historical scaffold | Language / myth / material consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Before monumental Great Zimbabwe | Farming, herding, and metalworking histories precede the surviving walls. | Do not make stone construction the invention of social complexity or spoken religion. |
| Great Zimbabwe, broadly 11th–15th centuries | Expanding settlement and monumental building; local production alongside long-distance exchange. | Distinguish archaeological objects from reconstructed titles, theology, and dress. |
| Later medieval/early modern regional states | Mutapa and Khami/Torwa histories overlap rather than form a single succession at one capital. | Political relocation need not replace every language, material skill, or ancestral attachment. |
| Later Rozvi and nineteenth-century changes | Regional power is reworked through conflict, migration, and changing trade. | Do not label every stone ruin “the same dynasty”; specific local dates are `(to-verify)`. |
| Colonial period from the 1890s | Land and religious repression; mission education, language standardization, and later gallery/workshop institutions. | Printed orthography, imported clothing, and modern sculpture need their own dates. |
| Independence, 1980 onward | National reuse of Great Zimbabwe/Bird imagery; continuing religious, artistic, and diasporic plurality. | A national emblem or stage costume is not automatically a medieval survival. |

- Historical orientation: Beach (1980), Chirikure and Pikirayi (2008), and Metropolitan Museum (2001); precise polity boundaries, reigns, and local chronologies require targeted checking.
- Do not assert one drought, one invasion, or a single date explaining every settlement's decline; political, economic, and environmental explanations need site-specific evaluation.

## Naming practices and social address
- Keep **given name**, **family/surname record**, **mutupo**, **chidawo**, **title**, and **spirit/medium designation** as separate fields, even when real usage links them. [Bourdillon 1976; Hodza and Fortune 1979]
- A person's public label need not disclose the lineage or praise form that makes a formal greeting appropriate.
- Given-name interpretation may involve circumstances, hope, religious commitment, or a message to relatives; a name's dictionary gloss is not its complete social biography.
- Familiar starting examples are **Chipo**, “gift” (shortened from names like Chipochangu “my gift” and ChipochaMwari “gift from God”), and **Tendai**, “(be) thankful” (from TendaiMwari, Tendaizvose), alongside Tsitsi “mercy,” Rudo “love,” Tatenda “we are thankful,” and Nyasha “mercy/grace” [Makondo 2013]; tone and morphological derivation still need a dictionary/community check `(to-verify)` before reuse.
- Do not generate pseudo-Shona by attaching noun-class prefixes to English roots; class membership and agreement are grammatical, not flavor particles.
- Keep ancestral/place names distinct from titles of their living representatives; an inherited designation does not establish identity of all its bearers.
- **D:** test the same fictional person's birth announcement, intimate address, praise greeting, marriage inquiry, and administrative record. Preserve contextual differences rather than one universal “full name.”

## Provenance cautions and stop rules
- Archaeology does not recover an entire spoken religion; oral histories, Portuguese accounts, missionary records, ethnography, and modern art catalogues have different dates, purposes, and omissions.
- Early excavation, removal, and colonial racial politics damaged both objects' contexts and interpretations. Do not present a precise original arrangement when provenance is incomplete.
- Ethnographic analogy is a bounded hypothesis generator, not a time machine; modern clan practice, spirit categories, and performance cannot simply be projected backward.
- “Pan-African symbolism,” universal totemism ladders, literal Jungian decoding, and a single timeless Shona worldview are not evidence frameworks for this entry.
- Neither an institution's heritage inscription nor an art dealer's authenticity claim proves thousand-year continuity of a specific practice; claims of exact antiquity require independent evidence.
- Do not invent quotations, praise verses, named sacred ancestors, or modern artists' explanations. Leave an unverified interpretation `(to-verify)` rather than supplying a plausible-sounding myth.
- Thin areas remain explicit: medieval tailoring and colors; clan-specific prohibitions/exceptions; exact tonal derivations; shrine jurisdiction across communities; object-specific modern sculpture exegesis.
- Borrow relationships and design problems, not living sacred authority. Reuse of real praise texts, ritual knowledge, or named communities calls for appropriate attribution and community-informed review; this file makes no rights ruling.
