## Language family, writing, and the preserved/vernacular coupling
- Arabic belongs to the Semitic branch of Afroasiatic; root-and-pattern morphology is important, but not every word reduces to a transparent three-consonant root. (Versteegh 2014.)
- Arabic script is a right-to-left abjad with conventions for long vowels; ordinary texts usually omit short-vowel signs. Scriptural, pedagogical, and other carefully vocalized texts can supply much more reading information.
- Separate the **rasm** (basic written skeleton), consonant-distinguishing dots, vowel/recitation signs, and performance. These are related layers with histories, not one complete system invented on one date. (van Putten 2022; Blair 2006.)
- The Quran's authority preserves a text and disciplined recitations; it does not make Quranic orthography, the grammarians' Classical ideal, later literary usage, and a modern standard identical.
- The **qirāʾāt** are regulated reading traditions, not arbitrary reader mistakes or merely regional accents. Distinguish accepted liturgical variation from manuscript variants and from everyday dialect variation. (van Putten 2022.)
- Arabic script also writes non-Arabic languages; Persian is Iranian/Indo-European and Turkish is Turkic. Shared letters, scholarly loans, or Islam do not establish shared linguistic ancestry. (Versteegh 2014.)

| Layer or practice | Historical function | Craft variable and guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Quranic Arabic and recitation | Revealed wording, worship, memorization, regulated performance | Fix an authoritative corpus, but separately specify who licenses its pronunciation. |
| Classical literary/learned Arabic | Grammar, poetry, law, theology, correspondence, scholarship | Give institutions prestige norms; learned prose is not exclusively religious. |
| Documentary and intermediate usages | Letters and practical writing can depart from a Classical ideal | “Middle Arabic” describes heterogeneous textual phenomena, not a single universal middle dialect. |
| Local vernacular Arabic | Household, street, work, storytelling, and other spoken domains | Develop local histories, not “broken scripture”; speech can be eloquent and politically potent. |
| Multilingual repertoires | Arabic alongside, for example, Greek, Coptic, Syriac, Persian, or Amazigh varieties | Stage region and period; Arabicization and conversion are different processes. |
| Modern Standard Arabic | Modern standard shaped by later institutions, print, and public communication | Do not put a modern broadcast register unchanged into an Abbasid marketplace. |

- Ferguson's **H/L diglossia** identifies functionally differentiated high and low varieties, with H learned through formal instruction; it is an analytical starting point, not two sealed languages or two hereditary castes. (Ferguson 1959.)
- Elite speakers can shift register within interaction; written texts also mix features. Map **domain × audience × medium × training**, not simply priest versus peasant. (Versteegh 2014.)
- Preserve asymmetry without stasis: a jurist can innovate vocabulary or argument while quoting an authoritative passage exactly; vernacular change need not alter liturgical wording.
- Fictional application: decide which elements are protected—wording, orthography, melody, grammar, or interpretation—and which institution pays to transmit each. Treat these as separable design choices.
- Do not infer an early city's complete pronunciation from formal grammars or a later chronicle. Any exact reconstructed local accent requires dated evidence and specialist checking (to-verify).

## Sacred narratives: monotheism, not a transplanted pantheon
- “Mythic” here means a narrative/cosmological analytical category, not a verdict that a living religion's truth claims are false. A pantheon template is a poor fit for Islamic theology.
- **Tawḥīd:** divine unity; Allāh is God, not one departmental deity among rivals. Quran 112 is a compact primary reference. The popular “Allah was simply a moon god” shortcut misrepresents Islamic theology.

| Narrative or cosmological field | Primary anchor | Coupled practice; limit |
|---|---|---|
| Revelation and prophetic succession | Quran 12; 20; 33:40 | Shared prophetic histories feed exegesis, sermons, personal names, and dynastic claims; prophets are not gods. |
| Angels, jinn, and Iblīs | Quran 18:50; 27:17 | Distinct kinds of created beings; jinn are not a race of minor deities. Do not import a single Christian demon taxonomy. |
| Judgment and accountability | Quran 99; 107 | Ethical narratives support giving and public piety; scripture alone does not specify a complete waqf administration. |
| Night Journey and ascent traditions | Quran 17:1 for the journey | Distinguish the Quranic passage from later elaborated miʿrāj narratives (for the reception history, see the lead at Buckley 2013); the particular hadith, sīra, or artistic version still needs checking (to-verify). |
| Fasting and sacred time | Quran 2:183–185; 9:36–37 | A lunar ritual calendar makes festivals move through solar seasons; a festival does not imply one permanent seasonal costume. |

- Sunni, Shiʿi, and other communities do not share every account of legitimate authority, sacred genealogy, commemorative practice, or interpretation. “Caliphate” is a contested claim to leadership, not a single theology. (Berkey 2003.)
- Saints' narratives, shrine practice, philosophical cosmologies, and court storytelling need their own region, date, and genre. Neither *The Thousand and One Nights* nor a saint's life is a universal creed.
- Fictional application: couple authorized narration to its medium—recitation, sermon, commentary, shrine story, court performance—then define disagreement without treating one register as every inhabitant's belief.

## Era staging: overlapping regimes, not a golden-age/decline ladder
- Dates below are CE and political anchors, not simultaneous changes in speech, theology, or clothing. Near Eastern reference frame: Berkey (2003); language and visual changes: Versteegh (2014), Blair (2006), Stillman (2000).

| Stage | Language and institution | Symbol/material coupling; anti-anachronism |
|---|---|---|
| Late Antiquity and prophetic lifetime, to 632 | Arabic varieties coexist with other regional languages; Islam emerges within an already connected Near East | Do not project mature madrasa institutions or later court ceremonial into the earliest community. |
| Early caliphs, 632–661 | Rapid expansion encounters established fiscal, scribal, and religious institutions | Conquest does not instantly replace all administrators, languages, or local dress. |
| Umayyad Damascus, 661–750 | Arabicization of state practices proceeds through concrete reforms, not universal overnight conversion | Monumental epigraphy and coinage can announce authority; match each reform to dated objects. |
| Abbasids, from 750; Baghdad taken in 1258 | Arabic scholarship and translation coexist with Persian and other repertoires; effective political control contracts long before 1258 | Court textiles, book culture, and patronage change without one empire-wide costume; Abbasid black has a dynastic context. |
| Rival caliphates: Fatimids, 909–1171; Córdoba's Umayyad caliphate, 929–1031 | Competing leadership and scholarly networks; Fatimid rule is not simply Sunni Baghdad transplanted westward | A shared script can carry rival genealogies, formulas, titles, and workshop patronage. |
| Ayyubid and Mamluk Egypt, 12th–early 16th centuries | Endowed colleges and urban teaching networks provide a later comparative lens | Berkey's Cairo evidence is especially useful here; it must not be silently backdated to the 7th century. |

- The Baghdad Abbasid collapse is not the end of Arabic learning, Islamic art, or every Abbasid caliphal claim; later Cairo claims require a different political model. (Berkey 2003.)
- Avoid an essential “Islamic golden age followed by inevitable decline.” Track patronage, trade routes, paper/book production, jurisprudence, and regional power separately.
- Treat the Maghrib, al-Andalus, Iraq, Egypt, Syria, and Arabia as interacting regions, not interchangeable scenery. Ottoman, Safavid, and Mughal references require additional period-specific research.

## Waqf: a legal vocabulary that builds an urban ecology
- A **waqf** dedicates assets and channels their benefits under legally articulated conditions; institutional forms and doctrines vary. It is not simply a modern charity, state budget, or a mechanically equivalent English trust. (Singer 2008.)
- Separate recurring endowed income from an isolated gift, and both from **zakāt**, a distinct obligatory giving framework. **Ṣadaqa** is a broader charitable term, not a synonym for one property regime.
- A founder's piety, family provision, prestige, and property strategy can coexist. Do not force a saintly-charity versus cynical-corruption binary. Women as well as men could found endowments. (Singer 2008.)

| Term | Working meaning | Design question or legal caution |
|---|---|---|
| **waqf**, plural **awqāf** | Endowment; context can emphasize the act, institution, or endowed assets | What property produces the benefit, and who can contest its status? |
| **ḥabs**, plural **aḥbās** | Related endowment terminology, especially important in the Maghrib | Preserve regional terminology; do not invent a second religion from a different legal word. |
| **wāqif** | Founder/endower | Who has standing and resources to establish it? |
| **mawqūf** | Endowed property | Distinguish the retained asset from the revenue or use it supplies. |
| **mawqūf ʿalayhi** | Beneficiary or designated beneficiary category | Can beneficiaries be relatives, students, a public service, or successive groups? |
| **waqfiyya** | Endowment deed/document | Which language and formulas make the record legible to a court? Documentary names vary. |
| **nāẓir / mutawallī** | Terms for oversight or administration | Functions and appointment procedures vary; do not assume modern interchangeable job descriptions. |
| **shurūṭ** | Conditions/stipulations | Founder, administrator, judge, and beneficiary may read the same clause differently. |
| **khayrī / ahlī / dhurrī** | Useful labels for charitable/public-benefit and family/descendant arrangements | Typology is not a universal original three-box statute; mixed and successive beneficiaries complicate it. |

- Vocabulary table is a comparative working map, not legal advice. For a specified school, century, or jurisdiction, verify ownership theory, perpetuity, alienability, cash/movable assets, and terminology against local deeds and legal scholarship (to-verify).
- **Coupling chain, not an automatic historical law:** endowed shop rents or agricultural income → maintenance, water, books, stipends → teaching opportunities → reproduction of a learned register → inscriptions naming benefactors and conditions.
- Such chains create grounded conflicts: a damaged watercourse affects school income; an ambiguous beneficiary term becomes a grammatical/legal dispute; an administrator's accounts and a donor's inscription describe different aspects of the same foundation.
- Fictional application: design the revenue-producing asset, beneficiary sequence, maintenance burden, record genre, and dispute forum before inventing ceremonial foundation titles. These are optional mechanisms, not canon.

## Madrasa register formation: money, persons, and textual genres
- A **madrasa** is not inherently a modern centralized school or an elementary Quran class. Medieval endowed colleges developed in particular regional and legal settings; mosques, homes, and other teaching sites remained important. (Berkey 1992; 2003.)
- Do not give one minister the invention of all Islamic education. The eleventh-century Niẓāmiyya foundations are important within a longer institutional history, not the beginning of learning itself. (Berkey 2003.)
- In Berkey's Cairo evidence, personal scholarly relationships remain crucial. A building's endowment does not by itself monopolize teaching authority, and formal establishments do not exhaust women's participation in transmission. (Berkey 1992.)

| Register-making mechanism | Vocabulary / genre | Coupled consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Recitation and elementary learning | Quran learning; **kuttāb** in relevant settings | Memorized wording can be widespread without equal access to advanced textual interpretation. |
| Linguistic disciplines | **naḥw** grammar; **ṣarf** morphology; **balāgha** rhetoric | Mastery of a high register becomes teachable, examinable socially, and useful to legal interpretation. |
| Juristic instruction | **fiqh**; **mudarris** teacher; **dars** lesson | Shared terminology need not erase disagreements between legal schools. |
| Commentary practices | **matn** base text; **sharḥ** commentary; **ḥāshiya** gloss | Generations conserve wording while extending argument; genre histories must be staged, not assumed timeless. |
| Personal authorization | **ijāza** permission/authorization; **isnād** transmission chain | Credentials depend on people, subjects, and texts; an ijāza is not automatically a standardized institutional degree. |
| Endowed support | Founder conditions, posts, stipends, books | Institutional incentives select what is taught and who can persist, without mechanically determining every scholarly conclusion. |

- **Morphology-to-institution coupling:** the Arabic root d-r-s links *dars*, *madrasa*, and *mudarris* through different derivations. A fictional language can similarly make lesson, teaching place, and teacher recognizably related. (Versteegh 2014.)
- Do not equate a madrasa with a European university and infer identical corporations, curricula, examinations, or degrees. Similar functions do not establish direct institutional descent. (Berkey 1992; 2003.)
- Application worksheet: identify a novice's home speech, recitation training, advanced text, authorized teacher, patron's funding conditions, and the register used when disputing the text. Allow partial competence and multilingual students.

## Symbolic system: contextual aniconism, arabesque, and calligraphy
- Distinguish **aniconism** (not using figural images in a setting), **iconoclasm** (actions against images), and a jurist's **normative prohibition**. They are not synonyms, and a prohibition is not a census of surviving practice. (Flood 2002.)
- Quranic opposition to idols should not be rewritten as a Quranic verse banning every representation. Hadith, jurisprudence, patronage, setting, medium, and use all matter. (Berkey 2003; Flood 2002.)

| Setting or visual practice | Productive comparison | Limit on inference |
|---|---|---|
| Mosque/prayer and Quran manuscript settings | Nonfigural decoration and writing can articulate sacred space | Do not extrapolate every local convention to every palace, textile, household, or illustrated book. |
| Courtly and other nonliturgical objects | Humans and animals occur in Islamic visual cultures | Their existence does not prove universal legal approval; date, patron, and use still matter. |
| Vegetal scrolls / “arabesque” | Stylization, branching, repetition, and surface integration | “Arabesque” is an art-historical label, not proof of a single native doctrine or exclusively Arab production. |
| Geometric pattern | Rule-based construction organizes surfaces and skilled labor | An eight-point star is not intrinsically a fixed theological proposition. Universal number decoding needs evidence. |
| Calligraphic inscription | Wording, script, scale, placement, and patronage work together | Not all Arabic writing is Quranic; blessings, poetry, ownership, dates, and titles have different functions. |
| Crescent, star, or a politically charged color | Emblems acquire histories through particular states and uses | Do not assign the modern crescent-and-star ensemble to all early caliphates: the crescent's association with Islam is Ottoman-mediated ([Britannica, “crescent”](https://www.britannica.com/topic/crescent-symbol)). Check the particular object. |

- Grabar's **mediation of ornament** supports asking what ornament makes a viewer do—attend, move, admire skill, perceive continuity—before asserting that every pattern encodes infinity. Mystical readings require a named author/context. (Grabar 1992.)
- Calligraphy gains sacred importance from the Quran's status as divine speech and from practices of copying and recitation; it also serves bureaucratic, poetic, commemorative, and courtly purposes. Sacred art is not the same as exclusively sacred employment. (Blair 2006.)
- **Kufic** is an umbrella label for diverse angular scripts, not every early hand or every monumental script; **naskh** and **thuluth** are not interchangeable labels for anything cursive. Avoid a one-script-per-century progression. (Blair 2006.)
- Material constraints matter: pen, support, page proportions, ink, pigment, workshop training, and viewing distance shape the result. An inscription can project prestige beyond the circle able to read it fluently.
- Never treat a real Quranic passage as semantically empty fantasy decoration. Identify text, intended audience, placement, and handling; for an invented culture, generate its own authorized wording only through the canon-owning handoff.

## Costume and material signatures: rank is enacted, not merely worn
- Use **garment × textile × inscription × distribution occasion × wearer × date/place**, not a generic “Islamic robe.” The same broad garment label can cover changing cuts and social meanings. (Stillman 2000.)

| Item or material category | Usable coupling | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| **qamīṣ**, **sirwāl**, **jubba** | Shirt/tunic, trousers, and outer-garment vocabulary connects embodied practice to language contact | Definitions are approximate; reconstruct cut from dated evidence, not a modern cognate. |
| **ʿimāma** and other head coverings | Wrapping, fabric quality, occasion, and office can mark status | “Turban = cleric” is not a timeless universal identification. |
| **iḥrām** and pilgrimage dress | Ritual status affects permitted clothing; for men, two wraps and restrictions on fitted garments form one specific regime | “Unsewn” is an imprecise shortcut: stitching itself is not the general prohibition. Women's dress differs; pilgrimage dress is not everyday uniform. |
| **khilʿa**, robe of honor | A ruler or patron publicly transfers favor; an ensemble can enact office and dependency | Not necessarily one standard garment; giver, recipient, ceremony, and period matter. |
| **ṭirāz** | Inscribed textiles and their production/administrative contexts tie words to workshops and authority | Not a synonym for any embroidered cuff or necessarily a Quranic text. |
| Linen, wool, cotton, silk, metal thread | Supply, climate, skill, price, and legal ideals intersect | A fiber is not inherently one ethnicity; silk rules and observed elite consumption require separate evidence. |
| Rank or confessional dress restrictions | Law can attempt to make distinctions publicly legible | A decree does not prove continuous enforcement or one uniform costume across all non-Muslim communities. |

- Stillman's historical synthesis is a starting point; a precise hem, dye, badge, wrap, or textile pattern requires an object, inventory, image, or local description (to-verify). Surviving luxury fragments overrepresent elite consumption.
- Court gift textiles and donor inscriptions connect **political favor → material value → written title → public recognition**. The receiver need not personally read every word for the garment to function socially.
- Do not project modern national dress, modern hijab styles, or later Ottoman uniforms backward as caliphal defaults. Nor should an ideal of modesty erase variation in occupation, gender, wealth, region, or communal practice.
- Application: choose how an office is conferred, what material communicates it, who makes it, and which text authorizes it; only then choose a silhouette. Route the resulting candidates, not decrees, to lore-shokunin.

## Naming practices: social coordinates rather than fixed modern surnames
- Working typology after Schimmel (1989), whose edition is verified in the source ledger; regional prevalence and documentary form are not established here `(to-verify)`. Components vary by genre, period, gender, and social position; not everybody displays every component.

| Component | Function | Guardrail for generative naming |
|---|---|---|
| **ism** | Personal name, e.g. Muḥammad or Fāṭima | A name may carry devotional associations without proving the bearer's politics or learning. |
| **nasab** | Descent sequence using **ibn/bin** or **bint** | Do not assume every sequence is a hereditary modern family surname. |
| **kunya** | Abū/Umm + a name or designation | May be honorific or figurative; do not automatically infer an actual child. |
| **nisba** | Relational adjective: locality, lineage, occupation, affiliation, among other relations | One apparent place-name does not establish current residence; multiple nisbas may coexist. |
| **laqab** | Epithet or honorific; political titles can be especially elaborate | Caliphal titulature is not a pool of random everyday surnames. |
| Devotional compounds | **ʿAbd Allāh**, **ʿAbd al-Raḥmān**, and other established formations | Preserve syntax and attestation; a random noun after “ʿAbd” is not automatically an appropriate divine epithet. |

- Distinguish **al-**, the definite article, from **Āl**, a family/house designation. Transliteration systems and assimilated pronunciation can produce different spellings without different lineages.
- Names do not mechanically reveal religion, ethnicity, first language, or citizenship. Arabic-speaking Christians and Jews, non-Arab Muslims, converts, clients, and mobile scholars defeat a one-name/one-identity map. (Versteegh 2014; Berkey 2003.)
- Fictional application: define short address, formal scholarly identification, court titulature, and archival indexing separately. Model which institution records each component; do not concatenate every available title.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **Preservation without linguistic death:** an authoritative textual layer can remain tightly constrained while home speech, literary expression, and contact vocabulary change.
- **Institutions as linguistic engines:** property income can finance people and books; training makes registers durable; disputes make precise wording materially consequential.
- **Sacred art as a production system:** couple authorized words, workshop craft, support materials, patronage, audience, and rules of placement rather than adding ornamental pseudo-script.
- **Authority worn and read:** an inscribed gift garment can combine office, allegiance, wealth, manufacturing capacity, and linguistic prestige in one public act.
- **Competing claims within shared media:** rival rulers can use the same language and script while changing titles, genealogy, formulas, educational patronage, and ceremonial distribution.
- **Handoff packet, not canon:** offer alternative register maps, endowment structures, narrative authorities, naming rules, and dated material palettes; attach source scope and uncertainty; lore-shokunin decides what enters the world bible.

## Provenance cautions and recheck triggers
- **Uneven coverage:** this entry is strongest on transregional Arabic and the cited Egyptian educational/dress evidence; it is not specialist coverage of every Maghribi, Andalusi, Arabian, Iraqi, or Shiʿi institution. Commission a local source pass before fine-grained adaptation (to-verify).
- **Chronicles are selective:** dynastic narratives favor rulers, conflict, and exceptional acts; they rarely supply representative everyday speech or complete ordinary wardrobes. Supplement with deeds, papyri, letters, inventories, inscriptions, and surviving objects.
- **Prescription is not practice:** Quranic passages, legal opinions, sumptuary orders, endowment conditions, and administrative accounts answer different questions. Triangulate rather than making one genre stand in for a whole society.
- **Colonial/Orientalist bias:** a timeless “Islamic mind,” a universal image ban, and an inevitable post-golden-age decline are inadequate explanatory shortcuts. Older travel accounts and museum classifications require authorship, collection-history, and local-counterevidence checks.
- **Interpretive calibration:** geometry-as-infinity is one possible situated reading, not a decoded fact for every surface; Ferguson's H/L binary and “arabesque” are analytical labels, not inhabitants' universal self-descriptions.
- **Evidence gaps are not creative permission to assert history:** exact early vernacular pronunciations, ordinary rural clothing, enforcement rates, and women's or enslaved people's lived experience are unevenly represented here. Mark proposed detail (to-verify), or keep it explicitly fictional rather than attributing it to a real population.
- **Recheck:** before assigning a date, local legal rule, script label, garment cut, sacred quotation, or name to an object, obtain a dated regional witness and a page/folio/object identifier.
