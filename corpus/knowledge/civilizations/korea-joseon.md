## Scope and calibration
- Joseon/Chosŏn denotes the dynasty founded in 1392; distinguish the kingdom from the Korean Empire proclaimed in 1897, although museum labels often use “Joseon, 1392–1910.”
- Romanization here is broadly Revised Romanization; cited titles retain their editions' spellings. Hangul/han'gŭl and Joseon/Chosŏn are romanization differences, not separate historical systems.
- Anchor every inference to date, region, household position, institution, and occasion. The late-Joseon gentleman is not a template for every Korean person or century.
- Calibration labels: **attested** = identified text/object or well-established institutional distinction; **model** = analytic abstraction; **(to-verify)** = unresolved wording, entitlement, recension, or historical extension.
- Court chronicles are abundant; evidence for ordinary speech, low-status households, and locally performed rites is much thinner and filtered through elite recording. Thin coverage here is not evidence that those people lacked culture.

## Era model: stage the coupled systems, not a single timeless culture
| Stage | Language/ritual situation | Material and social implications |
|---|---|---|
| 1392–early fifteenth century | New dynasty advances a Confucian state program; hanmun already has a long Korean history. | No hangul correspondence before its invention; institutional reform does not instantly erase earlier household practice. |
| Mid–late fifteenth century | Alphabet completed in 1443; *Hunminjeongeum* promulgated in 1446; royal textual projects coexist with hanmun. | Couple script innovation to sponsorship, translation, education, and resistance, not an instant mass-literacy switch. |
| Sixteenth century | Literati institutions and ritual scholarship gain importance; household arrangements remain historically variable. | Stage negotiations over inheritance, marriage residence, and sacrificial succession rather than assuming a fully formed later lineage order. |
| Wars of 1592–1598 and early seventeenth century | Japanese invasions, then Manchu invasions, disrupt institutions, archives, and households. | Reconstruction, displacement, and material loss can create discontinuities; “same dynasty” does not mean unchanged wardrobes or records. |
| Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries | Patrilineal organization and ancestor-centered elite practice become more entrenched, unevenly. | Genealogy, household space, mourning obligations, and ritual authority become mutually reinforcing status claims. |
| Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries | Vernacular letters, narratives, and court writing coexist with prestigious hanmun institutions. | Dating silhouettes and readership matters; H96 supplies a court woman's voice, not a sample of all women. |
| 1894 onward | Gwageo abolished in 1894; the 1895 topknot-cutting ordinance and 1897 imperial proclamation mark distinct changes. | Do not turn reform decrees into immediate universal compliance; V's 1880–1910 gat straddles political transitions. |
- Anchors: LR11, D92, L88, H96, U, V. These are working stages, not claims that every district changed simultaneously.

## Language family and writing: separate language, script, and institution
- **Family:** Korean belongs to Koreanic; a demonstrated genetic link to an “Altaic” superfamily must not be treated as settled. Contact with Sinitic and neighboring languages is not itself proof of common descent. (LR11)
- **Structural base:** predominantly verb-final, agglutinative, with particles and socially consequential honorification. Middle Korean phonology and grammar cannot be generated by merely replacing modern vocabulary with archaic titles. (LR11)
| System | What it actually is | High-value coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Korean speech | Vernacular language with historical and social variation. | Kinship, address, household interaction, oral instruction, and spoken explanation of texts. |
| Hanmun 漢文 | Literary/Classical Chinese as a learned written medium. | Memorials, learned composition, examinations, ritual documentation; not evidence that an official habitually spoke Mandarin. |
| Hanja 漢字 | Chinese characters used in Korean textual traditions. | Can write hanmun or participate in representing Korean; a script is not a separate spoken “high language.” |
| Hangul 한글 | Alphabet whose letters are arranged into syllable blocks; historical alphabet named *Hunminjeongeum*. | Korean letters, translations, instruction, literary narration, and royal projects; not exclusively female or exclusively low-status. |
| Idu 이두 / gugyeol 구결 | Different character-based practices for representing Korean elements or annotating/reading texts. | Scribal specialization and mediation between a learned text and Korean grammar; not synonyms for hanmun or hangul. |
- **Diglossia model:** map prestigious institutional hanmun against Korean vernacular functions, then overlay script choice. F59's original high/low-variety model is a heuristic here, not an exact description of this cross-language ecology.
- Model literacy as separate capacities: recognizing names; reading Korean letters; composing a letter; reading a classical text; producing examination prose. Do not assign one binary “literate” flag.
- Gendered script ideologies are evidence about prestige, not proof of exclusive users. H96 defeats “hangul was only for uneducated women”; male and royal uses also prevent a simple sex-based partition. (LR11; H96; U)
- *Eonmun* 언문 諺文 is a historical designation for vernacular writing; its valuation depends on speaker and context. “Hangul” is the modern label, not a safe assumption for fifteenth-century dialogue. (LR11)
- Craft application: build a text-routing matrix of writer → reader → medium → script → institutional consequence; only then choose visual letterforms.

## Confucian ritual registers: resolve the requested “yesa” before using it
- **Terminology calibration:** the supplied spelling “yesa” names no recognized Confucian ritual register — 禮事 *ye-sa* occurs only as a generic phrase for a ritual observance — so do not build a named register from it. The standard terms are *jesa* 제사 祭祀, ancestral sacrificial rites; *ye* 예 禮, the Confucian term for ritual order and procedure; and *yeseo* 예서 禮書, ritual books (the 삼대 예서 include the *Liji* and *Yili*). (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 예; 우리역사넷, “예와 제사”)
- Family rites and state rites are related but different scales. Zhu Xi's *Jiali* organizes capping, marriage, funerals, and ancestral sacrifices; Korean reception and adaptation must be studied rather than assumed identical. (E91; D92)
- Joseon court handbook *Gukjo oryeui* 國朝五禮儀 (1474) is confirmed as a primary-source lead for state ritual: five rite classes (길례 gillye, 가례 garye, 빈례 binnye, 군례 gunrye, 흉례 hyungnye) with the same-year companion *Gukjo oryeui seorye*. Name the recension, book, and prescription used for a scene. (Korea Heritage Agency notice; 국가유산진흥원 summary; cf. Korea Heritage Service on Jongmyo Daeje as gillye under the *Gukjo Oryeui*) Do not substitute a Chinese household manual for it.
| Register/surface | What to specify | What not to collapse |
|---|---|---|
| Ritual prescription | Authority cited, actors, sequence, spatial positions, vessels, offerings, garments. | A normative manual is not a census of actual compliance. |
| Ancestral address | *Chukmun* 축문 祝文: a written ritual invocation; identify ancestor, officiant, occasion, and textual tradition. | Do not fabricate an “ancient Korean prayer” by decorating modern polite speech. |
| Shrine authority | *Sinju* 신주 神主, ancestral tablet; *sinwi* 신위 神位, spirit position/designation. | Physical tablet, inscribed identity, and ritual position are not interchangeable props. |
| Spoken relationship | Status/kinship address, honorification, audience, and who may speak on whose behalf. | Do not assign one modern speech level to every ancestor rite or every noble household. |
| Performance | Spoken or chanted text, music, offerings, bowing, and coordinated movements. | Written script does not by itself establish pronunciation, melody, or timing. |
| Royal ancestral rite | Jongmyo 宗廟 integrates dynastic ancestors, designated ritual dress, music, and dance. | U describes a protected living form; current schedule and choreography cannot simply be projected backward. |
- Confucian “ritual” is also government and kinship technology: who inherits sacrificial responsibility affects residence, property, genealogy, and marriage. Couple those consequences to language and dress. (D92)
- A mourner's legitimate costume or a claimant's right to offer a cup may matter more than eloquence. Verify the exact relationship and rite before deciding either entitlement.
- Reconstruct utterances only from a dated text or linguistic study. Middle Korean marked tone with 방점 side-dots until tone was lost in the central dialect in the later sixteenth century, and its three-way honorific layer (subject/object/hearer deference) is not the modern speech-level taxonomy; exact invocation formulae, historical pronunciation, and court speech-level inventories therefore require a cited text or study for any scene requiring verbatim Korean. (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 중세국어; LR11)

## Gwageo: examination vocabulary and the reproduction of rank
- **Gwageo** 과거 科擧 is an institutional selection system, not a synonym for school, scholarship, or modern meritocracy. Resources, ancestry restrictions, networks, and changing regulations shape access. (L88; D92)
| Term | Working gloss | Coupling / translation trap |
|---|---|---|
| *Mungwa* 문과 文科 | Civil/literary examination. | Command of classical texts and learned composition; not a test of vernacular writing alone. |
| *Mugwa* 무과 武科 | Military examination. | Martial skills and textual knowledge; not “illiterate warrior” certification. |
| *Japgwa* 잡과 雜科 | Specialist examinations. | Interpretation, medicine, and other technical services; verify branch, office, and date rather than a fixed timeless syllabus. |
| *Sogwa* 소과 小科 | Lower civil examinations. | Licentiate achievement is distinct from higher civil success and office appointment. |
| *Saengwon* 생원 生員 | Classics licentiate. | Do not use as a generic word for every student. |
| *Jinsa* 진사 進士 | Literary/composition licentiate in this Joseon context. | Same characters as Chinese *jinshi* do not imply the same credential level. |
| *Daegwa* 대과 大科 | Higher civil examination. | Distinguish progression into this competition from holding a lower credential. |
| *Chosi* 초시 初試 | Initial examination stage. | Stage name, not a unique degree or guarantee of eventual success. |
| *Hoesi* 회시 會試 | Metropolitan examination stage. | Institutional location and filtering function matter. |
| *Jeonsi* 전시 殿試 | Palace-stage examination. | In the regular higher civil sequence, ranks successful candidates; not the structure of every specialist examination. |
| *Jangwon* 장원 壯元 | First-place distinction in an examination. | Not a permanent bureaucratic office title. |
| *Geupje* 급제 及第 | Passing/success in a state examination. | Record which examination; “passed the exam” otherwise hides the important distinction. |
| *Singnyeonsi* 식년시 式年試 | Regular triennial examinations. | Special examinations also occur; do not build every career around a single unchanging calendar. |
| *Chaengmun* 책문 策問 / *daechaek* 대책 對策 | Policy question / response. | Learned textual authority becomes an argument about governing, not ornamental quotation. |
| *Bangmok* 방목 榜目 | Examination passers' register. | Links achievement to names and ancestry; it is not a representative roster of the population. |
| *Hongpae* 홍패 紅牌 / *baekpae* 백패 白牌 | Red / white examination certificates. | Higher civil/military red certificates and lower-exam white certificates are a useful distinction; specialist practices and dated regulations need checking. |
- Institutional base: L88; the codified skeleton is checkable — 식년문과 ran 초시 → 복시 → 전시 with 33 places (갑과 3, 을과 7, 병과 23; 초시 240, later 243) — while exclusions were dated and revised: 장리 sons, sons of remarried or misconducted women, and 서얼, relaxed for 양첩 sons and grandsons in 1553, by the 1625 허통사목, and fully by 1696; 『속대전』 barred 천인 from 무과. A chosen year's syllabus, credential privileges, and enforcement still require the dated regulations and registers, not remembered drama scenes. (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 과거 §응시 자격; Wagner 1974; L88)
- **Yangban** 양반 兩班 begins with the civil/military “two orders” and develops broader elite-status meanings; **seonbi** 선비 is a learned/moral social ideal, not proof of a salaried appointment. Avoid one badge = one class = one occupation. (D92; L88)
- Fictional adaptation: make different credentials authorize different text genres, offices, ritual roles, and clothing claims; include a trained but unsuccessful candidate and a specialist whose expertise brings less prestige.

## Mythic and sacred core: overlapping jurisdictions, not one pantheon chart
| Layer | Attested/reference anchor | Worldbuilding use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Founding ancestry | I72 preserves the Dangun complex: heavenly Hwanung, a bear transformed into a woman, and their founder-son. | Divine descent can authorize a people or polity; this medieval textual witness is not eyewitness evidence for a third-millennium-BCE state. |
| Dynastic ancestors | Royal ancestors honored at Jongmyo; family ancestors located through descent and ritual succession. (D92; U) | Sacred authority can be genealogical and procedural without making every ancestor a deity in a universal pantheon. |
| Buddhist sacred persons | Buddhas, bodhisattvas, temples, and their patrons remain relevant despite state restrictions on Buddhism. (L88; I72) | Official orthodoxy and lived devotion need not coincide; medieval Buddhist tales are not a complete Joseon census. |
| Local/household spirits | Mountain spirits (*sansin* 산신 山神), household spirits, and spirit-mediated rites belong to differentiated local traditions, each separately documented (Seoul/Gyeonggi; east-coast 무가 with its narrative songs; Jeolla; Jeju; 서도). (K87 as a modern comparison; Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 무가; 우리역사넷, “지역별 무당의 호칭”) | Pick one named regional tradition and cite its own corpus; a twentieth-century ethnographic inventory cannot be backdated to a Joseon village unchanged. |
| Ritual specialists | *Mudang* 무당 and *gut* 굿 name practitioners and ritual events in Korean spirit-medium traditions. (K87) | “Shamanism” is a broad scholarly umbrella, not proof of a single church, priestly hierarchy, or common scripture. |
- Distinguish the date a story claims to describe, its earliest surviving witness, its recension, and later political reuse. Dangun ancestry is not a warrant for racial purity or a timeless homogeneous nation.
- Court condemnation of a rite records a contest over legitimacy; it does not establish the participants' own theology. Local voices and early modern performance detail are thinly covered in this entry.

## Symbolic grammar: read a motif with its carrier and occasion
| Sign/system | Supported interpretation | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Cranes + *yeongji* 영지 靈芝 fungus | Longevity imagery; on V's nineteenth-century badge, part of official status display. | Symbolic blessing and administrative rank can coexist on the same cloth. |
| Paired leopards | V's 1850–1900 badge identifies military rank, describing two leopards as first–third-rank insignia. | Retain the catalogue's animal identification; do not silently relabel every military beast a tiger. |
| *Eum-yang* 음양 陰陽 | Correlative/complementary forces; V's leopard badge actually includes a three-part emblem. | Do not reduce all variants to the modern national flag or to a good/evil opposition. |
| White/plain fabric | Mourning and cultivated restraint are possible context-dependent readings. | Fiber, finish, wearer, rite, and date must decide; white is not an essential “national personality.” (D92; E91) |
| Five-color/directional schemes | *Obangsaek* 오방색 五方色 is a documented correlative scheme — east blue, south red, center yellow, west white, north black, each tying direction to season and phase. (한국민속대백과사전, 오방색) | Garment color was policed separately: Joseon 복식금제 named banned colors (황·백·홍·자·회·옥·녹색) with reasons, most often 황색 and 백색, so a scene's color entitlement needs a dated regulation — no universal five-color lookup applies. (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 복식금제 §복색) |
| Dragons and other rank-sensitive emblems | Claw counts did grade rank: king/queen 오조룡, crown prince/princess 사조룡, grandson 삼조룡 — codified with the 1892 strengthening of the ritual code and carried into the 1897 empire (yellow dragon robe with 오조룡보). The popular “four claws for kings until the empire” story is contradicted by 『세종실록』 31:9:2 (1449), where Sejong already wore 오조룡의. (sillokwiki 사조룡의; Korea Heritage Service, 오조룡 왕비 보; 조선일보 “뉴스 속의 한국사,” 2023-12-28) | Keep a date and a bearer attached to every emblem; never import a Qing chart wholesale. |
- Method: record motif → object → material/technique → wearer → occasion → rule/date → interpreter. An emblem's political use is not exhausted by its mythological meaning.

## Costume and material signature: a gat is not an official's entire uniform
| Component/context | Usable distinction | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| *Gat* 갓; black *heungnip* 흑립 黑笠 | Black, partly openwork hat associated with a gentleman's public appearance; crown and brim are constructed separately. (V) | Not a universal Korean hat and not interchangeable with official gauze headgear. |
| Crown and brim | V describes a horsehair crown, bamboo-strand brim, joined and lacquered. | V's specimen anchors this construction; do not generalize every historical hat material from it. |
| Ties and storage | V's specimen has black silk-gauze ties and an associated storage box. | Beaded cords are not mandatory, but they were regulated by material and rank: 『경국대전』 allowed gold-and-jade 갓끈 to 당상관 and above, barred 마노·호박·산호·청금석 to lower officials, and 1469 bans extended to 서인. (우리역사넷, “모자의 나라, 조선”; Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 복식금제 §금은주옥; 정진영 2008) |
| Hair foundation | Topknot (*sangtu* 상투) and headband (*manggeon* 망건) help locate male headwear in bodily practice. The ritual manuals put the capping ceremony at 15–20, after which the headband marked adult status regardless of class, but household practice commonly folded it into marriage, and the 1894–95 topknot order ended the basis. (한국연구재단 한국복식사전, 망건; Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 관례) | Date the scene: a boy, a man in a married household, and a post-1895 haircut are different stages, and headband fittings follow the topknot. |
| *Baji* 바지 / *jeogori* 저고리 | Trousers / jacket, with different cuts and social uses across time. | Do not treat today's standardized hanbok silhouette as the fifteenth-century default. |
| *Dopo* 도포 道袍 | Scholar/gentleman's outer-robe vocabulary. | A social association, not automatic proof of examination success or current office. |
| *Danryeong* 단령 團領 / *samo* 사모 紗帽 | Round-collared official robe / official hat. | Distinguish office dress from a gat-and-robe public ensemble; exact authorized combination is date/occasion dependent. |
| *Hyungbae* 흉배 胸背 | Embroidered official rank badges; front/back placement and motifs make the body legible bureaucratically. (V) | Do not attach one to every yangban's everyday robe. |
| Family mourning / royal sacrifice | Mourning fabrics, ritual headwear, and royal ritual vestments have different functions. (E91; D92; U) | A king's Jongmyo ritual costume is not a black-gat gentleman outfit enlarged and gilded. |
| Silk, ramie, hemp, cotton | Fiber names describe different production, expense, season, and handling possibilities. | Class cannot be inferred from fiber alone; pin weaving, dye, condition, local availability, and occasion. |
- **Gat craft chain:** prepare/weave horsehair crown → construct bamboo brim → join → lacquer/finish → attach ties → fit and store. This is a material dependency model derived from V, not a complete artisan's manufacturing manual.
- **Dating rule:** crown height and brim diameter change; V explicitly notes changes around the 1895 topknot ordinance. Its small late specimen cannot establish the dimensions of an eighteenth-century portrait hat.
- **Entitlement rule:** “only examination passers could ever wear a black gat” is not established by these checked records. The dated laws concern hat material, weave, and brim — 1441/1449/1472 limits on 죽모·죽수, the 1504 입체 reform, a Hyojong-era (r. 1649–59) order against wide 양태, and 1670/1688 bans on 상민 grooms in 사모관대 — and the rank entitlement that was legislated sits on 갓끈 materials, not on the hat. (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 복식금제 §입모 and §금은주옥; 한국연구재단 한국복식사전, 갓)
- **Status rule:** keep hereditary standing, claimed lineage, actual office/rank, examination credential, wealth, and ritual role as separate fields. Clothing can assert or contest status rather than merely report it. (D92; V)
- **Scene check:** can the wearer sit, bow, travel, work, and enter the relevant room with this ensemble? Verify the rite's headwear practice rather than applying a modern remove-your-hat etiquette rule.

## Naming practices: identity is relational and differently public
- **Surname + origin:** *seong* 성 姓 and *bon-gwan* 본관 本貫 locate a person within a surname/descent-origin system; shared surname alone does not identify the same lineage, and clan origin need not be current residence. (D92)
- **Personal name:** an elite given name often has hanja specifying its written identity; romanized sound alone loses that distinction. Do not impose a universal two-syllable template on all classes and periods.
- **Generation:** shared generational characters, *hangnyeolja* 항렬자 行列字 / *dollimja* 돌림자, encode genealogical placement; each lineage fixes its own sequence (often cycling 오행·십간·수·오상) and prints the 항렬표 at the head of its *jokbo*, so take the chosen lineage's table rather than assigning one character to every sibling group. (Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 항렬; 권익기 2017, “성명(姓名)의 항렬자(行列字)에 관한 고찰”)
- **Courtesy/literary identity:** *ja* 자 字 and *ho* 호 號 can coexist with a personal name; their audience and stage of life differ. A literary style is not a birth name or a bureaucratic rank.
- **Posthumous identity:** *siho* 시호 諡號, posthumous title, and *myoho* 묘호 廟號, royal temple name, change the public naming regime after death; “Sejong” must not automatically become what subjects called the living king. (L88)
- **Women and archive:** natal surname, lineage, relationship, and bestowed title may dominate a surviving public designation; that does not mean women lacked personal names or self-authored identities. Read H96 alongside institutional records.
- **Registers:** a genealogy (*jokbo* 족보 族譜), an examination list, an ancestral tablet, and an intimate letter may identify the same person differently. Record aliases and name-use dates instead of choosing one “true name” for every surface.
- **Limit:** source survival favors elite genealogies. Low-status naming differed structurally — many Joseon slave records carry a bare name with no surname, and commoner men's formal name-characters surface mainly through military registers — while naming taboos (*pihwi* 피휘 避諱) were reign-specific: kings chose rare single-character names to lighten the avoidance burden (세종 이도, 영조 이금, 정조 이산; 정조's name was later revised 이산 → 이성 in the *Gyujanggak jeonun* context). Generalize from the selected record, not from elite examples. (우리역사넷, “성(姓) 없이 이름만 가진 사람들”; Encyclopedia of Korean Culture, 이름; 안대회 2010, 『한국문화』 52)

## What a worldbuilder can take — method, not transplanted canon
1. **Layered literacy:** specify spoken language, prestige written medium, vernacular script, training access, and translation offices independently; test one petition, one household letter, and one examination response.
2. **Ritualized kinship:** connect sacrificial succession to inheritance, marriage residence, naming, mourning dress, and authority to utter a text; allow the prescription and household practice to diverge.
3. **Credential ecology:** distinguish literary licentiate, higher civil success, military success, technical expertise, and actual appointment; give each different prestige, registers, and display permissions.
4. **Materially enforced rank:** design an outfit from head/hair foundation through robe, insignia, ties, and storage; attach a dated rule and a production cost to each regulated component.
5. **Plural sacred jurisdictions:** separate state ancestors, family ancestors, Buddhist institutions, and local spirit rites; build overlaps and disputes instead of a single interchangeable “Korean pantheon.”
6. **Era consequences:** make a script innovation, war, lineage reorganization, or haircut decree alter several systems together without making every person comply immediately.
7. **Handoff:** deliver a dated reference matrix, evidence pointers, alternatives, and unresolved checks to lore-shokunin; do not name a fictional dynasty, decree its religion, or write world-bible canon here.

## Provenance cautions and next checks
- **Norm versus life:** E91 prescribes; D92 reconstructs social change; H96 narrates a partisan court memory; I72 transmits medieval stories; K87 observes modern ritual; U presents heritage; V catalogues selected surviving objects. They answer different questions.
- **Contested reach:** Confucianization is a historical process, not a completed national conversion on a single date. D92's elite-centered model is influential but cannot erase regional, gender, class, and household variation.
- **Colonial/collection bias:** catalogues can inherit outdated status labels and collecting assumptions; V explicitly warns of this. Check acquisition history and Korean scholarship before turning a museum gloss into a universal law; no colonial “primitive survival” theory is accepted here.
- **Discredited shortcuts:** a proven Altaic genealogy, hereditary racial essence, “Confucianism has no religion,” “hangul was only women's writing,” and one timeless Chinese-to-Korean costume rank chart are not safe premises.
- **Thin knowledge flagged:** local Joseon spirit rosters, low-status everyday clothing, complete historical speech-level paradigms, exact state-rite prescriptions, and changing hat entitlements still require primary-text or specialist checking; the round-2 checks above give entry points (복식금제 for dress and color bans, 무가 by region, 중세국어 for speech levels, *Gukjo oryeui* recensions for state rites), not the last word.
- **Chronicle caution:** rich court documentation cannot supply every rural utterance or private rite. When evidence is scarce, narrow the claim or leave alternatives; do not fabricate an annal citation, a prayer, or a costume ordinance.
- **Next evidence packet:** choose a reign and locality; inspect the relevant *Joseon wangjo sillok* passage, ritual-manual recension, examination *bangmok*, and dated portrait/garment record. Record original text, translator, edition, object ID, and uncertainty before canon handoff.
