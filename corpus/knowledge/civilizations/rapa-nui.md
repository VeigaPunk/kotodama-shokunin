## Calibration: separate what survives from what is reconstructed

| Evidence layer | Can support | Cannot independently establish |
|---|---|---|
| Carved objects, quarry contexts, platforms, petroglyphs | Form, material, placement, relative sequence; dates with contextual limits | A unique divine identity, spoken wording, or complete ritual explanation |
| Late-recorded Rapa Nui testimony | Named traditions, remembered offices, accounts tied to places and families | An unchanged account of every earlier century |
| European visits and missionary records | Dated encounters and descriptions, after translation and observer criticism | An unmediated census of Indigenous beliefs or a neutral account of conversion |
| Modern grammar and recorded speech | Attested linguistic structures, contact layers, speaker variation | The precise language encoded by an undeciphered inscription |
| Genetic and agricultural models | Tests of particular demographic/contact hypotheses | Cultural superiority, language affiliation, or the intentions of historical actors |

- **Status vocabulary:** established observation; supported interpretation; contested reconstruction; `(to-verify)` for an unverified detail. “Reported by R20” is an attribution, not automatic historical confirmation.
- Rapa Nui is a living Indigenous society, not an extinct civilization whose survivors are merely sources about ruins.
- This entry is strongest on grammar and the calibration of writing/demography; thinner on contemporary community-led religious interpretation, women's ritual histories, and local variation in dress.
- Scarcity concerns especially contemporaneous evidence for pre-contact ritual speech and institutional origins; it does not mean Rapa Nui people lack knowledge of their own traditions.

## Language family, structure, and present writing

- **Affiliation:** Austronesian → Oceanic → Polynesian → Eastern Polynesian. Rapa Nui is not a language isolate. Its exact position within finer Eastern Polynesian subgroupings deserves qualification [K17 §§1.2.1–1.2.2].
- Geographic isolation and linguistic isolation are different variables; relatedness is established through comparative evidence, not distance or resemblance between a few words.

| Feature | Attested baseline [K17] | Coupling use; boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Native sound inventory | /p t k ʔ m n ŋ v h r/; five vowel qualities with short/long contrast; /r/ is a flap | Build names and ritual diction from one inventory; loan phonemes /f s/ require a contact layer |
| Syllables | Strongly restricted open syllables and metrical word constraints | Do not scatter consonant clusters into supposedly native names or use unrestricted random CV strings |
| Clause structure | Basic verb–subject–object order; extensive particle marking; predominantly isolating morphology | A small segment inventory does not imply a small grammar or impoverished thought |
| Pronouns | First-person inclusive/exclusive and dual/plural distinctions; no second-/third-person dual/plural contrast in K17's description | Decide which collectives include the hearer; do not import a generic “Polynesian” pronoun grid |
| Possession | ꞌa/o classes; “alienable/inalienable” is an inadequate complete gloss [§6.3.4.2] | Model usage categories and exceptions; clothing/land possession does not directly reveal a total cosmology |
| Modern orthography | Latin letters; conventions differ for /ŋ/, /ʔ/, vowel length, and word boundaries [§1.4.4] | Keep an orthography policy and source spellings; ng versus ŋ is not evidence of different ethnic groups |

- Contemporary Latin-script writing is not a decipherment or phonetic continuation demonstrated for rongorongo.
- K17 is based on particular corpora and fieldwork periods; its vitality estimates are historical observations, not a current speaker census.

## Isolation effects: mechanisms, not deterministic folklore

| Mechanism | Rapa Nui evidence or limit | Design consequence for a fictional culture |
|---|---|---|
| Reduced external contact | Relative isolation and substantial lexical innovation discussed in K17 §1.1.3 | Maintain inherited grammar alongside innovative vocabulary; do not equate distance with linguistic stasis |
| Internal transmission | A community still contains households, generations, specialists, and performance settings | Give chants, everyday talk, craft terms, and place-names different transmission paths |
| Catastrophic disruption | Raids, epidemics, displacement, and commercial/colonial coercion transformed transmission in the nineteenth century [K17 §1.1.3] | Loss of specialist knowledge requires a social mechanism, not a mystical “dying language” atmosphere |
| Tahitian contact | Borrowing through migration/remigration and later renewed contact; K17 §1.4.1 argues for more than one wave | Label donor language, earliest attestation, and register; apparently local words need not be ancient inheritances |
| Spanish contact | Borrowing, code-mixing, schooling and institutional domains prominent in modern speech [K17 §1.4.2] | Model bilingual speakers as agents, not degraded versions of an imagined pure language |
| Oceanic connectivity | MM24 supports pre-European Native American ancestry/contact | Reject absolute isolation as an axiom; DNA does not establish Quechua ancestry for the language or explain moai |

- **Concrete lexical control:** K17 contrasts older *maꞌa* ‘know’ with modern *ꞌite* borrowed from Tahitian; the everyday greeting *ꞌiorana* also belongs to the Tahitian contact layer.
- Do not use a modern greeting as proof of pre-contact ceremonial wording. Record lexical histories separately from the age of the practice being described.
- Small population size does not itself predict simplified syntax, universal mutual intimacy, or the absence of social differentiation.

## Rongorongo: an undeciphered calibration exercise

- **Established:** a distinctive Rapa Nui sign tradition survives on wooden objects; repeated linear sequences, ligatures, and corrections are observable. Outsiders documented it in 1864 [F24; K17 §1.6.2].
- **Layout:** alternating lines have inverted sign orientation, conventionally described as reverse boustrophedon; reading involves reorienting the object. Orientation is not a recovered pronunciation [R20 pp. 244–245].
- **Unknown:** a generally accepted full decipherment, stable sign-to-sound assignments, and complete textual translations. Logographic, phonographic, mixed, or mnemonic functions cannot be settled from pictorial appearance alone.
- F24 argues that structural features support language notation; distinguish that argument from proof of a particular encoding type.

| Temptation | Calibration | Safe working procedure |
|---|---|---|
| “Bird-shaped sign means bird” | An image can be semantic, phonetic, conventional, or composite | Separate drawn form, sign identity, hypothesized value, and textual interpretation |
| “A familiar ritual explains the whole tablet” | Contextual plausibility is not independent decipherment | Require the proposed values to work consistently beyond the chosen passage |
| “The wood dates to around 1500, so the text does too” | F24's early result concerns wood; reuse creates an old-wood problem | Treat wood age as a constraint preceding manufacture/engraving, not a direct inscription date |
| “First recorded in 1864 means invented then” | First outsider attestation is not origin | Keep invention date, object date, inscription date, collection date, and publication date distinct |
| “Unique appearance proves independent invention” | Independent invention versus contact stimulus remains debated | Record both hypotheses without denying Indigenous innovation under either scenario |
| “A numbered glyph catalogue is a dictionary” | Classification is not translation; corpus inclusion/counts also differ | Preserve object identifiers and transcription conventions; do not invent a consensus sign inventory |

- Claims of complete decipherment, including popularized readings, are **not established** merely because a book or automated system prints fluent translations.
- Specific phonetic values, full chants allegedly transcribed from a tablet, and a chosen Mamari calendrical reading require source-by-source verification [F24]; none is used here as settled knowledge.
- **Fictional application:** decide privately whether your invented script encodes language, cues memorized performance, or combines both; then define what observers can actually demonstrate. Do not “translate” real rongorongo to supply invented lore.

## Mythic and pantheon core: preserve categories and variants

| Attested narrative/category | Reference anchor | Coupling opportunity; caution |
|---|---|---|
| Hotu Matuꞌa, remembered founding/migrant chief | R20 ch. XVIII; K17 includes narratives about his arrival | Arrival, ancestry, place, and authority can reinforce one another; a named founder is not a securely dated reign |
| Makemake / Make-make, divine figure linked with Birdman accounts | R20 bird-cult narrative; K17 contains a Makemake narrative example | Associate a ritual with named divine agency without reducing the whole religion to one god |
| Aku-aku, spirits associated with persons/places in late-recorded accounts | R20 pp. 236–238 | Local supernatural relations can organize geography and obligations; not a universal species of “demons” |
| Ariki and ancestral authority | R20 pp. 240–243; KW02 on ancestral chiefs and moai | Distinguish chiefly descent, ritual restriction, living officeholder, and ancestral image |

- Do not build an Olympian-style chart assigning each figure one exclusive “portfolio”; these sources do not warrant a complete, timeless pantheon table.
- Makemake's precise creation episodes and Haua's relationships vary across reported versions and are not reconstructed here; name the text, narrator, collection date, and variant before using any single account [M40].
- R20 explicitly describes Christian-era changes in naming supernatural beings; its translations “god,” “spirit,” and “devil” are not interchangeable Indigenous categories.
- **Method:** index each story as narrator → collector/interpreter → collection date → named places/actors → variant differences. Keep mythic time distinct from an archaeological chronology.
- Treat ritual as evidence in its own right; a narrative and a ceremony need not preserve a single unchanged origin story.

## Moai: ancestral presence, not an interchangeable “idol” motif

- **Supported interpretation:** monumental moai represent ancestral chiefly figures whose power benefited communities [KW02]. “Every moai depicts a securely identified named individual” is a stronger claim, not established here.
- Ahu are platforms/ceremonial settings, not merely statue plinths; associations with burial and ancestors matter [KW02; R20].
- Coastal platform moai generally face inland toward communities [KW02]; do not turn a common pattern into an exceptionless rule for every statue and context.

| Material/sign relationship | What the evidence supports | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|
| Volcanic tuff and Rano Raraku quarrying | A specific material landscape and specialist production [KW02] | That every figure uses the same stone or that one transport method is settled |
| Enlarged head, emphatic face, frontal body | A repeated formal vocabulary [KW02] | A universal cross-cultural code in which “large head = intellect” |
| Red-stone pukao on some figures | Head treatment; headdress/hairstyle interpretations [KW02] | A literal hat pattern that every living person wore, or a fixed color dictionary |
| Ancestor image + locality + platform | Durable public presence linked with community and descent | A simple equation between greater statue size and an automatically measurable political rank |
| Fallen, relocated, or restored figure | Object biographies and later interventions require context | A single island-wide date when everyone rejected the same belief |

- Specific coral-eye reconstructions and identification of a particular moai's ancestor require object-level references before use in a factual reconstruction [MOAI-EYES].
- **Fictional application:** derive who commissions, carves, tends, addresses, and interprets an ancestor image; couple their vocabulary and obligations before choosing monumental scale.

## Tangata manu: ecological season, delegated risk, restricted office

- **Terminology:** *tangata manu* ‘bird-man’; *manu tara/manutara*, the sooty tern in the recorded rite; *hopu*, the egg-fetcher serving a candidate/sponsor [R20 glossary and pp. 254–266]. Orthographic forms vary by source.
- **Places:** Orongo, on the Rano Kau rim, overlooks the offshore islets; Motu Nui is central to the egg-fetching account. Terrain is part of the institution, not background scenery.
- **Core account, late-recorded:** a seasonal contest to obtain the first tern egg; contestants' representatives wait on the islet and return the egg, while the successful sponsor acquires Birdman status [R20].

| Link in the ritual chain | Language/material coupling | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Seasonal seabird return | Bird terminology, waiting, food provisioning, a recurring calendar event | Do not force the ceremony into a modern fixed-date holiday |
| Candidate and hopu | Distinct role names; the person taking the risk is not automatically the person receiving office | Preserve delegation; “winner becomes king” erases institutional distinctions |
| Egg retrieval and announcement | Fragile physical object, sea crossing, communication back to the settlement | Details come through testimony, not surviving recordings of the rite |
| Recognition and restriction | Status accompanied by separation and taboo observances | Sacred privilege need not mean unrestricted executive power |
| Birdman petroglyphs | Human–bird bodies and place-specific carving | R20 itself distinguishes observed figures from proposed explanations of whom each commemorates |

- R20 reports a red tapa fragment tied around the returning hopu's arm with accompanying words, and the egg handled on tapa: a concrete coupling of role, speech, cloth, and sacred object [pp. 262–264]. Treat it as a reported sequence, not a universal costume prescription.
- A clean “ancestor worship ended, Birdman replaced it in year X” model exceeds the evidence used here; duration, overlap, and political consequences require separate dating.
- R20 distinguishes the end of assemblies at Orongo from later continuation of altered egg rites. A single last-ceremony date is therefore unsafe without specifying the practice being dated.
- **Fictional application:** link animal ecology → seasonal opportunity → delegated hazard → public recognition → restricted status → emblem. Invent your own ecological and social particulars rather than copying sacred names and imagery wholesale.

## Era model: overlapping processes, not rise–collapse chapters

| Working historical slice | Anchor and confidence | Language / symbol / material staging |
|---|---|---|
| Settlement and early community formation | Late settlement around the twelfth–thirteenth centuries is supported in recent syntheses; F24 gives 1150–1280 CE, MM24 approximately 1250 | Inherited Polynesian resources plus local innovation; do not use early dates from KW02 without rechecking |
| Monument production and changing communities | Quarry/platform evidence; site-specific sequences needed | Carving and ancestral display vary; do not project one ceremonial wardrobe across the whole period |
| Birdman institutions and monument histories | Material remains plus later testimony; relative transitions debated | Stage possible coexistence and institutional change; no automatic linguistic replacement |
| European encounters from 1722 | Dated visits; descriptions are selective [K17; R20] | New observers, goods, names, and documentary filters enter; the encounter is not the beginning of local history |
| Raids, epidemics, missionization, displacement in the 1860s–1870s | Strong historical evidence for severe disruption [K17; MM24] | Breaks in transmission, new religious institutions, migration and remigration; survivors remain historical agents |
| Annexation in 1888; ranch/naval rule and later civil transformation | K17 §1.1.3; locally specific colonial chronology | Commercial/administrative constraints and later expanded mobility alter language domains and material access |
| Contemporary community and diaspora | K17 documents recent language/contact settings, not the present year's census | Bilingualism, revitalization, artistic practice, and heritage claims are living processes, not a “post-culture” appendix |

- **Contested/discredited as a default explanatory package:** inevitable “ecocide → famine/cannibalism → total pre-European collapse.” Environmental change is real; the whole causal chain is not established by that fact.
- D24 revises agricultural extent/population assumptions; MM24 rejects a severe seventeenth-century bottleneck in its tested demographic models. Neither proves there was no hardship, conflict, or ecological damage anywhere.
- Chronicle-era catastrophe must not be back-projected into an invented pre-contact “dark age.” Keep environmental, demographic, religious, political, and linguistic clocks separate.

## Costume and material signature: reconstruct by context

| Item/practice | Evidence and era caution | Craft implication |
|---|---|---|
| Paper-mulberry barkcloth, commonly called tapa | R20 pp. 218–219 describes loincloths and wraps; KW02 includes barkcloth art | Beaten barkcloth is not woven cloth; give production its own tools, labor, repair and vocabulary |
| Feather headgear | R20 records several forms, special occasions, and remembered/reconstructed examples | Specify wearer, occasion, feather supply, storage, and maker; not one generic “tribal crown” |
| Tattooing | R20 pp. 219–220 combines earlier visitor descriptions and later recollection | A visible body system is attested; exact motif meanings and rank assignments need separate sources |
| Wooden images, ornaments, dance paddles | KW02; R20 glossary and accounts | Separate worn, carried, displayed, and sacred-handling contexts; not every portable carving is an amulet |
| Tuff, basalt tools, red stone | KW02 on carving and head treatments | Map material sourcing and skilled work without deriving a whole caste system from the tools |
| Rock gardens / lithic mulching | D24: stones modify cultivation conditions and available nutrients/moisture | A stone-rich visual environment can be agricultural technology, not only monumental religion |

- KW02 cautions that virtually all surviving non-stone artworks in its overview date to the eighteenth–nineteenth centuries; survivorship does not license a complete earlier costume atlas.
- R20's “sole form of dress” and “universal” tattooing are broad observer claims; use the positively described materials/practices without treating such absolutes as exhaustive facts.
- Rank, gender, age, everyday labor, ritual office, and tourist/performance contexts must be separately sourced. Detailed pre-contact garment construction and motif-by-clan mappings are not established by the sources consulted here.
- **Material-to-language worksheet:** object → raw material → maker/action term → permitted handler → occasion → repair/disposal → attestation date. Missing cells are research gaps, not permission to invent Rapa Nui facts.

## Naming practices: spoken names, titles, and collector spellings

- Separate **personal names**, **chiefly/ritual titles**, **descent-group designations**, **place-names**, and **modern civil names**. One list cannot safely stand in for the others.
- *Hotu Matuꞌa* is a named traditional founder; *ariki* denotes chiefly status; *tangata manu* is a ritual designation. Do not mechanically turn titles into inherited surnames [R20; K17].
- K17 §1.4.4 discusses phrasal proper-name spelling: **Rapa Nui**, **Haŋa Roa**; older/common **Hanga Roa** reflects another representation of /ŋ/, not necessarily another spoken form.
- Toponymic elements in R20's glossary include *hanga* ‘bay/foreshore’, *motu* ‘islet’, and *maunga* ‘hill’. Use these as attested categories, not a guarantee that every place-name has a transparent literal translation.
- K17 notes that **Rapa Nui**, literally ‘great Rapa’, may be a nineteenth-century name; the exact naming history is qualified, not a securely attested primordial self-name.
- Preserve source spelling, normalized spelling, pronunciation evidence, referent type, narrator/community attribution, and earliest attestation in separate fields.
- Do not extract names from unverified rongorongo “translations,” treat glottal marks as decoration, or infer a historical naming taboo merely from missing names in one collector's list.
- **Fictional application:** generate native names with your conlang's phonology and morphology, then layer titles, exonyms, mission/civil names, and spelling reforms according to dated institutions. No fictional names or canon are assigned here.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Coupling pattern:** persistent ancestral places and images can coexist with annually renewed, bodily restricted offices; durable and seasonal authority need not share the same transmission system.
- **Design constraint:** language, myth, emblem, era, and dress should meet at a named practice: who says what, handles which material, in which place/season, under which restriction.
- **Reusable comparison:** public monument versus specialist tablet; inherited authority versus recurring recognition; vernacular speech versus transmitted recitation; locally made cloth versus introduced goods. These are design questions, not fixed Rapa Nui binaries.
- **Work sequence:** choose a fictional ecological condition → identify social roles → define speech and transmission → select materials and emblems → stage changes across eras → check contradictions.
- **Consistency test:** can one explain a headpiece, title, ancestor image, and seasonal term without using incompatible eras or pretending an undeciphered text supplies the missing explanation?
- Hand lore-shokunin a proposal with evidence tier, source, analogy-versus-invention labels, era alternatives, and unresolved questions. Lore decides whether anything enters its world bible.

## Provenance cautions and stop conditions

- R20 preserves valuable Rapa Nui testimony but frames it through colonial-era racial/evolutionary assumptions, interpretation, collecting, and Christian-era categories. Attribute narrators such as Te Haha and collaborators such as Juan Tepano where recoverable; do not erase them behind the collector's name.
- Missionary “demon” language, outsider claims of religious absence, and sensational cannibalism stories require contextual criticism; they are not neutral Indigenous self-descriptions.
- KW02 is a dated museum synthesis, not a current chronological authority; do not repeat its early settlement dates or asserted transport solution simply because the institution is reputable.
- F24's wood dates do not settle inscription age or independent invention. MM24's ancestry results do not identify a language donor, a migrating dynasty, or the direction and itinerary of a specific voyage.
- This entry does not adjudicate custodianship or return of objects/human remains. Museum possession and online availability do not establish community consent to reproduce sacred designs or practices.
- Present community perspectives, women's accounts, detailed costume chronologies, individual moai identities, and claimed textual readings need further named, checkable sources; retain `(to-verify)` rather than filling the gaps with generic Polynesian material.
- Stop a factual reconstruction where its next step needs a deciphered tablet, an exact origin date for Birdman rites, or a universal clan-color/garment code that the cited evidence does not supply. Fiction may invent those things, but must label the invention and must not present it as recovered Rapa Nui knowledge.
