## 1. Scope and calibration

- Base: the Cairo-centered sultanate of Egypt and Syria, conventionally 1250–1517; neither all societies using mamluk soldiers nor an ethnically homogeneous “civilization.”
- **Institution, not species of person:** Arabic *mamlūk* means “owned,” plural *mamālīk*; it names a condition/origin that could persist as a military identity after emancipation.
- The ruling elite was not the whole population: civilian scholars, scribes, artisans, merchants, cultivators, women, and religious minorities require separate evidence tracks (Petry 2022).
- **Evidence labels:** established = well-supported institutional outline; variable = changes with date, household, or locality; debated = competing readings; `(to-verify)` = do not use as a settled historical detail.
- **Craft labels:** “design move” below means an analytical extrapolation, not a claim that every Mamluk household behaved that way and not a proposed canon decision.
- Prefer a dated object + inscription + documentary context over a floating “Mamluk aesthetic.” A Cairo monument does not automatically describe an Aleppine household or a rural wardrobe.

## 2. Era model — stage institutions, not racial replacements

| Stage | Historical anchor | Coupling to model | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ayyubid antecedents, before 1250 | military slave households already operate; al-Ṣāliḥ Ayyūb's recruits matter to the transition | imported recruits + patron households + existing Arabic institutions | military slavery began with the Mamluk sultanate |
| Formation, 1250–1290 | Baybars rules 1260–1277; Qalāwūn 1279–1290; Mongol and crusader wars | military legitimacy + court reorganization + endowed monuments | an already fixed hereditary nobility or one uniform constitution |
| Qalawunid ascendancy and succession, 1290–1382 | al-Nāṣir Muḥammad's third reign 1310–1341; descendants remain central afterward | dynastic succession coexists with household competition; Arabic–Turkic grammars and technical writing | sons never rule because recruitment is nonhereditary |
| Later sultanate, conventionally 1382–1517 | Barqūq's accession; Qāytbāy 1468–1496; al-Ghawrī 1501–1516 | Circassian recruitment prominence + composite household blazons + continued multilingual patronage | every soldier becomes Circassian, or Turkic disappears overnight |
| Ottoman conquest, 1516–1517, and afterlives | conquest ends this sovereign sultanate | regime boundary differs from persistence of households, buildings, and names | mamluk institutions and material habits cease immediately |

- “Baḥrī”/“Burjī” and “Turkish”/“Circassian” are useful conventional period labels, not interchangeable biological descriptions; late fourteenth-century transitions include interruptions and continuities (Petry 2022).
- Add independent clocks: patronage sequence, plague episodes beginning in 1347–1348, recruitment routes, manuscript copying, garment fashion, and commercial change. They need not turn together.
- Reject the pop ending “honorable horsemen refused guns and therefore vanished”: firearms, finance, logistics, command, and Ottoman expansion need separate assessment; cavalry identity is not a complete causal explanation.

## 3. Military-household institution — the generative mechanism

| Term / relation | Working meaning | Design leverage | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| *mamlūk* / emancipation | purchased enslaved recruit; training and manumission can precede an elite career | legal status and continuing household identity can diverge | ruling amirs were not simply unfreed property throughout their careers |
| patron / former master | purchase, education, support, advancement, and continuing affiliation | allegiance vocabulary can preserve an earlier owner after promotion | not identical to biological fatherhood |
| *khushdāsh*, *khushdāshiyya* | fellow-mamluk relationship / solidarity linked to a shared master | cohort loyalty cuts across blood descent | solidarity can fail; it is not an automatic voting bloc |
| *ustādh* | master/teacher; context can identify the household patron | an apparently pedagogical title can encode political dependence | distinguish ordinary teaching from military-household usage |
| *amīr*, plural *umarāʾ* | commander; grades and offices structure precedence | separate numerical military grade, court office, and household membership | do not translate every amir as an autonomous hereditary “prince” |
| *awlād al-nās* | freeborn descendants of the military elite | inherited wealth, education, and connections without repeating enslavement | not proof that all descendants were excluded from power or arms |
| *iqṭāʿ* | assignment of revenue rights connected with service | a horseman's resources can come from distant agricultural revenue | not automatically private ownership of land or a European fief |
| *waqf*, plural *awqāf* | legally constituted endowment | connect property income, family strategy, service, and commemorative memory | not synonymous with *iqṭāʿ* |

- Institutional outline: Petry (2022). Recruitment age, training length, emancipation timing, and the strength of cohort bonds vary; never impose a single initiation syllabus on the whole period.
- **Status transition chain:** enslavement → household formation/training → emancipation/service → office/patronage → commemoration; actual lives can branch, stall, or end violently.
- **Ethical constraint:** an elite career does not cancel coercion, sale, dislocation, or the experiences of people who never reached elite status. “Slave meritocracy” is a sanitizing slogan, not an adequate model.
- Do not make every dependent a soldier: domestic enslavement, women's trajectories, eunuchs, and nonmilitary service demand their own sources; this entry's coverage there is thin.

## 4. Language families, registers, and writing

| Stratum | Family / written evidence | Social placement to investigate | Fictional coupling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arabic | Semitic, within Afroasiatic; Arabic script | chancery, religious and legal learning, chronicles, urban speech, inscriptions | documentary prestige can belong to a language other than recruits' first languages |
| Kipchak Turkic | Turkic; Arabic-script grammars, dictionaries, translations | important recruitment and military-cultural connections, especially in earlier periods | recurring recruitment can replenish a minority prestige repertoire |
| Oghuz / other Turkic inputs | Turkic, but not simply identical to Kipchak; manuscript varieties mix features | migration, translation, literary contact, changing Turkic usage | stage innovations by text and community rather than relabeling everything “Turkish” |
| Circassian languages | Northwest Caucasian, not Turkic; much less direct vernacular evidence here | Caucasian recruitment and private/social networks, especially in the later period | a recruit's first language need not become the state's documentary language |
| Persianate terminology | Persian is Iranian, Indo-European; borrowed office/cultural terms can circulate through Arabic and Turkic | titles, literary prestige, administrative and material vocabulary | lexical ancestry does not prove every officeholder speaks Persian |

- **Do not count Kipchak, Turkish, and Circassian as one language family.** “Turkish” in older scholarship may mean Turkic broadly, not the modern standard language of Turkey.
- **Abū Ḥayyān's test case:** *al-Idrāk*, completed in Cairo in 712 AH / 1312 CE, presents Turkic vocabulary and grammar through an Arabic grammatical framework (Develi 2002; Ermers 1999).
- Develi describes its variety as Kipchak with Oghuz features and notes explicit distinctions between Kipchak and Turkmen usages. The manuscript is evidence of analysis and contact, not a transparent recording of every barracks.
- The grammar's existence supports cross-language learning; it does **not** establish universal elite bilingualism, a single official spoken language, or an empire-wide standardized “Mamluk Kipchak.”
- Arabic-script Turkic requires conventions for sounds not represented straightforwardly by Arabic spelling; distinguish manuscript spelling, editorial normalization, and reconstructed pronunciation.
- Written Arabic and local spoken Arabic likewise are not one undifferentiated register. Do not invent period Cairene dialogue by simply using modern Egyptian Arabic without diachronic checking.
- **Thin area:** Circassian everyday speech, code-switching patterns, and household-specific pronunciation are poorly represented by the sources used here. Exact dialogue or a uniform Circassian military lexicon is `(to-verify)`.
- **Design move:** assign first language, drill repertoire, petition language, religious literacy, and public inscription language independently to each social position; then test who needs an interpreter.

## 5. Naming — a social biography, not a random name generator

| Name component | Historical function | Reader question |
|---|---|---|
| personal name, often non-Arabic among recruits | can preserve a Turkic or other recruitment-world name in Arabic transcription | which edition normalizes its vowels and consonants? |
| *ibn* + father's name | genealogical identification; *ibn ʿAbd Allāh* also occurs in mamluk naming | does this text record ancestry, a conventional formula, or incomplete knowledge? |
| *laqab*, e.g. Rukn al-Dīn / Sayf al-Dīn | honorific such as “pillar of religion” / “sword of religion” | personal honorific, office title, or later narrator's shorthand? |
| *al-Malik* + regnal epithet | royal titulature: al-Ẓāhir, al-Manṣūr, al-Ashraf, etc. | title reused by several rulers, or unique identifier? |
| relational *nisba*, e.g. al-Ṣāliḥī / al-Ẓāhirī | can identify affiliation to a patron and his household | who is the referent, and at what career stage? |
| place / occupation / office designation | another attachment layered onto a person's public identity | birthplace, residence, actual office, or inherited designation? |

- Baybars combines the royal style *al-Malik al-Ẓāhir*, the honorific Rukn al-Dīn, a personal name, and *al-Bunduqdārī*, a designation tied to an earlier patron's title; it is not a modern surname (Petry 2022).
- Patron-derived affiliation is not interchangeable with ethnicity; an Arabic-looking name does not establish an Arabic first language, nor a Turkic name a living command of Turkic.
- Do not translate a personal name from a modern Turkish resemblance without an attested historical form; specific name etymologies not established here are `(to-verify)`.
- **Design move:** maintain separate fields for birth name, sale/household affiliation, honorific, regnal title, document spelling, and street abbreviation. Let a promotion change some fields while leaving an older allegiance legible.

## 6. Religion and narrative repertoire — not a pantheon

- **Religious core:** Sunni Muslim rulership in a religiously diverse society; Qurʾanic revelation, the Prophet Muḥammad, law, prayer, pilgrimage, pious patronage, and scholarly authority are load-bearing (Petry 2022).
- This is not a pantheon slot-map: God, prophets, angels, jinn, and saints occupy different theological categories. A saint is not a lesser god; speaking Turkic does not imply an intact pre-Islamic steppe pantheon.
- Baybars's restoration of an Abbasid caliphal presence in Cairo in 1261 supplied a legitimating relationship, not the transfer of all governing power to that caliph.
- Protection of holy places and pilgrimage, victories against external enemies, and provision for learning can be narrated together as evidence of rightful rule; inscriptions are interested claims, not neutral performance audits.
- Sufi institutions, saintly *baraka*, visitation, and disputes about devotional practice need local and author-specific treatment; “official Islam versus folk superstition” is too crude a two-box model.
- Coptic and other Christians and Jewish communities are not background decoration. Legal restriction, administrative employment, patronage, and episodes of persecution cannot be flattened into either timeless harmony or uninterrupted uniform oppression.
- *Sīrat Baybars* belongs to the popular heroic narrative repertoire; separate its layered storytelling from a contemporary chronicle and from the historical ruler's biography. Dating a particular episode to his lifetime is unsupported without a manuscript/recension study: Brockelmann classifies the *Sīrat al-Ẓāhir* among the Arabic popular romances that professional storytellers kept reworking into modern times (Brockelmann 2017, vol. 2).
- **Design move:** give military prestige, charitable patronage, and religious endorsement different custodians; a ruler may possess one and still have to negotiate the others.

## 7. Furūsiyya — technical knowledge with a transmission history

- **Named framework:** al-Sarraf (2004:142–144) separates **thematic treatises** on particular disciplines from **general compilations**; general “manuals” are not the entire literature.
- His transmission model distinguishes earlier, substantially Abbasid materials from Mamluk copying, recombination, and original contributions; a manuscript's copying date is not the invention date of its techniques.

| Knowledge cluster | Attested literary/practical concern | Coupled design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| horses and veterinary knowledge | conformation, training, care, disease, farriery | stable personnel and diagnostic vocabulary are part of the military institution |
| riding and mounted weapons | lance, archery, close combat, coordination | drills generate imperatives, equipment terms, and reputations for mastery |
| training on foot | archery, weapon handling, wrestling and related exercises | a cavalry-centered elite still has dismounted training, not a horse-only identity |
| hunting and polo | courtly recreation also maintains skills and public distinction | sporting office, equipment, ceremony, and martial competence can overlap |
| military technology / organization | technical and tactical knowledge beyond riding alone | avoid reducing learned warfare to individual swordsmanship |

- **Checkable manuscript anchor:** Muḥammad ibn ʿĪsā ibn Ismāʿīl al-Ḥanafī al-Aqṣarāʾī, *Nihāyat al-sūl wa-l-umniyya fī taʿlīm aʿmāl al-furūsiyya*, British Library Add. 18866; identified in al-Sarraf (2004:141, 154).
- **Method:** record author, attributed author, title variants, copy date, older source material, intended reader, and practical corroboration separately; anonymous and misattributed works are a known problem (al-Sarraf 2004:143).
- Do not read a normative drill book as proof that every unit performed it, or an illustrated maneuver as a measured record of combat frequency.
- Furūsiyya can include virtue and courtliness, but translating it simply as European “chivalry” hides technical, veterinary, and institutional content; similarity is not identity.
- **Design move:** produce a fictional technical-register specification: who teaches, what is oral, what is written, which old authorities are cited, and how trainers correct an obsolete manuscript instruction. No historical command phrases are fabricated here.

## 8. Blazons — office, person, patron, and recognition

- “Rank blazons” must not be mistaken for a universal rank ladder. The emblem term often transliterated *rank* is not the English word for numerical military grade.
- Mayer (1933), Meinecke (1972), and Gonnella's Aleppo record support a distinction between office-associated devices and later composite emblems shared by followers of a particular master.

| Device | Conventional association | Safe reading / limit |
|---|---|---|
| stemmed cup | *sāqī*, cupbearer | court-service association; not “every cup = a lieutenant” |
| pen box / inkwell apparatus | *dawādār*, inkwell-holder/secretarial office | administrative access can be represented through its tool |
| “napkin” / cloth-bundle device | *jāmdār*, master of robes | conventional device name, not a secure garment cutting pattern |
| several devices in horizontal compartments | composite blazon, notably in the later period | can signal household/corps affiliation, not the wearer's simultaneous possession of every represented office |
| royal animal or epigraphic device | ruler-specific visual/titulary assertion | identify the ruler and object before assigning a zoological or religious interpretation |

- **Object test:** Aleppo inv. 533 has an upper “napkin,” central cup charged with a pen box, flanking forms described as possible powder horns, and a lower cup (Gonnella n.d.).
- Its original provenance is unknown and its dating is comparative; a proposed association with particular Aleppo patrons is not a secure excavation context.
- The record relates this widespread composition to Qāytbāy's circle, following Meinecke. Do not convert that interpretation into one legally standardized, timeless “national flag.”
- The flanking devices are described only as “what has been identified as a pair of powder horns” in the Aleppo record; using them as proof of a particular firearm unit or invention date is unsupported by that record.
- Blazons occur on architecture, metalwork, textiles, glass, and pottery: they connect household membership, sponsorship, service, and conspicuous material production.
- Do not assume European hereditary arms, tincture rules, a matching European spoken blazon grammar, or a one-to-one mapping of motif and biological lineage.
- **Design move:** specify issuer, authorized bearer, carrier, viewer, recognition distance, penalty for false use, and rules of succession. These are design questions, not claims that a surviving Mamluk ordinance answers all of them.

## 9. Costume and material signatures — dated ensembles, not ethnic uniforms

| Carrier / term | What to track | Coupling and caution |
|---|---|---|
| *qabāʾ*, outer coat; *sirwāl*, trousers | layer, fabric, fastening, riding movement | terms circulate across regions; a name alone does not reconstruct a cut |
| *kalawta*, cap; turban forms | shape, wrapping, court setting, date, office | headgear is politically legible but changes; do not assign one silhouette to three centuries |
| *khilʿa*, robe of honor / investiture ensemble | donor, recipient, occasion, textile, accompanying items | clothing performs a relationship, not merely personal taste |
| belt, boots, arms, horse equipment | commissioned quality, maintenance, inscription, use context | stable and workshop labor support the visible warrior |
| blazon-bearing cloth and equipment | owner, household, maker, object function | portable insignia connects body, horse, household, and building |
| inlaid metal, enamelled glass, carved stone/wood | inscription, patronage, production, reuse | recognizable prestige media, not exclusive “ethnic fingerprints” |

- Costume references: Mayer (1952), calibrated against Stillman (2003); monuments and endowed furnishings: Behrens-Abouseif (2007). Treat a philological clothing description, an image, and a surviving textile as different evidence types.
- No universal rank-color chart is established here; exact cap proportions, fur grades, permitted fabrics, and office-specific ensembles require a dated passage or object and are `(to-verify)` if asserted without one.
- Non-Muslim dress restrictions must be dated and distinguished from enforcement and daily practice; a decree is not a street photograph.
- Coverage is strongest for elite male display and surviving prestige objects; women's clothing, working dress, laundering, and rural variation are thinner here. Do not fill those gaps with later Ottoman or nineteenth-century orientalist costume plates.
- **Design move:** describe an investiture as a linked event: title announced → robe conferred → insignia recognized → household allegiance displayed → patron's name preserved in an inscription or narrative.

## 10. Waqf-city vocabulary — law becomes urban form

| Word / form | Working gloss in this context | Coupled system |
|---|---|---|
| *waqf* / *awqāf* | endowment / endowments | property, specified beneficiaries, and continuing provision |
| *wāqif*; *waqfiyya* | founder; endowment deed | authorizing person versus documentary instrument |
| *nāẓir* | administrator/supervisor | management, appointments, maintenance, accounts |
| *madrasa* | institution of learned instruction, especially religious/legal study | teaching posts, students, stipends, patron's memory |
| *khānqāh*; *zāwiya* | institutions associated with Sufi life, with variable functions | residence, devotion, teaching, neighborhood networks |
| *bīmāristān* / *māristān* | hospital; a Persian-derived term used in Arabic | endowed care, staff, medical learning; not a modern hospital in every respect |
| *sabīl*; *kuttāb* | public water provision; elementary school | civic service, children, water labor; combined forms must be dated |
| *khān*, *wakāla*, *rabʿ*, shops | commercial/storage/residential properties, with local terminological variation | income-generating property may support a separate service complex |
| *qibla*, *miḥrāb*, *minbar*, *īwān* | prayer orientation, niche, pulpit, vaulted open-front hall | distinguish orientation, fixture, furnishing, and spatial form |

- Use Behrens-Abouseif (2007) to connect foundation, ceremonial visibility, deed, revenue property, and service; do not define a city solely by a list of picturesque building types.
- **The waqf circuit:** designated revenue assets → rents or agricultural income → administration/maintenance → staff and beneficiaries → ritual/service and founder's commemoration.
- A shop can finance a school elsewhere; the endowed entity is not necessarily one enclosed architectural compound. Ownership, income, residence, and public access are different map layers.
- Family benefit, property strategy, public service, and pious intention can coexist. “Pure charity” versus “nothing but tax evasion” is a false binary; legal conditions and actual performance need documents.
- A deed prescribes salaries, beneficiaries, and activities; it does not by itself prove uninterrupted payment or that users matched the founder's plan.
- **Design move:** map a hypothetical foundation by income source, accountant's language, appointment authority, water/repair labor, public inscription, recitation or teaching schedule, and succession dispute; leave outcomes undecided for lore-shokunin.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take — coupled procedures, not a costume kit

1. Separate imported recruit identity, emancipation, freeborn descent, household allegiance, court office, and military grade; test where their rights diverge.
2. Build a register matrix rather than one national tongue: home speech, drill, legal petition, learned text, public inscription, and trade each get speakers and transmission routes.
3. Generate names from career layers; let an affiliation survive the patron who created it, and let regnal titles recur without confusing individuals.
4. Build a horsecraft tradition from specialist knowledge, oral coaching, compilations, veterinary work, and older authorities; distinguish prestige from demonstrated competence.
5. Link an office's tool to an emblem, the emblem to authorized carriers, and those carriers to an actual patronage network; do not pretend every badge is a military grade.
6. Couple title, clothing gift, public ceremony, and urban commemoration; material display should reveal who pays and who can recognize the claim.
7. Give foundations revenue and staff as well as domes; connect charity, religion, household succession, and neighborhood use through enforceable or contested documents.
8. Handoff only design variables, source-backed analogies, era constraints, and uncertainty labels. lore-shokunin selects canon and owns world-bible consistency.

## 12. Provenance cautions and open gaps

- **Chronicle bias:** elite and learned male authors are comparatively visible; recruits' early lives, private Circassian conversations, ordinary labor, women's experiences, and unsuccessful careers are less recoverable from this source set.
- **Institutional-model debate:** older depictions of a permanently foreign, perfectly bounded military caste are heuristics, not axioms; dynasties, kinship, civilian connections, and individual careers complicate them (Petry 2022).
- **Colonial/European comparison bias:** retain Mayer's historical title *Saracenic Heraldry* for retrieval, not as an ethnic category; European coats of arms and “chivalry” are comparisons to test, not default organizing laws.
- **Preservation bias:** surviving mosques, luxury objects, and copied manuals overrepresent patronage and durable prestige; they do not establish the normal outfit, technical literacy, or wealth of the whole population.
- **Manuscript caution:** al-Sarraf (2004) explicitly identifies reused earlier material, unstable titles, and misattribution; use his article as a research map, not proof that its 2004 publication-status observations remain current.
- **Edition caution:** Develi (2002) distinguishes manuscripts, the faulty first printing, Caferoğlu's 1931 edition, and Ermers's translation; do not merge an editor's normalized spelling with a manuscript reading.
- **Known thinness:** exact Circassian linguistic repertoires, household-specific dress regulations, and the chronology of individual popular-epic motifs remain unresolved here. Mark further particulars `(to-verify)` until checked against specialist editions.
- **Verification scope:** bibliography/edition identities and selected institutional pages were checked; al-Sarraf's open article was consulted directly. The books listed are reference editions, not a claim that every page was newly inspected; no invented quotations or page-level precision are supplied for them.
