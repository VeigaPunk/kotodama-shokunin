## Scope and calibration
- “Aksum,” “Ethiopia,” “Ethiopian Orthodox,” and “Geʿez-using communities” are overlapping, not interchangeable categories.
- Aksum's northern-Horn heartland includes territory now in Ethiopia and Eritrea; modern national borders are not ancient cultural boundaries. [MH; DP]
- This entry concentrates on Christian highland institutions; it is not a description of every Ethiopian or Eritrean language, religion, costume, or naming system.
- Geʿez also serves traditions outside the Ethiopian Orthodox Church; do not infer identical theology or ritual from a shared language. Their comparative treatment remains thin here (to-verify).
- Evidence labels: **attested** = inscription, object, or documented practice; **interpretation** = scholarly explanation; **tradition** = community account, not automatically independently dated history; **transfer** = proposed design method, not an Ethiopian historical claim.
- “Liturgical fossil” is usable only for retention of older forms: **not** for a motionless culture, perfect preservation of ancient pronunciation, or an inability to compose new work.
- Keep archaeological chronology, manuscript transmission, liturgical tradition, and present-day ethnography as separate evidence tracks.

## Language family and the productive “fossil”
- Geʿez, also called Classical Ethiopic, is an Ethiopian Semitic language within Semitic/Afroasiatic. Language relationship and the ancestry of letterforms are different questions. [TL; WL]
- Do not describe Geʿez as Sabaic written differently or as the automatic direct ancestor of every modern Ethiopian Semitic language; a detailed subgrouping requires specialist linguistic evidence (to-verify here).
- Retention of Geʿez in worship coexists with vernacular communication and teaching. “Old sacred language versus modern everyday language” is a register relationship, not two isolated civilizations. [HD]
- The date of the loss of ordinary native transmission is not pinned here (to-verify); no precise “death year” should be used as an era boundary.
- New composition, grammatical instruction, biblical interpretation, copying, and musical performance keep the learned language productive. Memorized recitation and understanding are not the same attainment. [HD]

| Register or competence | Institutional work | Coupled consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Elementary reading/recitation | Nebab Bet trains reading and prayers; comprehension need not precede performance | A fluent ritual reader need not be an independent interpreter. [HD] |
| Liturgical specialization | Qedasse Bet trains deacon/priest functions | Ritual authorization is not simply the highest available literary skill. [HD] |
| Geʿez composition | Qene Bet teaches grammar, vocabulary, models, and sung poetry | Innovation can certify mastery of tradition rather than rejection of it. [HD] |
| Exegesis | Metsehaf Bet studies texts and commentaries; HD describes translation into Amharic | A vernacular explanation can transmit the authority of a nonvernacular text. [HD] |
| Royal/monumental display | Aksumite inscriptions and coin legends use Greek and Geʿez in differing contexts | Script, language, and audience must each be recorded separately. [MH, §§9, 11.5, 13.1] |

- **Transfer:** define who can pronounce, parse, compose, interpret, and authorize a text; give each ability its own route of acquisition.
- **Do not transfer:** the stereotype that every sacred-language specialist is a priest, magician, or member of a single hereditary caste. HD distinguishes learned debtera from sacramental office.

## Ethiopic script invention: adaptation is not lack of agency
- Ethiopic letterforms have historical connections with South Arabian writing; the exact route through monumental/cursive forms is debated. Local selection, modification, and vocalization are developments, not a claim of creation without antecedents. [MH, §13.1]
- The history is not “foreigners brought civilization”: older large-scale South Arabian colonization narratives are challenged by archaeology emphasizing local populations, limited elite contact, and continuity. [DP]
- Aksumite writing develops from consonantal notation to a vocalized system attested in the fourth century CE. Neither a named sole inventor nor an exact invention ceremony is established. [MH, §13.1]
- MH notes a vocalized sign on Wazeba's coinage as possible evidence before Ezana; vocalized pagan inscriptions also defeat an automatic equation of vocalization with Christian conversion. Coin sequences and attribution remain specialized evidence, not exact calendar dates. [MH, §§9, 11.5, 13.1]
- Typologically call the mature script an **abugida/alphasyllabary**: related consonant signs vary with vowel order. Calling it a “syllabary” in teaching/encoding does not erase that internal organization. [TL; UNI]
- A pedagogical series is `ለ ሉ ሊ ላ ሌ ል ሎ`; one common transcription is `lä, lu, li, la, le, lə/l, lo`. These are transcription conventions, not IPA guarantees across languages and periods. [TL; UNI]
- The sixth order can represent a consonant with a central vowel or without a vowel according to context; gemination is normally not explicitly marked. Do not make a supposedly perfectly phonemic script from the visible matrix. [TL; UNI]
- Mature Ethiopic runs left to right. A modern Unicode inventory incorporates extensions for multiple languages; it is not the ancient Geʿez sign inventory. [UNI]
- MH describes Geʿez rendered in different scripts: “three visual versions” must not automatically become “three different languages.” [MH, §§11.5, 13.1]
- **Transfer:** design letter-shape inheritance, vowel marking, sound mergers, scribal archaism, and later language extensions on separate timelines.
- **Material coupling:** the availability of writing and of vowel signs does not imply universal literacy; costs, schools, copying, and genre determine access. [HD; MH]

## Mythic core: distinguish pagan evidence from Christian theology

| Layer | Supported core | Calibration and design use |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-Christian Aksumite royal religion | Inscriptions invoke Astar, Mahrem, Beher, and Meder; Mahrem legitimates royal descent/protection | Attested names and royal formulae; a complete narrative mythology is not recoverable from them. [MH, §§10.1, 11.5] |
| Cross-cultural divine naming | Greek versions associate Mahrem with Ares and Astar with Zeus | Evidence of translation/equivalence in a particular political setting, not proof of identical cults or stories. [MH, §10.1] |
| Christian court transformation | Ezana's record spans non-Christian and Christian dedications; coin symbols change | Court declarations are evidence of official change, not instant conversion of every household. [MH, §§9–10] |
| Orthodox sacred cosmos | Trinity, Incarnation, Mary, angels, saints, and salvation history organize devotion | Saints and angels are not additional gods in a reconstructed “pantheon.” [EC; HT] |
| Solomonic/Ark narrative | The Kebra Nagast links Makeda, Solomon, Menelik, and the Ark's transfer | Treat as a medieval textual and legitimating tradition, not archaeological proof of tenth-century-BCE events. [MH, §2.1; TT] |

- Beher/Meder's exact domains and the interpretation of a putative pagan divine triad are uncertain; MH discusses incompatible sea/land readings. Do not fill the gaps with a standardized elemental pantheon.
- The Kebra Nagast's surviving medieval form and the much earlier period it narrates require separate dates; legendary descent is not a verified royal genealogy. [MH; TT]
- **Transfer:** distinguish an institution's claim about its remote origin from the date at which that claim is documented.
- **Limit:** this entry does not reconstruct pre-Christian household religion, a full oral myth corpus, or every regional saint tradition; evidence and expertise here are thin.

## Symbolic system: concealment, inscriptions, crosses, and presence
- **Tabot** is not safely glossed as just “a little Ark-shaped box.” The word can refer to the consecrated altar tablet, its containing chest, or their combination; **menbere tabot** names the tablet's throne/supporting chest. [HT]
- The tablet is inscribed with Christ's name and the saint's name to which it is dedicated; the Eucharistic offering takes place upon it. A parish's sacred identity therefore couples writing, dedication, object, place, and authorized service. [HT]
- HT describes episcopal authorization, restricted handling/viewing, and ornate coverings. These are active rules constituting holiness, not arbitrary secrecy around a museum collectible.
- Public procession does not mean public unveiling: a wrapped tabot can be publicly central while remaining visually inaccessible. [HT; U]
- Timkat commemorates Christ's baptism; parish tabots travel to a water site with clergy, prayers, hymns, and an overnight vigil, then return in procession. Water, calendar, object mobility, and sacred speech form one event. [U]
- UNESCO's shorthand “replicas of the Ark” describes festival significance; HT's tablet/chest distinction supplies the finer material terminology. Preserve both levels rather than treating one as a contradiction.
- Do not conflate every consecrated parish tabot with the particular Ark traditionally held at Mary of Zion in Aksum. A belief about that Ark's presence is not an externally verified identification. [HT; MH, §2.1]
- Other symbols must be staged: crescent-and-disc motifs precede Christian crosses in the coin sequence; royal titles and portraits persist through religious change. Exact motif meanings are less secure than the observable change. [MH, §9]
- Monumental stelae associated with elite tombs imitate architectural features, including doors/windows; “solar obelisk” is not a sufficient identification. [MH, §§5.5–5.6]
- **Transfer:** record `object → authorized handler → permitted viewer → covering → inscription → site → movement date`; this produces ritual constraints without copying a sacred object as loot.
- **Costume coupling:** coverings and processional equipment act on visibility, while garments mark participants; an object-wrapper and a garment may share materials without being the same object category.

## Calendar and hour-counting: separate calendrical and clock differences

| Variable | Ethiopian reference | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Year structure | Twelve months of thirty days, then Pagumen's five days or six in a leap year | Not thirteen equal months. [EC; DR] |
| Leap rule | Extra day every fourth year, without the Gregorian century exception | Modern Gregorian correspondences are not valid for all centuries. [DR] |
| Epoch/year number | Incarnation reckoning produces a seven- or eight-year difference from Gregorian numbering, depending on date | Not a constant subtraction of seven; not merely the Julian/Gregorian day offset. [EC; DR] |
| New year | For current-era correspondences, 1 Mäskäräm is normally 11 September Gregorian, or 12 September before a Gregorian leap year | Specify year; the familiar 1900–2099 alignment is not timeless. [DR] |
| Liturgical scheduling | Fixed commemorations coexist with Easter-dependent observances; Bahre Hasab is taught among advanced church studies | A solar civil calendar alone does not calculate every festival. [EC; HD] |
| Hour labels | Contemporary traditional counting uses daytime/nighttime twelve-hour cycles starting approximately at civil 06:00/18:00 | A six-hour difference in labels is not a six-hour difference in time zone. [LF] |

- EC locates the ecclesiastical calendar in an Egyptian/Coptic relationship; a shared calendar structure does not mean identical year epochs, saints' lists, or festival practice.
- Current hour-label examples: civil `07:00` → daytime `1`; `12:00` → daytime `6`; `16:00` → daytime `10`. State daytime/nighttime and the counting convention explicitly. [LF]
- This is an approximately dawn-based convention, not evidence that every clock is re-zeroed at each day's exact astronomical sunrise.
- Timkat is Ter 11; its familiar Gregorian January date must be checked against the particular year's alignment. UNESCO describes the common 18 January eve / 19 January main celebration, not a perpetual conversion algorithm. [EC; U; DR]
- **Transfer:** maintain separate fields for epoch, month/day, leap rule, festival computus, day/night division, hour origin, and time zone.
- **Scene check:** can a visiting merchant and a local cantor agree on the same instant while giving different year numbers and hour labels? If not, the design has mixed independent variables.
- Do not explain divergence as backwardness or “being outside time”: it demonstrates coexisting temporal institutions, not absence of chronology.

## Qene: learned registers, layered readings, and performance
- **Qene** is learned poetic composition, particularly in Geʿez church education; it is not a synonym for every Ethiopian poem, proverb, or contemporary Amharic lyric. [HD; DL]
- **Säm əna wärq / wax and gold** names a surface reading and a further reading, commonly made available through lexical or syntactic ambiguity and shared allusion. The casting metaphor is an interpretive model, not a two-word translation recipe. [DL]
- The gold need not be an insult or a political secret: biblical typology, saintly praise, and theological association also reward trained interpretation. Do not turn a literary practice into a national propensity to deceive. [HD; DL]
- HD describes students conjugating Geʿez verbs, examining vocabulary, hearing a saint's story, composing to a model, fitting a musical form, and submitting work for criticism. A “fossil language” here remains a generative instrument.
- The relevant registers include grammatical competence, scriptural/saints' allusion, musical form, and occasion-specific address; the surface/hidden distinction is not simply Geʿez versus Amharic.

| Form named in HD's account | Reported verse count | Use as reference, not universal prescription |
|---|---|---|
| Gubae Qana | Two rhyming verses | Introductory short model; useful for testing whether both readings survive brevity. |
| Wazema | Five verses | A named model linked to performance, not just a five-line shape on paper. |
| Sellassie | Six verses | Do not infer every theological rule of the form from its name alone. |
| Mewadis | Eight verses | Larger model; still constrained by delivery and occasion. |

- Counts and spellings above follow **HD (1970)**; he explicitly notes other forms and differences in the Gonji/Washera schools. “Verse” need not equal a modern printed line; do not standardize every school from this table.
- Oral improvisation can coexist with preparation, revision, memorized models, and long schooling. HD's student workflow is not evidence that all qene is spontaneous first-attempt speech.
- **Transfer worksheet:** identify the public occasion → intended listeners → overt proposition → second interpretation → exact linguistic hinge → shared reference → musical/metrical constraints → competent reviewer.
- A successful hinge must allow both readings in the fictional language's grammar; merely announcing that an English sentence has a secret meaning does not build a poetic register.
- Couple the audience to education: who recognizes the biblical allusion, who understands the grammar, and who can judge the performance need not be the same people.
- Precise founder dates, derivation of all schools from a single master, and continuity of every modern form from Aksum are not established here (to-verify). Do not backdate HD's twentieth-century description wholesale.

## Era staging: continuity without a timeless costume box

| Evidence stage | Language/symbol/material combination | Constraint on fictional comparison |
|---|---|---|
| First millennium BCE northern Horn | Local societies and South Arabian connections; inscriptions and monumental sites | Do not compress the region into a single foreign-founded “pre-Aksumite” state. [DP] |
| Early Aksum, first centuries CE–third century | Red Sea connections; developing royal display; coinage begins late in the third century | Separate port/merchant audiences from inland and household language use. [MH, §§3–4, 8–9] |
| Fourth–seventh centuries | Ezana's religious transition; vocalized writing; Christian institutions; continuing international connections | Writing change, court conversion, and popular adoption have different clocks. [MH] |
| Post-Aksumite to Zagwe, approximately seventh–thirteenth centuries | Political relocation and changing church/settlement landscapes; archaeological and textual continuity | Sparse chronicle evidence is not proof of empty centuries or total cultural collapse. [DP; MH, §§4.8, 15] |
| Solomonic medieval, late thirteenth–sixteenth centuries | Royal/ecclesiastical institutions, Geʿez textual production, Kebra Nagast transmission; educational development | Use medieval evidence for medieval arrangements; the complete HD school system is not directly attested in Aksum. [TT; HD] |
| Early modern–modern reference horizon | Eighteenth-century ecclesiastical vestment; nineteenth-century royal cotton/silk garments; twentieth-century schooling and contemporary hour conventions | These are separately dated witnesses, not one uniform “traditional era.” [VA; HD; LF] |

- These bins organize evidence, not a claim that one dynasty, language, or garment instantly replaced another.
- A copied manuscript may preserve an older text; the manuscript's manufacture, text's composition, narrated events, and later performance each need dates.
- Do not make Islam, environmental pressure, or a legendary destroyer the single automatic explanation for Aksum's transformation; competing explanations require archaeological and chronological controls. [MH, §15; DP]

## Costume and material signature: use dated objects, not silhouettes alone

| Witness | Material/visual facts | Coupling and limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Aksumite royal coin portraits | Draped shoulders, headgear, insignia; motifs vary across issues | Royal self-presentation, not a complete costume plate for commoners or women. [MH, §§7.2, 9] |
| Metropolitan's vestment, VA 1424-1870, c. 1748 | Hooded, sleeveless white satin robe with red satin, metallic-thread/pearl decoration; Arabic and Coptic inscriptions | Cross-language ecclesiastical material links; catalogue suggests Egyptian embroiderers, not a proven maker attribution. [VA] |
| Tiruwork Wube's kamis, VA 400-1869, 1830–1868 | Two soft cotton layers with silk embroidery in red, yellow, black, and green | Court-associated garment; not evidence that every region or class wore the same dress. [VA] |
| Her shamma, VA 401-1869, c. 1860 | White cotton with yellow/orange/red woven border; catalogue calls it a light natala shawl | Garment-category overlap is documented; do not impose a rigid one-English-word taxonomy. [VA] |
| Present ritual presentation | Tabot coverings and restrictions on visibility; head covering described in worship | Distinguish clerical dress, lay dress, and sacred-object textiles instead of labeling every cloth a costume. [HT; U] |

- Cotton, silk, metallic thread, and pearls imply different production, purchase, and repair chains; material availability must be staged rather than borrowed from a single attractive image.
- Arabic/Coptic lettering on a garment used in Ethiopia does not prove its wearer spoke both languages; inscription language, workshop, donor, and recipient can differ.
- The robe's catalogue explicitly records looting at Maqdala in 1868. The queen's garments have their own documented posthumous transfer history; do not describe every object's acquisition as the same event. [VA]
- Ancient nonelite dress is especially thinly evidenced here. MH reports royal images and a literary reference to white cloth, not a complete surviving Aksumite wardrobe; a modern netela must not fill that gap by default.
- **Transfer:** specify `garment term → cut/layers → fibre → dye/embroidery → production site → wearer/office → permitted context → date → evidence grade`.

## Naming practices: grammar, affiliation, and claims of authority
- Aksumite royal formulae can combine personal name, filiation, **bisi** epithet, territorial titles, and divine affiliation: Ezana's inscriptions give his father Ella Amida and the epithet Bisi Halen. [MH, §§6.1, 7.5, 11.5]
- The interpretation of **bisi** affiliations is debated; it is not safe to translate every instance as a modern hereditary surname or to derive a whole clan system from it. [MH, §6.1]
- Treat titulary as a political claim: a royal list of territories can exceed demonstrable everyday control. A title's presence is evidence of the assertion, not an independently surveyed border. [MH, §7.5]
- Christian names can couple ordinary human identity to sacred vocabulary: **Gäbrä Mäsqäl** is conventionally glossed “servant of the Cross,” **Wäldä Maryam** “son of Mary.” These illustrate compound/construct relationships, not a license to concatenate dictionary stems without morphology. [WL; TL; MH on Gabra Masqal]
- Transliteration systems differ: scholarly vowels and conventional English spellings must be mapped, not treated as different historical people.
- **Transfer:** maintain separate fields for given name, filiation, devotional/baptismal identity, regnal designation, title, and affiliation; not every person or era requires every field.
- Contemporary surname/patronymic rules, women's naming variation, and differences among Ethiopian/Eritrean communities need a community-specific source before application (to-verify); neither a royal inscription nor a dictionary supplies them.

## What a worldbuilder can take
1. **Productive archaism:** let an old learned language authorize new verse while vernacular explanations and different literacy competencies coexist.
2. **Script biography:** preserve inherited forms, local redesign, and later extensions; never equate shared script with shared ancestry or religion.
3. **Sacred opacity:** build authority through restricted viewing, inscribed dedication, coverings, custody, and procession rather than through an object's spectacular appearance alone.
4. **Plural time:** derive disagreements from separately specified epochs, calendars, festivals, and hour labels; test them against the same real instant.
5. **Interpretive education:** design the schools and shared references needed to make layered poetry intelligible; retain performance, not just wordplay.
6. **Material multilingualism:** a textile can connect donor, workshop, liturgy, and trade in different languages without making every participant multilingual.
7. **Handoff, not canon:** give lore-shokunin candidate mechanisms, era limits, source keys, confidence, and unresolved alternatives. Do not create fictional rulers, sacred relics, national histories, or world-bible facts here.

## Provenance cautions and verification backlog
- **Source hierarchy:** inscriptions/objects constrain attested forms; archaeology constrains chronology/context; church sources explain meanings and norms; later narratives establish remembered claims. No track automatically substitutes for another.
- **Colonial bias:** reject racial “Hamitic” explanations and the notion that African achievement requires an external civilizing population. MH's 1991 linguistic and diffusion hypotheses are not all current consensus; DP explicitly revises older colonization models.
- **Institutional bias:** HD/EC are 1970 church presentations, with dated assumptions about gender, hierarchy, pedagogy, and national culture. Their statistics and universalizing rhetoric are not used here as present-day facts.
- **Interpretive caution:** Levine's wax/gold analysis is influential, not a psychological law for all Amhara people or all Ethiopians. A formal literary technique is not evidence that a population is inherently secretive.
- **Museum bias:** catalogue names, dates, maker attributions, and collecting histories remain revisable. Elite objects survive disproportionately; British possession after an expedition is not evidence of consensual transfer.
- **Expertise gaps:** regional qene terminology/metrics, ancient pronunciation, pre-Christian narratives, nonelite ancient dress, and community-specific naming are thin here; keep their inline (to-verify) limits rather than filling them from analogy.
- **Respectful application:** borrow structural relations, not restricted sacred designs or claims of access to concealed tabots; consult living custodians before direct representation of practices whose viewing is restricted.
- Recheck precise date conversion against DR or a tested implementation, exact lexemes against WL/TL, and ritual terminology against dated community-specific accounts before delivering a detailed fictional system.
