## 2. Era staging: “Classical” is a modern frame, not a native year-name

| Working stage | Approximate dates, BCE | Coupling implications | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late Bronze Age background | c. 1600–1100 | Mycenaean Greek in Linear B; palace administration supplies a linguistic and religious comparison | Treating palace tablets as Classical civic records |
| Early Iron Age / Geometric background | c. 1100–700 | Discontinuous settlement and uneven evidence; changing uses of old tombs; alphabetic writing appears late in this span | A fully documented genealogy across the palace collapse |
| Archaic formation | c. 700–480 | Polis institutions, overseas settlements, local scripts, sanctuary networks, and changing military equipment | Assuming every institution appeared together in a single “Greek awakening” |
| Fifth-century Classical | 480–400 | Persian-war memories, rival alliances, Athenian imperial power; locally differentiated decrees, festivals, and dress representations | Making Athens representative of Sparta, Crete, or every island |
| Fourth-century Classical | 400–323 | Interstate warfare and mercenary service; Macedonian expansion; continued local institutions | Giving every earlier hoplite a Macedonian pike |
| Hellenistic comparison | after 323 | Attic-based Koine spreads alongside persistent local forms; royal institutions add another layer | Erasing poleis, local gods, or regional dress overnight |

- Boundaries are heuristic and vary by discipline; 480 and 323 are convenient political/art-historical anchors, not simultaneous changes in speech, weaving, or cult. [C, B, L]
- For a usable reference slice, specify **place + interval + institution + social position**: “a fifth-century Athenian civic procession” is actionable; “Greek clothes” is not.
- Epic preserves and recomposes several historical layers. Its heroic equipment and divine genealogies are not transparent photographs of either Mycenaean or Classical life. [C, A]

## 3. Language family and writing: keep dialect, script, and register separate

- Greek is an Indo-European branch. Mycenaean is its Bronze Age attested form, not a non-Greek predecessor; the relationship among later dialect groups requires linguistic evidence rather than a literal reading of migration myths. [C]
- Classical Greek is inflected, with grammatical gender, case, verbal aspect, and dialect-specific morphology; reconstructions of its pronunciation include vowel-length distinctions and pitch accent. Modern Greek pronunciation is not a neutral default for every Classical century. [C]
- Alphabetic Greek adapted Phoenician writing, notably representing vowels, with surviving evidence from the eighth century BCE. **Epichoric** means local: letter forms and letter-values could differ among communities. A dialect boundary need not coincide with an alphabet boundary. [C]
- Earlier Linear B is a syllabic administrative script, not the Classical alphabet. Classical Cyprus also preserves Greek in the Cypriot syllabary: alphabetization was not a single universal switch. [C]
- Athens adopted the Ionic alphabet for public use in 403/2 BCE. A script reform does not prove an overnight change in spoken dialect or uniform private literacy. Later editors' accents, word spacing, and normalized spelling are not facsimiles of an original stone. [C]

| Layer or group | Local speech / documentary anchor | Literary convention | Craft use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attic–Ionic | Attica; Euboea; Aegean islands and western Anatolian communities, with internal differences | Attic prose and dramatic dialogue; Ionic prose in Herodotus | An administrative prestige variety can differ from a narrator's scholarly register |
| Doric / Northwest Greek | Distinct local varieties in the Peloponnese, Crete, northwest Greece, and settlements abroad | Doric coloring conventionally marks much choral lyric | A choral voice need not identify the performer's birthplace |
| Aeolic | Lesbian, Boeotian, Thessalian varieties; do not equate them mechanically | Sappho and Alcaeus are major Lesbian lyric witnesses | A prestigious song tradition can preserve regional forms selectively |
| Arcadocypriot | Arcadia and Cyprus; Cypriot evidence includes syllabic documents | Not a dominant surviving panhellenic literary medium | A small literary footprint does not mean a community lacked language or institutions |
| Epic *Kunstsprache* | Not a fifth regional vernacular | Homeric epic mixes inherited forms, chiefly Ionic with Aeolic elements and archaisms | Preserve metrically useful forms after everyday speech has changed |

- **Diagnostic, not costume accent:** inherited long *ā* often corresponds to Ionic *ē*, with Attic qualifications; Doric *mātēr* versus Attic/Ionic *mētēr* “mother” illustrates a sound correspondence, not permission to replace every letter mechanically. [C]
- A Theban poet such as Pindar can use a conventional Doric-colored choral language rather than simply transcribing Boeotian speech. Tragedy similarly combines an Attic-based dramatic medium with different choral conventions. [C]
- Application: maintain separate fields for **home dialect / public document dialect / poetic register / script / date**. Derive personal names and divine epithets through the same historical sound rules; do not assign every genre a separate ethnic population.

## 4. Mythic core and the Olympian pantheon

- Shared narrative resources include divine succession—Ouranos, Kronos, Zeus in Hesiod's *Theogony*—and epic accounts of gods intervening in human conflict. Shared stories do not constitute a centralized creed or a complete guide to local sacrifice. [B]
- A useful Olympian roster includes Zeus, Hera, Poseidon, Demeter, Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Ares, Aphrodite, Hephaistos, Hermes, and Hestia/Dionysos in differing twelve-god groupings. **The Twelve is an attested grouping, not one universally fixed membership list.** [B]
- Hades is indispensable to the divine world without thereby being a regular member of an Olympian twelve. Do not make “underworld” synonymous with moral evil, or treat every god as a single occupation with exclusive jurisdiction. [B]
- **Olympian** refers to the gods associated with Olympos; **Olympic** concerns Olympia and its festival. Olympia is in the Peloponnese, not on Mount Olympos. Zeus' sanctuary there belongs to a particular landscape and institutional history. [B, D]

| Divine relationship | Common narrative / symbolic associations | Cult-level question to ask |
|---|---|---|
| Zeus and Hera | Sovereignty, divine kinship, marriage; eagle/thunderbolt and royal imagery | Which epithet, sanctuary, priesthood, and festival define this relationship locally? |
| Athena and Poseidon | Civic protection, skill, sea power; competing claims in Athenian tradition | How do rival claims coexist in one city's sacred landscape? |
| Apollo and Artemis | Prophecy, song, purification; hunting and transitions of age | Which age-group or civic body performs which rite, rather than “all citizens”? |
| Demeter and Kore/Persephone | Agriculture, mother–daughter relations, underworld transitions | Is this a civic festival, a restricted rite, or an initiatory setting? |
| Dionysos | Wine, performance, altered social states; ivy, thyrsos, drinking vessels | Who organizes performance, carries ritual objects, and pays? |
| Hermes, Hephaistos, Aphrodite, Ares | Movement/exchange, skilled making, desire, conflict | Which local combinations cut across a simple domain chart? |
| Hestia and household/civic hearths | Continuity, hospitality, sacrificial order | How does household practice connect to the civic hearth? |

- Treat this table as an orientation map, not an exhaustive theology. Particular epithets can foreground relationships that the short English gloss conceals. [B]
- A god's name plus a cult epithet—Athena **Polias**, “of the city,” versus Athena **Nike**, “Victory”—couples language to jurisdiction, place, and ritual. It does not automatically mean two wholly separate beings or two interchangeable titles. [B, I]

## 5. Polis cults: institutions make the shared gods local

| Anchored case | Institutional / material coupling | Transferable mechanism | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Athena Polias and the Panathenaia, Athens | Civic procession and a woven peplos link goddess, textile work, imagery, and public time | A cloth gift reproduces a political relationship through skilled labor | Exact production/presentation cycles and personnel need period-specific checking |
| Athena Nike, Athens | IG I³ 35 concerns a priestess, sanctuary arrangements, and public authority | Cult administration can change access to office without changing the divine name | AIO discusses competing dates; do not present one proposed date as settled [I] |
| Hera, Argos / the Argive Heraion | A major regional sanctuary makes Hera more than a portable “marriage goddess” | A sanctuary can organize a territorial identity beyond the urban center | Do not infer identical local rites from the shared name [B] |
| Apollo Karneios, Sparta | A locally salient festival places cult time alongside military decisions | Festival obligation can constrain the campaign calendar | Exact rite sequences and historical explanations are unevenly documented [B] |
| Demeter and Kore, Eleusis | Sanctuary, initiation, and Athenian political relations intersect | Initiatory membership need not equal citizenship | Secret ritual details are especially vulnerable to speculative reconstruction [B] |
| Zeus, Olympia | Sacrifice, dedications, athletic competition, and victor memory create a shared meeting point | Competitive prestige can connect politically hostile communities | Panhellenic does not mean politically unified or equally accessible [B, D] |

- Keep **polis / deme / household / kin group / association / panhellenic sanctuary** distinct. Participation, ritual office, funding, and access vary by institution, sex, status, and occasion. [B, I]
- AIO's commentary on Athena Nike distinguishes selection from a traditional *genos* from selection among the relevant citizen population; it is evidence for institutional specificity, not proof that every priesthood was hereditary or elective. [I]
- Food portions, dedications, processional position, garments, and the right to speak a ritual formula are possible carriers of membership. For any named historical privilege, verify the particular inscription or account before importing it.

## 6. Hero cult: powerful dead, local history, and contested possession

- A cult hero is not merely a protagonist or a morally exemplary person. Recurring honors at a tomb or shrine can make a deceased figure an active local power; heroic and divine status can overlap, notably with Herakles. [B, A]
- Distinguish **hero cult** from household ancestor observance and from archaeological **tomb cult**, a description of activity at a tomb whose recipient may not be identifiable. Offerings alone do not name the occupant or prove continuous memory from the Bronze Age. [A]

| Type of reference case | What the evidence can support | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Epic figure honored locally | A community attaches a named story to a site and ritual | That the epic person historically occupied the tomb |
| Founder / *oikist* cult | Public memory organizes settlement identity and political allegiance | An uncontested account of foundation, migration, or land entitlement |
| Offerings at an old tomb | Later visitors treated an earlier monument as significant | The exact name, narrative, or beliefs of every visitor |
| Heroine or heroic family group | Female and relational recipients belong within the cult landscape | An exclusively male, warrior-only definition of heroic power [R] |

- **Worked historical anchor:** after Brasidas' death at Amphipolis in 422 BCE, Thucydides 5.11 reports public burial, sacrifice, games, and annual honors, with Brasidas made founder and Hagnon's memorials removed. Language of foundation, ritual expenditure, and civic allegiance change together. [T]
- This is an author's account of a particular political transformation, not proof that every founder cult began through deliberate replacement. Treat settlers' foundation stories as claims over landscapes, not neutral deeds of ownership. [A, T]
- **Contested simplification:** “Olympians get white victims and shared meals; heroes get black victims and total burning” is not a reliable universal rule. Ekroth's comparative treatment exposes varied practices; meals and ritual forms cross the textbook binary. [E]
- Application: record **recipient / claimed history / cult location / ritual sponsor / annual obligation / competing claimant**. Historical truth, public story, and ritual entitlement need not coincide; lore-shokunin decides whether any fictional claim is true.

## 7. Symbol systems: attributes are contextual, not a secret universal alphabet

| Sign or object | Attested association / function | Safe design inference | Unsafe shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Owl and olive | Athena and Athenian civic imagery | A divine association can circulate on civic objects and money | Every owl everywhere means Athens or “wisdom” |
| Eagle and thunderbolt | Zeus and divine authority | Different media can reiterate a claim to protection | A single permanent heraldic meaning across all regions |
| Gorgoneion | Frontal Gorgon image in protective and startling uses, including martial contexts | Display, gaze, and placement matter alongside narrative identity | A modern good/evil alignment chart |
| Lion skin and club | Herakles' recognizable iconographic equipment | An exceptional figure's attributes can identify him without a written label | Ordinary Greeks dressed like Herakles |
| Ivy, thyrsos, wine vessels | Dionysiac imagery and ritual/performance contexts | Clothing, carried objects, and gesture work as a bundle | One isolated ivy motif proves a particular rite occurred |
| Meander and palmette | Repeated decorative vocabularies in multiple media | Borders organize surfaces and can acquire local associations | A universal, fixed occult translation for every geometric pattern |

- Associations above are orientation-level syntheses. Read **object + placement + accompanying figures/text + find context + date** before assigning meaning. [B, L]
- A serpent, for example, can occur with multiple gods and heroes; neither “serpent = evil” nor “serpent = one chthonic god” is a defensible universal dictionary. [B]

## 8. Dress grammar: chiton and himation are constructions and social acts

| Term | Construction / arrangement baseline | Social and material range | Coupling handle |
|---|---|---|---|
| *Chitōn* / chiton | Tunic-like garment, commonly reconstructed as a joined textile body with shoulder/arm openings and fastenings; often girded | Men and women; lengths, fullness, fabric, and fastening vary | Hem length and available cloth can mark task or occasion without changing the base term |
| *Himation* | Large rectangular mantle arranged by wrapping and draping, commonly wool | Both sexes; over another garment, or in some male contexts alone | Wrapping can conceal/expose an arm, manage movement, or cover the head |
| *Peplos* | Common reconstruction: body wrap with shoulder fastenings and an upper overfold, *apoptygma* | Strong female associations; wool common; arrangements and identification vary | Cloth geometry joins divine clothing, human dress, and votive production |
| *Chlamys* | Short pinned cloak | Especially associated with travel, youths, riding, and military activity | Closure placement and compactness signal mobility rather than a universal male uniform |
| *Exōmis* / exomis | One-shoulder arrangement leaving the right shoulder/arm free | Labor, craft, and some military representations | Freedom of movement can acquire classed associations |
| *Zōnē*; *kolpos* | Girdle/belt; blousing of cloth above it | Adjust the apparent length, fullness, and silhouette | “Gird / ungird / draw up” can be a productive verb system as important as garment nouns |

- Baselines are not archaeological sewing patterns for every locality. Lee (2015), chapters 4–5, should govern detailed reconstruction; a name in a text and a modern museum label may not identify exactly the same cut. [L]
- **Grammar:** textile dimensions/weight → joins and fastenings → girding → outer wrap → hair, footwear, and ornaments → movement and occasion. Build a dressed person, not a list of “Greek costume items.”
- “Doric” and “Ionic” garment classifications are not passports: neither clothing terminology nor modern dress typology maps cleanly onto a wearer's spoken dialect or ethnic identity. [L]
- Wool and linen, colored cloth, patterned borders, and ornament complicate the modern white-marble image. Do not derive a whole population's palette from surviving unpainted stone, nor impose later Roman imperial purple regulations on Classical Greece. [L]
- Nudity in athletic, heroic, or divine imagery is a culturally coded presentation of the body, not evidence that armies normally fought naked. Artistic transparency and elaborate folds also require interpretation rather than literal dressmaking. [L]
- **Myth–costume coupling:** the Panathenaic peplos makes weaving and the goddess' imagery part of civic practice; it is not merely an accessory. Scholarship reconstructs the peplos as woven by the *ergastinai* and presented to Athena Polias, while the relationship between an annually woven peplos and a larger quadrennial one remains debated (Mansfield 1985; Shear 2021); reproduce no single reconstruction as settled.

## 9. Hoplite material culture: a variable equipment package, not national uniform

| Component | Reliable baseline | Era / reconstruction control |
|---|---|---|
| *Aspis* | Large round, concave shield; wooden structure, often bronze facing; central forearm support and a separate handgrip | Not a solid bronze disc; decoration does not establish universal family heraldry |
| *Doru* / *dory* | Thrusting spear, with shaft, head, and often a butt-spike (*sauroter*) | Do not substitute the later Macedonian two-handed *sarissa* or assert one universal length |
| Secondary blade | Sword forms include straight *xiphos* and curved *kopis* | Equipment, depiction, and preservation vary; not every warrior's kit is recoverable |
| Cuirass | Bronze cuirasses and other body defenses appear; absence or lighter protection also matters | “Every hoplite wore a glued-linen *linothorax*” is not established; the glued, multi-ply construction is a modern reconstruction hypothesis, tested by experiment rather than established for the ancient garment (Aldrete, Bartell & Aldrete 2013) |
| Helmet and greaves | Several bronze helmet types and lower-leg defenses | Corinthian, Chalcidian, and *pilos*-type helmets are not one timeless ensemble or a mandatory sequence everywhere |
| Clothing, straps, supplies | Tunic, cloak, footwear, repair materials, and provisioning make the weapon package usable | Organic material is less well preserved than bronze; a sanctuary display is not an average field inventory |

- **Calibration:** shield-and-spear heavy infantry is well attested; the timing of a complete “hoplite revolution,” formation density, and the interpretation of *ōthismos* (“pushing”) remain debated. Equipment development, tactics, and political change need separate chronologies. [K]
- The deterministic chain “new shield → equal farmers → democracy” is not a settled law. Krentz questions timeless ritualized battle conventions; disagreement is evidence against using one reconstruction as universal Greek behavior. [K]
- Citizen service is important but not exhaustive: armies can include allies, mercenaries, cavalry, missile troops, and dependents; avoid making the male citizen hoplite stand for the entire society. Thucydides' Amphipolis narrative itself describes mixed forces. [T]
- Application: define **procurement / household cost / maintenance / training / unit composition / sanctioned insignia / sacred obligations** before choosing a silhouette. Any exact insignia rule needs a dated source: the earliest testimony to the Spartan lambda blazon is late-fifth-century BCE (the comic poet Eupolis, quoted in Photius' *Lexicon* s.v. λάμβδα), so an early or universal lambda should not be assumed.

## 10. Civic calendars and Olympiad dating: several clocks, not one era

| Clock | How a date is identified | Scope / limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Civic year | Named magistrate, such as Athens' eponymous archon or a Spartan ephor; local month and day | Cities have different month names, year starts, and intercalations |
| Festival cycle | Annual rites and multi-year festivals organize obligations | Ritual recurrence is not automatically a universal numbering system |
| Olympiad synchronism | Numbered four-year interval plus its first, second, third, or fourth year | Useful across local chronologies, especially in later historical writing; not every everyday document uses it |

- Conventionally **Olympiad 1.1 begins in summer 776 BCE**; **2.1 begins in summer 772 BCE**. The epoch is a retrospective chronological convention tied to victor lists, not proof of a contemporary surviving archive dated “year 1.” [D]
- For dates wholly before the common era, the starting summer of Olympiad `n`, year `y` is `776 − 4(n − 1) − (y − 1)` BCE, with `n ≥ 1`, `1 ≤ y ≤ 4`, and a positive result. This computes the year label, not the exact festival day.
- Example: `75.1 → 480/479 BCE`; `75.4 → 477/476 BCE`. The slash matters: an Olympiad year spans portions of two modern numbered years. Do not apply this BCE-only formula across the BCE/CE boundary; historical numbering has no year zero.
- In Thucydides 5.19, the peace is dated separately by Spartan ephor/month/day and Athenian archon/month/day; 5.20 warns against treating magistrate lists as exact measures of elapsed time. This is direct evidence for plural local dating practices. [T]
- Numbered Olympiad reckoning becomes especially useful in later historical synchronization; giving every fifth-century speaker a modern-style Olympiad date overstates everyday standardization. Early victor lists and retrospective foundation dates need stronger caution than contemporary dated decrees. [D]
- Application: provide **local legal date + festival position + historian's synchronization**, keeping reconstruction uncertainty visible. A disputed leap month can alter an apparent synchronism without changing the underlying event.

## 11. Naming practices: identity slots, not modern surnames

- A common Greek identification pattern is **personal name + father's name**, often genitive in Greek; civic contexts can add a demotic or an ethnic. In post-Cleisthenic Athens a male citizen's demotic identifies deme affiliation, not a modern hereditary family surname. [N, C]
- **Dithematic names** combine name-elements; **theophoric names** invoke a god, as in *Apollodoros* (“gift of Apollo”) or *Dionysios*. A name may preserve older morphology or a religious association without proving personal devotion to that deity. [N]
- Regional and chronological attestation matters: check an LGPN bearer and underlying document before asserting “typical Spartan,” “only Athenian,” or “already common in 480.” The database spans many centuries, not just the Classical period. [N]
- Women, non-citizens, and enslaved people are not guaranteed the same displayed identification formula as adult male citizens. Legal status, document genre, and the recorder's choices affect what survives; a Greek-form name alone does not establish citizenship or ethnic self-identification. [N]
- Keep **personal name / kinship link / deme or ethnic / office / divine epithet** as separate fields. An epithet in a prayer and a demotic in a decree perform different social work even when both derive from places.

## 12. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Select a reference slice:** place, century-range, institution, and social position; explicitly label every additional borrowing from another slice.
2. **Build three linguistic layers:** local speech, public writing, and a conservative poetic register; decide whether script reform and sound change occur at different times.
3. **Localize a shared pantheon:** for each selected god specify epithet, shrine, ritual sponsor, access, festival, and material obligation; leave theology more complex than an occupation table.
4. **Make memory institutional:** distinguish the official founder story, the physical monument, competing narratives, and yearly honors; hero cult supplies a mechanism, not a mandate for historical truth.
5. **Generate dress from work and ceremony:** cloth supply, cut/fastening, girding, mantle arrangement, body presentation, and ritual gift; attach symbols to specific media and occasions.
6. **Run plural clocks:** local legal dates, festival cycles, and a later synchronizing chronology. Check arithmetic and year boundaries before plotting journeys or war seasons.
7. **Hand off options to lore-shokunin:** proposed coupling rule, reference slice, source pointer, uncertainty, and consequences for naming/dress/calendar; no fictional nation, god, event, or costume becomes canon in this entry.

- Stress-test one procession, one military muster, one dedication, and one inter-city treaty: who speaks which variety, whose epithet is invoked, which garment/attribute is legible, who may participate, and how is the date recorded?

## 13. Provenance cautions and deliberately thin areas

- **Access calibration:** public Thucydides passages and AIO commentary were checked directly; Lee's publisher front matter verifies edition/contents, not every argument; other works are standard research references with bibliographic checks, not claims of full-text rereading. Page-level support for a new detailed reconstruction remains **(to-verify)**: resolving it requires inspecting the named editions' passages rather than publisher pages.
- **Survival bias:** Attic literature, public inscriptions, elite images, sanctuaries, and durable metal overrepresent particular people and activities. Local oral practice, organic clothing, and poorer households are much less evenly recoverable; absence from this selection is not absence from Greek life.
- **Chronicle scarcity:** many local cult histories and early festival chronologies are late reports or modern reconstructions, not continuous contemporary chronicles. Hero-cult origins, exact sanctuary routines, and the earliest Olympic sequence require case-level evidence; do not invent missing annals. [A, D]
- **Thin coverage here:** detailed Laconian, Cretan, Cypriot, and western colonial ritual calendars; enslaved people's self-naming; actual textile dimensions and dye recipes by site. Each needs a specialist/local corpus — epigraphic corpora, textile studies, and, for enslaved people, evidence not mediated only by owners' records — before precision is justified **(to-verify)**.
- **Settler-source bias:** foundation narratives can legitimate seizure, exclusion, and later territorial claims; Greek authors' accounts of neighboring peoples do not supply those peoples' own voices. “Panhellenic” and “civilized/barbarian” are historical categories to analyze, not neutral rankings.
- **Modern distortion:** racialized “Dorian versus Ionian” essences and a timeless white, isolated Greece are not sound explanatory models. Language, material exchange, political status, and local cult do not line up as one biological people-map.
- **Contested models, not discredited data:** local worship, the named gods, and heavy infantry are strongly evidenced; fixed cult binaries, one hoplite revolution, and automatic weapon-to-democracy causation are disputed interpretations. Keep the disagreement attached to the transferred method. [E, K]
- **Boundary:** these are reference patterns and generative constraints. lore-shokunin owns fictional truth, continuity, and canon acceptance; uncertain claims stay flagged through that handoff.
