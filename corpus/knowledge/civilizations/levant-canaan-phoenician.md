## 1. Scope and calibration: related traditions are not interchangeable

- **Mainstream baseline:** Phoenician is a Canaanite language within Northwest Semitic, within the Semitic branch of Afroasiatic; Punic is its western historical continuation, not an unrelated language family (Krahmalkov 2001).
- **Canaanite** is a linguistic classification as well as a variable historical/geographical label; it does not denote one timeless state, race, theology, or costume.
- **Phoenician** is a useful scholarly umbrella for first-millennium coastal and connected communities, not proof that Tyre, Sidon, Byblos, and Arwad shared a national government or a uniform self-description.
- Quinn (2018) foregrounds civic, familial, colonial, and religious affiliations; her strongest conclusions about collective identity are debated. Borrow the demand for local evidence, not “these people did not exist” as a literal demographic claim.
- Ugarit supplies unusually rich Late Bronze Age Northwest Semitic mythological texts. Ugaritic is normally distinguished from the Canaanite subgroup; its poems are comparative evidence, not a lost Phoenician Bible (Wyatt 2002; Pardee 2002).
- Evidence vocabulary here: **attested** = a specific text/object supports the observation; **reconstructed** = an inference across sources; **contested** = live scholarly disagreement; **design option** = an explicitly fictional choice.
- These statuses are local: secure dye chemistry does not make the identity of its wearer secure, and a well-attested divine name does not supply a complete myth.

## 2. Language, grammar, and writing: keep three histories separate

| Layer | Reference pattern | Coupling opportunity | Constraint |
|---|---|---|---|
| Linguistic descent | Phoenician and Hebrew belong to Canaanite; Aramaic is a related Northwest Semitic language, not a Phoenician dialect | related neighbors can share roots without sharing cult or allegiance | shared vocabulary is not evidence of one people |
| Morphology | root-and-pattern morphology, verbal stems, pronominal suffixes, and noun construct relationships | names, offices, and sanctuary titles can use the same grammatical machinery | do not make a Semitic-looking language by deleting English vowels |
| Historical sound | the Canaanite shift of earlier long *ā* toward *ō* is a comparative diagnostic | stage inherited divine and personal names through regular sound change | consonantal spellings alone underdetermine vowels and chronology |
| Script | standard Phoenician uses a right-to-left consonantal alphabet of 22 letters | short dedications, seals, ownership marks, and civic legends have distinctive visual economies | “abjad” is a modern analytic term, not an attested native ideology |
| Vocalization | much Phoenician spelling omits vowels; later Punic evidence includes Greek/Latin transcriptions and Latin-script texts | preserve a conservative sacred spelling alongside changing pronunciation | printed vocalizations are editorial reconstructions, not recovered recordings |
| Register | the surviving corpus strongly favors royal, funerary, dedicatory, and other formulaic texts | public formula and everyday speech can diverge | formula frequency does not establish ordinary conversational word order |

- Grammar and vocalization require cross-checking: Krahmalkov (2001) is a substantial reference, not a guarantee that every disputed inscription or verbal analysis has one accepted solution.
- **Alphabet spread, not creation from nothing:** earlier second-millennium West Semitic alphabetic experiments precede the standard Phoenician script (Naveh 1987).
- Late Bronze Ugaritic normally uses an alphabetic cuneiform repertoire of 30 signs. Its medium and sign forms differ from the linear Phoenician alphabet; cuneiform does not necessarily mean syllabic writing.
- Greek alphabets adapt a West Semitic/Phoenician model and repurpose consonant signs for vowel notation. Eighth-century BCE Greek inscriptions provide firm evidence; the first transfer's date, place, and number of encounters remain debated.
- Aramaic and Hebrew scripts belong to the wider West Semitic alphabetic history; their languages did not descend from Phoenician merely because their scripts are related.
- The route to Latin involves Greek and Etruscan adaptations, not a single direct “Tyre taught Rome” event (Naveh 1987).
- **Craft rule:** maintain separate ledgers for language descent, script adoption, and literacy institutions. A port may borrow a script while retaining its language and restricting monumental writing to specialists.
- Alphabetic economy does not prove universal literacy. Perishable writing media also make the surviving stone corpus a biased sample of actual writing.

## 3. Pantheon core: a regional comparison, not one family tree

| Figure or title | Secure reference anchor | Usable relation | Do not flatten into |
|---|---|---|---|
| El | senior divine authority in Ugaritic literature | council, kinship, blessing, and legitimacy | a universally exclusive “sky god” |
| Baal / Haddu | storm deity and contested kingship in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle | rain, fertility, palace-building, divine rivalry | every deity called *bʿl* in every city |
| Athirat | El's consort and a maternal divine figure at Ugarit | divine households and intercession | Astarte simply under a different spelling |
| Anat | forceful, martial actor in Ugaritic narratives | divine violence, loyalty, and the limits of royal order | a generic passive fertility goddess |
| Yam and Mot | Sea and Death, respectively, in Baal's conflicts | sea as political adversary; death as a competing power | a moral Satan or one undifferentiated “chaos” |
| Astarte / Ashtart | important Iron Age Phoenician cults; royal/priestly Sidonian evidence in KAI 14 | city-specific worship, rulership, and gendered ritual offices | a universal goddess of love with identical rites everywhere |
| Eshmun | Sidonian sanctuary and royal patronage, including KAI 14 | healing, sacred locality, and civic benefaction | merely “the Phoenician Asclepius” |
| Melqart | Tyrian cult and its Mediterranean connections | city kingship, homeland ties, and translated cult identities | simply Heracles with a different name |
| Baal Hammon and Tanit | prominent western Punic divine recipients | regional cult combinations and changes in prominence | an unchanged Late Bronze Ugaritic pair |

- Sources: Wyatt (2002) for Ugaritic narrative; Pardee (2002) for ritual evidence; Gibson (1982), Bonnet (1988), and Markoe (2000) for inscriptional and later regional contrasts.
- *Bʿl* means “lord/master” and can serve as a title or a divine designation; an epithet, locality, and date are needed to identify its referent. Likewise, a word for “god” and the proper name El require contextual distinction.
- The Baal Cycle offers a powerful **council–contest–palace** design framework: authority is negotiated through assemblies, messengers, threats, and building permission, not only combat.
- Seasonal readings of Baal and Mot are influential but do not prove that every episode transcribes an annual ritual. Match literary texts to dated ritual evidence before asserting performance (Wyatt 2002; Pardee 2002).
- Frazerian universal “dying-and-rising vegetation god” templates are discredited as a default explanatory system; particular interpretations of Baal or Melqart remain contested rather than disproved by that rejection.
- **Fictional application:** distribute jurisdiction among a senior council authority, an active storm patron, and local civic cults; allow their genealogies to disagree between cities and eras.

## 4. Melqart: the city travels, but not unchanged

- The name *mlqrt*, conventionally Melqart, is understood as “king of the city”; its civic semantics are more productive than assigning the god the modern job “patron of merchants” alone (Bonnet 1988).
- Tyrian homeland, sanctuary, royal authority, and overseas cult connections form a **civic cult network**. A linked sanctuary can support claims of origin and affiliation without proving direct political control over every settlement.
- Herodotus 2.44 describes a Tyrian sanctuary under the name Heracles and reports priests' claims of great antiquity. This is Greek inquiry and translation, not a Phoenician foundation chronicle or a verified construction date.
- His account of two remarkable pillars is an attested description; the material and luminous properties should not be treated as modern mineralogical observations.
- The Arados base AO 7676, cataloged to 25–24 BCE, pairs Greek Heracles with Phoenician Melqart in a bilingual dedication involving the gymnasium's gods. Its very late date matters: do not project that institutional setting into Bronze Age Tyre.
- **Interpretatio graeca** is the framework for Greek divine equivalence: it creates a bridge between cults rather than proving identical biographies, rites, or beliefs.
- Briquel-Chatonnet (2012) emphasizes that bilingual versions can differ in audience, identity claims, and formulation; design a diplomatic equivalence table, not a perfect translation dictionary.
- Melqart's festival is reported at second hand: Josephus, citing Menander of Ephesus' Tyrian archives, has Hiram I set up the temple of “Heracles” in the month Peritius (*Against Apion* 1.18; *Antiquities* 8.5.3), and modern scholarship discusses an *egersis* (“awakening”). Exact reconstructions of the rite and its theology still need inscription-by-inscription checking; no death-and-resurrection script is supplied here `(to-verify: rite reconstruction)`.
- **Design option:** an overseas shrine preserves the homeland god's old title while adopting local ritual clothing and an external divine name; rival parties disagree over whether equivalence entails obedience.

## 5. Symbolic system: give every emblem a carrier and a date

| Carrier | Reference affordance | Fictional coupling | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Storm-god image | weapons, commanding posture, and storm attributes in regional iconography | storm verbs become royal epithets; regalia repeat the patron's attributes | identify the particular object before naming its deity |
| Egyptianizing royal image | Eshmunazor's Egyptian-made anthropoid sarcophagus carries Egyptian royal attributes and a Phoenician text | foreign visual prestige can coexist with local genealogy, language, and gods | AO 4806 is funerary royal representation, not a wardrobe survey |
| Metalwork, ivory, and ornament | Egyptian, Syrian, Assyrian, Cypriot, and Aegean elements circulate and recombine | motifs can signal workshop, customer, route, or status | visual borrowing does not prove adoption of a whole theology |
| Pillar or sacred stone | particular sanctuaries can privilege non-figural cult markers | shape titles, processional routes, and sanctuary vocabulary | not evidence that all Phoenician worship was aniconic |
| “Sign of Tanit” | conventional modern label for a prominent Punic emblem | reserve its distribution for a dated western cult network | detailed original meaning is disputed; not a universal Bronze Age goddess logo |
| Purple textile | costly dyed material links bodies, exchange, and display | a restricted border can carry more social information than a whole robe | prestige is supported; universal legal exclusivity is not |

- Sources: Markoe (2000); Bonnet (1988); Herodotus 2.44; Louvre AO 4806; Sukenik et al. (2021).
- Use the **carrier–context–audience** test: who commissions the emblem, what bears it, who sees it, and which locality can read it? This is design tooling, not a recovered native theory.
- Keep material color, color word, and symbolic value separate. “Purple” is neither one chemically invariant hue nor a universal word meaning “royalty.”

## 6. Era staging: overlapping clocks, not a civilizational ladder

| Reference window, BCE unless stated | Historical anchor | Language/myth/material consequences for design |
|---|---|---|
| Late Bronze Age, roughly 1600–1200 | Levantine city kingdoms and imperial connections; Ugaritic texts before Ugarit's destruction | multilingual scribal environments, court myth and ritual corpora; do not label every coastal institution Phoenician |
| Early Iron Age, roughly 1200–900 | uneven disruption, continuity, and political reorganization | distinguish a destroyed archive center from a surviving port; inherited divine names need not retain the same hierarchy |
| Maritime expansion, especially ninth–sixth centuries | settlements and exchange across Cyprus and the Mediterranean | new place-name strata, diaspora affiliations, contact registers, and locally adapted material assemblages |
| Imperial pressures, eighth–fourth centuries | Assyrian, Babylonian, then Achaemenid interventions and differing civic accommodations | tribute, royal display, and political titles change without instantaneous language replacement |
| Persian-period Sidon | KAI 14 combines Sidonian genealogy, temples, territorial claims, and Egyptianizing burial display | layer institutions and media instead of inventing a “pure Phoenician costume” |
| Hellenistic and Roman transitions | Alexander's capture of Tyre in 332; Rome destroys Carthage in 146; eastern and western histories diverge | Greek civic institutions, bilingual dedications, and regional political ruptures have different clocks |
| Roman-era western continuities | Punic and Neo-Punic usage continues after Carthage's destruction | political conquest does not end speech or cult on one day; script, pronunciation, and identity can diverge |

- These are overlapping reference windows, not synchronous stages for every city (Aubet 2001; Markoe 2000; Krahmalkov 2001).
- Carthage's traditional foundation year, 814 BCE, belongs to later chronological traditions. Separate it from excavated occupation horizons; this entry does not resolve the earliest-settlement debate.
- “Colony” covers unlike situations: new settlement, trading enclave, sanctuary-linked community, and later dependent territory should not be treated as one constitutional type.
- Scarce continuous native chronicles mean that political narratives lean heavily on inscriptions, archaeology, and external accounts. Do not fill gaps with a seamless invented Phoenician national history.

## 7. Purple dye, costume, and the trade signature

- **Attested technology:** Mediterranean muricids including *Hexaplex trunculus*, *Bolinus brandaris*, and *Stramonita haemastoma* can supply shellfish dyes; species, processing, light, and oxidation affect the resulting shade (Sukenik et al. 2021).
- **Chaîne opératoire:** collection → gland processing → controlled dye bath → dyeing/air oxidation → spinning or weaving as appropriate → redistribution. The order of textile preparation must follow the actual sample; fibers can be dyed before spinning.
- Do not present one reconstructed recipe as the sole Iron Age method. The Timna paper distinguishes secure chemical detection from more tentative species mixtures and double-dyeing interpretations.
- Timna's late-eleventh/early-tenth-century BCE wool fibers provide direct chemical evidence of shellfish purple far inland. They demonstrate consumption and connectivity, not that the wearers were Phoenicians; proposed coastal production origins remain inference.
- Purple technology predates Iron Age Phoenician expansion and occurred beyond Phoenician communities. A Phoenician monopoly on its invention or use is unsupported (Sukenik et al. 2021).
- Broken shell heaps alone are ambiguous: food, lime, reuse, and dye production can overlap. Demand context, characteristic processing, stained installations, or chemical evidence.

| Costume/material layer | Terms and materials | What the evidence licenses about wearers | Explicitly fictional rule to generate |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prestige cloth | dyed wool, woven band, fringe/tassel | Timna fragments support costly colored components, not necessarily a completely purple garment | access is graded by amount, shade, quality, and occasion |
| Everyday dress | tunic, wrap, belt are comparative English labels; wool/linen belong in the regional material brief | a locality-specific everyday Phoenician cut and its native term still need verification (to-verify) | workers and travelers use cheaper, repairable cloth while elite display concentrates at visible edges |
| Royal funerary display | Egyptian royal headgear, false beard, and broad collar represented on AO 4806 | secure for this image of a Sidonian king; not proof of routine wear by all citizens | rulers adopt external prestige forms while inscriptions assert local descent |
| Artisan production | dye vessel, shell refuse, prepared yarn | identifies an activity more reliably than the artisan's rank, gender, or legal status | a craft corporation controls expertise; whether it controls wearing rights is a separate choice |
| Portable commercial identity | seal, inscribed dedication, luxury textile, mixed ornamental repertoire | trade objects circulate; their owners' ethnicity cannot be read directly from style | civic membership is signaled by seal formula and textile border rather than a national uniform |

- The last column is a design menu, not an assertion of Phoenician sumptuary law. A universal king-only purple statute in Iron Age Phoenicia is not established here (to-verify); do not back-project Roman or Byzantine restrictions.
- Textile survival is poor, and monumental images select rulers, gods, and ritual settings. Exact female everyday dress, occupational distinctions, and city-by-city headdress rules are thinly covered here (to-verify).
- A useful **trade costume signature** is therefore a system of costly finish, portable affiliation, imported ornament, and occasion-specific display—not every sailor in a purple robe.
- Derive a fictional garment lexicon from fiber, weave, finish, dye grade, fastening, repair, and permission to wear; do not pass modern labels off as attested Phoenician vocabulary.

## 8. Naming: divine sentences, civic affiliations, and new cities

| Pattern | Historical illustration | Portable mechanism | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Divine-service personal name | *ʿbd* “servant” plus divine name; Abdmelqart / *ʿbdmlqrt* | the household declares affiliation through ordinary morphology | not proof that every bearer held a priestly office |
| Divine-help sentence name | Eshmunazor / *ʾšmnʿzr*, conventionally “Eshmun has helped” | birth, gratitude, and divine agency enter the name lexicon | vowels and tense glosses are editorial, not fully written in the consonants |
| Divine-favor name | Hannibal / *ḥnbʿl*, conventionally “Baal has been gracious” | a recurrent deity element links otherwise unrelated individuals | a shared theophoric element is not a hereditary surname |
| Genealogical/public identification | KAI 14 names Eshmunazor, his father Tabnit, grandfather, and royal mother | given name + kin links + civic/royal title establishes a claim | inscriptional genealogy is selective political self-presentation |
| New settlement name | *qrt ḥdšt*, “new city,” behind Carthage; also used for Carthaginian Cartagena | names can recur; “new” is relative to a naming community | a shared name does not prove identical founding institutions |
| Descriptive settlement name | Gadir / *gdr*, conventionally connected with “enclosure/walled place” | a fortification term becomes a settlement name and then an exonym | etymology does not date the walls or founding event |
| Transmitted city name | local Gubla/Gebal versus Greek Byblos | one place accumulates names through different language routes | never derive the local city's identity backward from a later Greek gloss |

- Reference anchors: Krahmalkov (2001); Gibson (1982); Aubet (2001); AO 4806 / KAI 14. Romanizations here are conventional display forms, not a single reconstructed period pronunciation.
- **Colony-naming method:** choose whether a settlement's public name is descriptive, transferred, commemorative, or a retained local name; then derive the form in each contact language using that language's sound system.
- For each name retain `{consonantal spelling, reconstructed reading, literal analysis, date/locality, naming authority, external forms, confidence}`; do not silently turn a Latinized form into the ancestral pronunciation.
- Utica = “old city” and Ibiza/Ebusus = “island of Bes” are familiar proposed etymologies, not safe templates without a dedicated philological check (to-verify).
- Separate a city's name from civic affiliation and cult title: settlement, citizen, ruler, and sanctuary need not all use the same naming formula.

## 9. What a worldbuilder can take

1. Choose **one city, one era, and one network position** before generating a culture; the comparison is a reference base, not a prescription to clone a real society.
2. Couple language to institutions: root-and-pattern names, construct titles, conservative dedicatory formulas, and external pronunciations should have distinct social users.
3. Couple myth to civic life: build a local patron, council relationships, sanctuary titles, and rival city variants; do not import the whole Ugaritic pantheon unchanged into a Punic setting.
4. Couple symbol to material: name an emblem's carrier, workshop, audience, and era; imported imagery can speak prestige while a local inscription speaks genealogy.
5. Couple costume to production: choose cloth and dye costs, then decide who displays which amount on which occasion. Treat a legal restriction as a separate institution requiring its own explanation.
6. Couple overseas naming to historical change: ordinary words can fossilize as place names, recur at new settlements, and acquire divergent local and external forms.
7. Use Melqart-like cult translation as a **relationship generator**: homeland loyalty, foreign equivalence, and local ritual innovation can coexist without being resolved into one theology.
8. Hand lore-shokunin an option packet with derivations, era tags, material constraints, evidence status, and unresolved choices. Only lore decides what becomes true in the fictional world.

## 10. Provenance cautions and thin spots

- Public checks for this entry directly inspected the Timna paper, Louvre object records, Herodotus' Tyrian passage, the opening of Briquel-Chatonnet's article, and publisher/bibliographic records. The listed monographs are standard reference leads, not a claim that every page or disputed reading was re-collated.
- Native Phoenician narrative and continuous chronicle evidence are sparse compared with the royal, funerary, and dedicatory record. Ugarit partly fills a comparative gap; it cannot erase the chronological and geographical gap.
- Greek and Roman accounts impose external categories, divine identifications, and literary interests; biblical polemic and imperial inscriptions have different but equally real agendas. Cite what each source witnesses, not what it seems to confirm about an entire civilization.
- Settlement archaeology and modern national or colonial narratives can both overstate coherent ethnic expansion. Neither a pottery type nor a foreign god-name establishes the ethnicity, consent, or political dependence of a whole population.
- Greek *phoinix* means “purple/red” (also “palm tree”), and its derivation for the exonym remains a scholarly hypothesis, not a recovered native self-name; Akkadian *kinahhu* is “probably sunset land/west,” with purple-dye associations alongside. Do not derive “Canaan” from a dye word or treat “Phoenician” as a secure native ethnonym without argument [López-Ruiz 2021].
- Tophet deposits and the interpretation, distribution, and extent of child sacrifice require dedicated archaeological and osteological sources; this entry does not adjudicate them `(to-verify: dedicated archaeological and osteological sources)`. Neither classical atrocity stories nor blanket apologetic dismissal substitutes for that work.
- Thin coverage remains in everyday costume, native garment vocabulary, women's non-royal voices, local ritual calendars, and exact colony-name etymologies; proposed specifics in those areas require source-level checking (to-verify).
- Preserve contested and reconstructed tags in every handoff. These notes authorize comparisons and design procedures, not canon, national-origin claims, or a seamless reconstructed Phoenician worldview.
