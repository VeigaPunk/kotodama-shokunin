## Scope and evidence ladder

- “Turkic” names a language family; “Türk” here identifies the early medieval imperial formations; neither means every steppe population nor only modern Turkish speakers.
- Center this base on the sixth–eighth-century Türk empires, especially the eastern inscriptional record. Western regions, households, and subordinate groups are less evenly documented.
- Use four evidence labels: **attested** in a dated text/object; **reported** through an outside account; **interpretive** scholarly explanation; **analogy** a design comparison, not demonstrated descent.
- Separate an object's date, a narrative's recording date, and the date an author assigns its events. A late manuscript can preserve earlier material without making every detail early.
- The evidence is elite-heavy: memorial rhetoric, diplomatic histories, high-status archaeology, and much later ethnography. These do not supply a census of everyday belief or dress.

## Era staging: preserve transitions, not a timeless nomad kit

| Stage | Language, writing, and authority | Material/myth coupling to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Before 552: Xiongnu and later Rouran comparisons | Earlier steppe imperial institutions; the Xiongnu language problem remains unresolved | Compare sacral rule and transmitted offices without labeling every predecessor Turkic (Di Cosmo 2002; Golden 1992) |
| 552 to early seventh century: first Türk imperial formation and eastern/western divisions | Multilingual diplomacy; Sogdian participates in elite written representation | Mobile courts are embedded in exchange with Iranian-speaking regions, China, and Byzantium; do not assume language-pure institutions |
| Seventh-century eastern subjection and restoration | Tang rule, service, rebellion, and changing allegiances precede the restored eastern polity | Later memorials organize this past into loss-and-restoration rhetoric, not disinterested chronology |
| 682–744: second Türk empire | Orkhon Old Turkic memorial texts; Kül Tigin monument 732, Bilgä Qağan monument 735 | Couple heavenly authorization, political memory, monumental landscapes, and elite display; Tonyuquq's precise monument date remains debated |
| 744–840: Uyghur empire; subsequent oasis polities | Runiform writing coexists with other literacies; Manichaean and Buddhist textual histories require regional staging | Conversion, translation, urban centers, and mobile pastoralism need not replace one another in a single step |
| Tenth–twelfth-century Islamic Turkic settings | Arabic-script traditions and new religious/intellectual registers; *Kutadgu Bilig* belongs here, not at Kül Tigin's court | Rework royal fortune through an Islamic mirror-for-princes context rather than treating later meanings as unchanged eighth-century doctrine |
| Modern Kazakh/Kyrgyz/Karakalpak yurt practice | Living, differentiated Turkic languages and heritage institutions | Valuable construction and vocabulary comparators; not direct photographs of the early medieval household |

## Language family and writing: design the sound–script interface

- Old Turkic is an early attested Turkic language, not a synonym for reconstructed Proto-Turkic or the direct unchanged ancestor of every modern branch.
- Turkic is distinct from Mongolic and Tungusic. Shared suffixing structure, vowel harmony, and imperial vocabulary do not by themselves establish a genetic “Altaic” family; that macrofamily remains disputed.
- Useful structural baseline: predominantly suffixing morphology, vowel harmony, head-final constructions, and subject–object–verb ordering. These are linguistic patterns, not explanations of national character (Tekin 1968).
- “Runiform” describes visual resemblance to runes. It does not establish descent from Germanic rune alphabets, common religion, or common ethnicity.
- Orkhon writing commonly runs right to left; several consonants have front-/back-vowel-associated signs, while vowels are not uniformly written in full. Do not mistake selective spelling for a language with only the written vowel distinctions.
- Script-origin hypotheses often involve Iranian/Aramaic-derived writing traditions; exact routes and indigenous contributions are debated. A completely demonstrated Sogdian-to-Orkhon family tree is **not** assumed here (to-verify).
- A tamga, an inscriptional letter, and a decorative motif must be catalogued separately even when their outlines resemble one another.
- The Kül Tigin and Bilgä memorials combine Turkic and Chinese textual representation. Language choice can distinguish audiences without mapping neatly onto “native” versus “foreign” ownership.
- Script use is not a one-script-per-people law: stone commemoration, merchant correspondence, administration, and religious translation can favor different literacies.
- **Apply:** specify phonemes and harmony first; then choose which distinctions the invented script marks, omits, or disambiguates through context. Define different documentary registers before drawing ornamental letters.

## Mythic and divine core: a small attested set, not a complete pantheon

| Name/concept | Attested or securely grounded use | Limit on extrapolation |
|---|---|---|
| *teŋri* / Tengri | Sky/heaven and divine authorization; inscriptional political agency | Context decides whether “sky,” “Heaven,” or a deity-name is the least misleading rendering |
| Umay | Kül Tigin east 31 compares the ruler's mother, a *qatun*, to Umay; Clauson also records childbirth/placenta associations in later lexical evidence | Maternal protection is grounded; a complete Olympian-style biography or universal goddess hierarchy is not |
| Sacred *yer sub* (“earth–water”) | Sacred terrestrial powers/landscape language in inscriptional evidence | Do not automatically turn this collective expression into one named earth goddess |
| Ötüken | Sacral-political landscape associated with the imperial center | Do not reduce its significance to a generic “sacred mountain” interchangeable with any peak |
| Ancestors and commemorated rulers | Memorial complexes and origin traditions make descent and remembered service politically consequential | A ruler's memorial account is not proof that every subject shared an identical ancestor cult |

- Locator convention: **KT** = Kül Tigin inscription; **E/S/N** = east/south/north face, followed by line. Check Tekin (1968) against the inscription rather than quoting an unattributed online translation.
- Useful anchors: KT E1 for sky/earth and human order; KT E10 for divine and territorial protection; KT E31 for Umay and the *qatun*; KT S9 for *qut* and accession. Clauson (1972), s.v. *kut*, *umay*, and *ıduk*, supplies parallel lexical locators.
- “Tengrism” is a modern organizing label, not evidence for a single eighth-century church, closed scripture, or uniformly standardized theology.
- Later Siberian accounts of Erlik, detailed world layers, or specialist rituals need their own locality and recording date before inclusion. Their presence in an “ancient Turkic pantheon” chart is insufficient evidence.

## Qut: heavenly favor, not an automatic loanword diagnosis

- Normalize *qut* and Turkish-style *kut* as alternative scholarly spellings here; do not treat them as two separate concepts.
- Clauson (1972:594a, s.v. *kut*) traces a semantic range from heavenly favor through good fortune to happiness, with differing later developments, including soul/life-force senses in some languages.
- Consequently, “divine mandate” is a useful **political-context gloss**, not an exhaustive dictionary translation or proof of a codified constitutional doctrine.
- KT S9 connects Heaven's authorization and the speaker's possession of *qut* with accession. KT E31 also attributes *qut* to the ruler's mother: it is not exclusively a male sovereign's technical office.
- The design coupling is **superhuman favor → successful provisioning/protection → public claim to legitimate rule**. This is a model distilled from royal rhetoric, not an assertion that every succession obeyed a fixed legal test.

| Requested “pre-Turkic borrowing case” | Calibration | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| Sacral sovereignty existed before the Türk empires | Earlier steppe imperial comparison is well grounded (Di Cosmo 2002) | An incoming dynasty can inherit and refashion an older political idiom |
| Chinese heavenly mandate and Turkic *qut* can be compared | **Analogy** does not identify direction, date, or mechanism of transfer | Contrast legitimate descent, performance, and divine favor without declaring the institutions identical |
| The word *qut* was borrowed from a specified pre-Turkic language | No donor, dated form, and sound correspondence are established by the evidence assembled here (to-verify) | Keep a proposed donor in a hypothesis field; do not publish it as settled etymology |
| Similar sacral vocabulary proves common ancestry | Methodologically invalid without independent historical-linguistic evidence | Test inheritance, loan, calque, and independent parallel separately |
| *Qağan* predates Türk imperial use in earlier steppe titulature | A firmer institutional-transmission comparator, notably Rouran usage (Golden 1992) | Distinguish borrowing an office/title from borrowing a word for fortune; ultimate title etymology is another question |

- **Borrowing test:** require an earlier donor attestation, plausible contact channel, regular adaptation of form, and compatible historical meaning. Similar English translations satisfy none of these alone.
- **Apply:** let a fictional dynasty adopt an older ceremony but rename its authority with a native word; alternatively retain a prestigious foreign title while changing its theology. These are different historical mechanisms and should leave different lexical strata.

## Ashina wolf ancestry: keep the witnesses and narrative roles separate

- The Ashina ruling grouping is associated with wolf-ancestry stories reported in Chinese histories, especially *Zhoushu* 50; compare Sinor (1982), Golden (2018), and Kincses-Nagy (2019:131–132).
- In a familiar reported version, a mutilated surviving boy is sustained by a she-wolf; she becomes pregnant, escapes to a mountain refuge/cavern, and bears sons from whom lineages arise. This is an origin narrative, not biological history.
- Details, genealogies, and emphases vary across witnesses. Do not silently combine their readings into one supposedly original sacred text.
- Distinguish **Ashina, the dynastic/clan designation**, from modern retellings naming a she-wolf “Asena.” An ancient witness for that precise personal name must be produced before using it as an attested historical detail (to-verify).
- The wolf can serve ancestry, rescue, martial analogy, or guidance. A wolf **guide** in an Oghuz narrative is not automatically the Ashina **ancestress** under another spelling.
- Kincses-Nagy (2019) compares changing Oghuz narratives and Islamic adaptations; these later witnesses must not be placed wholesale in an eighth-century court.
- “Totemism” is an interpretive framework in older scholarship, not a proven universal social system inferred from any animal ancestor. Record who uses the term and what evidence supports it.
- **Apply:** define who owns the descent claim, who repeats it, and who benefits from it. Subjects can accept a dynasty's story without all claiming the same ancestry; rival houses can preserve incompatible versions.

## Symbolic system: function and medium before a universal meaning

| Element | Grounded association | Productive coupling | Common failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wolf | Dynastic ancestry reports; martial comparison in imperial discourse | Lineage narrative ↔ audience ↔ emblem-bearing office | Making all Turkic peoples one wolf-worshipping nation |
| *Kök* / sky-blue vocabulary | Sky and color meanings intersect | Lexical polysemy ↔ elevated political language | Declaring every blue textile a fixed priestly or royal uniform |
| *Tamga* | Marks used in affiliation, ownership, or authority contexts | Reproducible sign ↔ marked object ↔ authorized user | Treating every similar mark as one clan, alphabet, or coat of arms |
| Ötüken and sacred terrain | Landscape is bound to political continuity and protection | Route/center ↔ legitimate rule ↔ memorial geography | Reading sacred geography as a purely decorative map feature |
| Monument and commemorative complex | Durable political memory in a mobile imperial society | Written address ↔ elite commemoration ↔ future audience | Assuming mobile pastoralists cannot commission monumental architecture |
| Roof crown in modern heritage | A structural element can bear household and collective identity | Craft inheritance ↔ domestic vocabulary ↔ later national symbol | Backdating a modern emblem and its complete interpretation to 732 |

- **Calibration:** do not assign a pan-Turkic table of direction–color–animal correspondences without a dated source for the particular community. Exact correlations in this early base remain (to-verify).
- **Apply:** every emblem record needs owner, medium, authorized occasion, audience, and change history. Reuse across belt fittings, banners, and manuscripts requires evidence or an explicitly fictional design decision.

## Yurt vocabulary: distinguish site, dwelling, frame, and component

Clauson (1972), s.v. *yurt*, documents early “abandoned campsite” senses and later abode/country meanings. English “yurt” does not simply reproduce the earliest Turkic category boundary.

| Term | Language/era label | Working sense and design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| *yurt* | Early Turkic attestations; later senses broaden | Campsite/abandoned camp in early contexts; later dwelling-place and country. A place-name can outlast its movable house |
| *eb / ev* | Old Turkic and later Turkic forms | House/dwelling vocabulary; do not infer masonry from an English “house” translation |
| *kerekü* | Old Turkic textual/lexicographical evidence | Tent framework/tent; not a license to equate every attested tent with one modern construction plan |
| *kiiz üy*; *boz üy* | Modern Kazakh; modern Kyrgyz | “Felt house”; “grey house.” These names foreground material/color rather than the campsite |
| *kerege* | Modern Kazakh and Kyrgyz | Lattice wall/frame vocabulary; a domestic technical lexicon can distinguish replaceable frame units |
| *uıq*; *uuk* | Modern Kazakh; modern Kyrgyz | Roof-pole vocabulary; keep forms assigned to their languages rather than creating an undated mixed glossary |
| *shañyraq*; *tündük* | Modern Kazakh; modern Kyrgyz | Roof crown/opening vocabulary; distinguish structural part, opening, and symbolic extension in the local usage |

- Modern labels above are a provisional comparative glossary, not an official unified orthography. Auxiliary locator: Wikipedia contributors (2026), [“Yurt,” terminology/component tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yurt); this tertiary overview does not replace language-specific dictionaries. Exact local spellings, senses, and historical development remain (to-verify), especially the Kyrgyz component vocabulary.
- UNESCO (2025) describes a collapsible wooden circular frame, felt covering, ropes, and transmitted craft skills. It is living-practice evidence, not a reconstruction of an Ashina ruler's tent.
- Frame construction, cover manufacture, ornament, transport, repair, and assembly are separate skill bundles. Record their workers and ownership arrangements rather than presuming one household member does everything.
- UNESCO describes contemporary gendered craft participation; this cannot establish an unchanged early medieval division of labor.
- **Apply:** derive a dwelling lexicon from the actual assembly sequence; then distinguish the house, its frame, its occupied site, the household, and the territory. Allow metaphorical “home/country” extensions only through a stated history.
- Andrews (1999) is the specialist comparative reference for felt tents and princely tentage; exact sixth–eighth-century construction details and tent-specific rank rules need source-level checking (to-verify).

## Imperial titulature: offices are not a single frozen ladder

| Form used here | Working role in early Türk contexts | Naming/system implication |
|---|---|---|
| *qağan* (qaghan, khagan, kağan) | Supreme imperial ruler | Distinguish an accession title from a birth name; broader steppe circulation predates these empires |
| *qatun* (khatun, hatun) | Royal woman/consort, especially the ruler's consort | A named royal office and sacral representation, not a generic synonym for every woman |
| *tegin / tigin* | Dynastic prince | A rank element in names such as Kül Tigin; do not translate it as an ordinary family surname |
| *yabğu / yabghu* | High ruler/deputy or regional dynastic authority, context-dependent | A western power-holder's title cannot be assigned one invariant relation to all other titles |
| *šad / shad* | Dynastic command/governorship | Couple territorial or military responsibility to appointment and descent |
| *beg* | Lord/chief; elite designation with variable scale | “Noble” is an approximate gloss, not proof of a European feudal estate |
| *tarqan / tarkhan* | High title whose functions and privileges vary across settings | Do not import every later tax exemption into the earliest attestation |
| *tudun* | Official associated with supervision/administration, including subordinate populations | Gives an empire a vocabulary for delegated control beyond its central court |

- Baseline references: Golden (1992); Tekin (1968), texts/glossary; Clauson (1972), relevant headwords. Hierarchy, jurisdiction, inheritance, and translation must be fixed **per polity and date**.
- Similar title forms across Turkic, Iranian, Mongolic, and Chinese records often reflect contact. Ultimate etymologies of several titles remain disputed; a native-looking form is not proof of native invention.
- Do not assume a universal *qağan > qan > yabğu > šad* chart. A title can change value, survive political fragmentation, or be claimed competitively.
- **Apply:** maintain separate fields for office, personal name, throne epithet, kin relationship, and external diplomatic rendering. Let an outsider transliterate the office instead of reproducing its full institutional meaning.

## Costume and material signature: mobility plus exchange, not ethnic uniform

- Use a **constraint–technique–status** analysis: riding and weather constrain clothing; fiber processing and tailoring determine solutions; wealth and office affect elaboration. None of those alone identifies language or ancestry.
- A belted coat/tunic, trousers, and boots form a useful comparative riding ensemble, not a diagnostic ethnic costume. Exact cuts, overlap direction, headgear, and gender differences require dated depictions or excavated garments (to-verify).
- Start garment vocabulary cautiously: *ton* can designate clothing/a garment rather than one immutable coat pattern; *börk* is headgear vocabulary, not proof of one modern national cap (Clauson 1972, relevant headwords).
- Felt, wool textiles, leather, metal fittings, and imported luxury cloth belong to different production/exchange chains. Do not make pastoral production synonymous with economic isolation.
- Stark (2008; also his 2015 Bard Graduate Center seminar summary) stresses Türk elite participation in cultural exchange and self-representation between China, the Iranian world, and Byzantium.
- Accordingly, imported or appropriated court styles need not contradict local legitimacy. A foreign textile can become a prestigious medium for a dynasty's own political language.
- Belt fittings and elite display must be interpreted by archaeological context; a portable object is not a self-authenticating ethnic badge. Rank-by-plaque-count or universal color laws require independent evidence (to-verify).
- Keep a reconstruction ledger: artifact/depiction → date/place → depicted wearer → identifiable construction → proposed interpretation. Missing fabric survives as a gap, not permission to invent its color.
- **Apply:** build variants by season, wealth, duty, and contact history. Give maintenance and repair their own vocabulary; do not dress every person as an armed royal envoy.

## Naming practices: semantic transparency without modern surname assumptions

| Layer | Reference example | Reusable mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Auspicious quality | *Qutluğ*, “possessing/endowed with qut” | Productive derivation can put a political-religious value into a personal name; the suffix is not English word concatenation |
| Valued epithet | *Bilgä*, “wise” | A quality word becomes a title/name component without implying every bearer is literally wise |
| Office/rank | *Qağan*, *Qatun*, *Tigin* | Title-bearing forms should not automatically enter the birth-name list |
| Clan/dynastic identification | Ashina in Chinese accounts | A recorded clan designation and a personal name need different fields; transcription mediates the form |
| Opaque transmitted name | Tonyuquq | Preserve uncertainty; a confident folk translation of its full etymology remains (to-verify) |

- Use Tekin (1968) and Clauson (1972) to distinguish attested morphology from a modern name generator. *Qut* plus a productive possessive adjective-forming suffix is a pattern; random “steppe-sounding” syllables are not.
- Chinese spellings, modern Turkish conventions, and philological transcriptions can refer to the same historical individual. Index variants rather than manufacturing extra people.
- An inscription may foreground office, achievement, and kinship while omitting information a modern passport would require. Do not infer universal hereditary surnames from that documentary form.
- **Apply:** define the occasion on which a name/title is acquired, who can utter it, and whether translators preserve or translate its meaning. Do not give every social stratum imperial throne names.

## How to apply to a fictional culture: a coupling worksheet

1. Choose a specific comparative slice: restored eastern empire, western diplomatic court, later oasis setting, or modern craft analogy. Mark every cross-era import deliberately.
2. Define the political problem: coalition maintenance, succession, provisioning, tributary oversight, or legitimation. Choose an institution before selecting its exotic-looking title.
3. Assign separate mechanisms to native inheritance, borrowed words, semantic calques, and appropriated ceremonies. Keep the *qut* donor hypothesis unresolved unless actual evidence supports it.
4. Choose the narrative owner: a royal lineage, ritual household, military group, or later chronicler. Specify alternative witnesses instead of flattening the tradition.
5. Bind speech and image through a stated action: an accession formula, a memorial address, a marked object, or a dwelling assembly. Decide who can interpret it and who cannot.
6. Bind material choices to production: who obtains timber, makes felt, repairs straps, tailors cloth, and supplies luxury imports? Then generate the relevant technical vocabulary.
7. Stress-test a succession failure, a relocated court, and a change of religion. Which names, titles, symbols, and garments persist, change meaning, or become contested?
8. Hand lore-shokunin a **proposal**, not a canon assertion: system options, dated comparators, linguistic forms, uncertainty labels, and consequences for other cultures. Lore owns selection and consistency in the world bible.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- A mobile court can make durable monuments and use several literacies; “nomadic” and “textual” are not opposites.
- Legitimacy vocabulary can couple divine favor, maternal authority, remembered service, and political performance without a neatly enumerated pantheon.
- An adopted imperial office can predate its new ruling language; political inheritance need not equal biological or linguistic continuity.
- Animal ancestry, military comparison, and animal guidance are distinct narrative functions even when the animal is the same.
- A dwelling's site, frame, component parts, household, and homeland can form a historically layered semantic network.
- Dress and emblem systems become convincing through materials, makers, access, and audiences—not through a universal list of “Turkic meanings.”

## Provenance cautions and checkable routes

- **Inscriptional bias:** a ruler's account is evidence for self-presentation as well as events. Preserve the named speaker and memorial setting; translations are interpretations, not neutral substitutes for Old Turkic.
- **Chronicle bias:** Chinese dynastic histories mediate steppe narratives through court categories and political relationships. They are indispensable witnesses, not transparent recordings of a single indigenous original.
- **Colonial/evolutionist bias:** later ethnographies and older comparative scholarship may treat living communities as survivals of a primitive stage. Reject a ladder from “nomad/shaman” to “civilized/monotheist”; audit each claimed continuity separately.
- **Modern politics:** wolf imagery also circulates in modern Turkish nationalism (Kincses-Nagy 2019:132, n.16). Contemporary resonance is not evidence for ancient meaning and may matter to the eventual audience.
- **Coverage limit:** this entry is strongest on elite eastern Old Turkic language and memorial ideology. Household ritual, women's complete wardrobes, western regional variation, and exact early tent construction are thin here; missing specifics remain (to-verify).
- **Verification boundary:** bibliographic identity is not equivalent to checking every page of a monograph. Tent-history details and garment reconstructions above are explicitly conditional; never remove their flags merely because a bibliography exists.
