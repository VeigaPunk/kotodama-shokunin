## Working model: a network, not one civilizational costume

- **Persianate** names participation in prestigious literary, administrative, aesthetic, and ethical practices; it does not mean ethnically Persian, uniformly Persian-speaking, or exclusively Muslim. [AL, BU]
- “Mughal” here centers the dynasty and its patronage networks, not all South Asian societies between 1526 and 1857; Rajput, Deccani, regional, village, and trading communities have distinct histories.
- Use three linked scales: **imperial claim → patronage institution → situated speaker/wearer**. A court painting primarily documents the first two; it only partly reveals the third.
- Operational evidence ladder: dated inscription/object → contextualized administrative or narrative passage → multiple related images → later dictionary/traveler → modern costume reconstruction.
- This is a task-specific triangulation order, not an absolute ranking: a hostile chronicle and a flattering portrait require different corrections.
- Calibration labels: **attested** = source supports the stated case; **interpretation** = analytical explanation; **design transfer** = fictional mechanism; **(to-verify)** = unresolved identification or historical extension.

## Era staging: attach register and garment to a patronage moment

| Stage | Linguistic/institutional emphasis | Visual/material staging rule |
|---|---|---|
| Babur and Humayun, 1526–56 | Timurid/Chaghatay inheritance beside Persian prestige; conquest, exile, restoration | Do not dress these courts as a fully developed Shah Jahan durbar; Mughal rule in north India was interrupted by Sur rule, 1540–55 |
| Akbar, 1556–1605 | Persian consolidation, translation projects, multilingual recruitment; Brajbhasha patronage also matters | Collaborative illustrated narratives and expanded workshops; read wardrobe regulations against this court, not against an eternal “Indian style” [AF, AL, BU, M02] |
| Jahangir, 1605–27 | Connoisseurship, memoir, increasingly individualized artistic recognition | Portraits, natural studies, and albums become especially useful; the shift is an emphasis, not the disappearance of narrative books [M03] |
| Shah Jahan, 1628–58 | Highly articulated imperial presentation and court history | Formal audience scenes, idealized hierarchy, costly surface detail; [OBJ] supplies a dated accession-era portrait, not every courtier’s uniform |
| Aurangzeb, 1658–1707 | Changing imperial patronage, religious politics, and an extended Deccan theater | Do not translate personal austerity or particular patronage decisions into the extinction of music, painting, or luxury everywhere [BR] |
| After 1707, through 1857 | Reconfigured sovereignty and regional patronage; later Urdu literary developments need their own chronology | Delhi, Awadh, and other centers cannot be collapsed into one “decadent late Mughal” wardrobe; Awadh’s court culture, crafts, and dress are studied on their own evidence [M03; TR] |

- Keep **reign**, **object manufacture**, **represented event**, **album assembly**, and **later restoration** as separate date fields.
- A calendar is also political design: Hijri dating, regnal years, and Akbar’s Ilahi reckoning answer different institutional needs; do not convert by a single constant offset.
- Akbar’s Ilahi era was introduced in 1584 and reckoned from his 1556 accession; solar ceremonial time can couple royal legitimacy, inventory order, and gift occasions. [AF, MO]
- Customs can outlast rulers or change within one reign; period boundaries are research scaffolding, not synchronized culture resets.

## Language family, writing, and register ecology

| Repertoire | Family / writing distinction | Social function and coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Persian | Iranian branch of Indo-European; Perso-Arabic writing, with nasta‘liq especially prestigious | Administration, historiography, poetry, correspondence, cultivated comportment; a learned repertoire, not proof of the speaker’s ancestry [AL] |
| Chaghatay / Chagatai | Turkic, not an Iranian language; also written in an Arabic-derived script | Timurid dynastic memory and early Mughal connections; sharing letters with Persian does not imply shared grammar |
| Hindavi/Hindi/Hindustani labels | Indo-Aryan; Perso-Arabic and Indic-script witnesses require text-by-text dating; the labels have shifting reference | Everyday communication and literary/contact varieties must be distinguished; do not read each old label as a modern standardized language [MA, AL] |
| Brajbhasha | Indo-Aryan literary repertoire; Nagari transmission alongside other manuscript practices; circulated beyond Braj | Krishna-centered devotional expression and courtly praise/aesthetic expertise coexist; not merely “folk speech below Persian” [BU] |
| Sanskrit | Indo-Aryan learned language; script choice varies by region and manuscript | Scholarly, ritual, and translation networks; Persian translations do not erase the source tradition or prove universal Sanskrit literacy |
| Arabic | Semitic, unlike Persian and Hindustani; Arabic script, with different calligraphic hands by function | Qur’anic, legal, scholarly, and devotional authority; Arabic-derived vocabulary can travel through Persian rather than direct bilingual contact |
| Dakhni | An Indo-Aryan literary/contact history in the Deccan | Compare with northern developments, but do not make it a late provincial copy of imperial Delhi; stage it on Deccan evidence — Tughluqid transfer, the Bahmanid phase, then Golconda and Bijapur court patronage of Dakhani poetry [IR] |

- Language ≠ script ≠ religion: record each separately; neither Perso-Arabic letters nor Persian competence guarantees a Muslim author. [AL, BU]
- **Register stack**: an official’s Persian petition, vernacular negotiation, Braj praise poem, and Arabic religious formula can coexist without a single blended “national tongue.”
- **Contact versus creole:** interpret “Hindustani creolization” as cultural/register contact unless a specific linguistic argument is supplied; Hindustani is Indo-Aryan, not established as a Mughal-camp pidgin that became a creole. [MA]
- Mixed vocabulary alone does not demonstrate a pidgin/creole genesis. Model inherited grammar, multilingual acquisition, borrowing, leveling, and register selection separately.
- The Turkic etymological association of *urdu* with a camp does not establish where or when the language originated; a word history is not a population-language history. [PL; origin narratives require independent verification]
- Useful fictional contact fields: fiscal office → borrowed administrative nouns; atelier → technique terms; court → honorifics; devotion → formulas; household → persistent local kin terms. These are hypotheses to test, not fixed distributions.
- A lexical inventory may contain Persian *izafat* constructions beside Indo-Aryan possessive patterns; borrowing a title does not require importing all donor-language morphology.
- Retain phonological alternatives: learned /q, x, ɣ/ and local adaptations need not be distributed identically across speakers. Modern description documents both retention and substitution of these loans [IS12], but no source in this evidence base records an occupational, regional, or chronological distribution for the Mughal period; treat that spread as an open design choice, not a documented one.
- Transliteration warning: “kh” can disguise either /x/ or /kʰ/ under different systems. Maintain original script, source romanization, normalized form, and pronunciation as separate fields.

## Naming practices: a person is not a modern first-name/surname pair

| Component | Historical pattern | Design use / restraint |
|---|---|---|
| Personal name | Arabic, Persian, Turkic, and Indic resources appear in overlapping networks | Do not assign one vocabulary to one biological population |
| Regnal style | Prince Khurram becomes Shah Jahan; Jahangir is an imperial name/style | Maintain a life-stage alias table; a new title can enact a changed political relationship |
| Honorific/title | Khan, Mirza, Begum and other styles have histories and context-dependent privileges | They are not interchangeable suffixes, a caste table, or proof of the language spoken at home |
| Geographic association | Forms such as “of Tabriz” or “of Shiraz” locate artists in court accounts [AF, pp. 107–108] | Separate birthplace, training network, migration claim, and later attribution |
| Poetic name | A *takhallus* belongs to literary identity and signature conventions | Store separately from office, household name, and dynastic designation |
| Women’s styles | Titles such as Nur Jahan and Mumtaz Mahal are not modern inherited surnames | Record name/title chronology and who uses each form; the grant dates themselves diverge by source — “Nur Mahal” from the 1611 marriage and “Nur Jahan” from 1616 [FI], while standard reference accounts place Mumtaz Mahal’s title at the 1628 accession [EB] — so name the account a chosen date follows instead of treating one as settled |

- Transparent Persian compounds can assert world-order: *Jahangir* roughly “world-seizer,” *Shah Jahan* “king of the world”; do not turn these title translations into an ordinary naming template for everyone. [PL]
- One record should carry **attested spelling → transliteration variants → title/date → kin or patron relationship → source**, rather than forcing all references into one canonical modern spelling.
- Design transfer: promotion can change title, robe entitlement, address formula, and position at an audience together; none needs to change the bearer’s household language.

## Mythic and sacred core: distinguish traditions and truth claims

| Layer | Referents / repertoire | What not to flatten |
|---|---|---|
| Islamic sacred history | One God, prophets, Qur’anic authority; Muhammad is a prophet | This is not a pantheon of gods with saints as lesser deities |
| Sufi sacred geography | Saints, lineages, shrines, blessing, pilgrimage; Chishti connections matter to Mughal rule | A saint’s authority, a jurist’s authority, and imperial authority can cooperate or compete; “Islam” is not one court office [MO] |
| Timurid/Persian kingship | Dynastic genealogy, exemplary rulers, Iranian epic resources, royal glory | Rustam or Iskandar can supply literary comparison without becoming objects of a single imperial cult |
| South Asian traditions | Vishnu/Krishna/Rama, Shiva, Devi, epic and devotional repertoires in distinct communities | “Hindu pantheon” is an inadequate substitute for identifying a text, sect, locality, audience, and patron [BU] |
| Translation and romance | *Razmnama* as a Persian Mahabharata; *Hamzanama* as illustrated heroic romance | A translation is a selected mediation; Hamza romance is not simply a visual transcription of Qur’anic scripture [M02, M03, ST] |
| Imperial sacrality | Auspicious ruler, saintly affinities, light, millennial expectation | Moin’s sacred-kingship model is an interpretation of connected practices, not proof that every subject shared one theology [MO] |

- Akbar’s *sulh-i kul* (“peace with all”) is a political-ethical formulation, not evidence that coercion or confessional disagreement ceased. [AL, MO]
- Avoid the textbook shortcut “Akbar invented one syncretic mass religion.” Imperial discipleship and court ideology require separate analysis from population-wide religious practice. [MO]
- For fiction, let rival narrators classify one ceremony differently: homage, saintly blessing, improper worship, or diplomatic courtesy. Specify whose vocabulary the narration adopts.
- Evidence for nonelite understandings of court theology is much thinner than evidence for elite self-presentation; a reconstructed popular consensus is not available from this source base and is not supplied here.

## Symbolic grammar: bind motif to carrier, audience, and date

| Carrier | Attested or analytical reading | Coupling test |
|---|---|---|
| Radiating halo and hovering angels | [OBJ] explicitly identifies imperial rank and European-derived angels | Imported visual devices can enter royal legitimation without importing the donor’s whole theology |
| Solar/light language | Royal glory and sacral rulership are productive interpretive contexts [MO] | Ask whether light appears in titulature, audience ritual, dating, and cloth classification, not just decoration |
| Royal portrait pendant | [OBJ] depicts Shah Jahan holding a pendant containing his own portrait | Separate an image’s claim about imperial presence from an independently documented rule of distributing portrait jewels |
| Calligraphy with painting | Albums combine valued writing and images, sometimes of different dates [M03, OBJ] | Read inscription, image, border, and mounting as potentially different commissions |
| Floral, animal, and landscape motifs | Natural studies and decorative systems vary by atelier and patron [M03, ST] | Do not assign an eternal meaning to “lotus,” “cypress,” “lion,” or a color from an uncited symbol dictionary |

- A halo is a represented rank claim, not a sewing instruction; a painted jewel can be ideological amplification as well as material evidence.
- Color hierarchies in a wardrobe inventory describe that classificatory operation; they do not automatically explain mourning, marriage, military rank, or village dress.
- Design transfer: make symbols travel through gifts, translations, artist migration, and copied books; retain disagreement and partial understanding rather than a perfectly coherent universal code.

## Atelier vocabulary and the social life of an image

| Term / term cluster | Working gloss | Craft use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| *kar-khana / karkhana* | Workshop or organized production department | Connect material supply, oversight, wages, and patronage; do not picture a modern mechanized factory [AF] |
| *kitab-khana* | Book-house/library, with production functions in relevant contexts | Distinguish storage, copying, painting, and binding; library administration (inspection, valuation, librarian succession) is documented for the imperial collection [SE97], while production is better modelled as a network of collaborating hands than as one department, and staffing for any single reign is only partly known [YR; MAP] |
| *tasvir; musavvir* | Image/depiction; image-maker or painter | [AF, p. 107] names the art of likeness-making; spelling normalization must not be taken from OCR alone |
| *katib; khushnavis* | Writer/scribe; fine writer/calligrapher | A literary manuscript’s calligrapher and an administrative clerk are not automatically the same job [AF, Ain 34; PL] |
| *darogha* | Overseer/superintendent | [AF, p. 107] places overseers and clerks in the presentation of painters’ work; a rank-bearing administrative process, not solitary inspiration |
| *tarh; ‘amal; chehra* | Design; execution/work; face | Useful attribution vocabulary in Mughal painting studies; a dated case does read as a divided workflow — V&A O9627 carries the contemporary librarian’s note “Tarh Basawan / Amal Tara Kalan,” catalogued as composition / work [VA] — but notes of this kind are themselves objects of study, with the practice not visible before 1580, so read any particular inscription against its own catalog entry [SE87] |
| *muraqqa‘* | Album/assembled collection | Track selection, juxtaposition, mounting, and recontextualization, not just an original painter’s intention [M03, PL] |

- [AF, pp. 107–108] describes weekly review, rewards or salary increases, named masters, and differentiated skills; this is courtly praise of an administered craft, not an independent payroll audit.
- [M02] supports collaboration among artists with different backgrounds under Akbar; [M03] describes Jahangir’s preference for a single painter’s work. Do not turn that contrast into an absolute ban on collaboration.
- Keep **language of text**, **artist’s name**, **training**, **patron**, **designer**, **executing hands**, and **later album owner** separate in the evidence record.
- Fictional atelier lexicons should name process bottlenecks—paper preparation, pigment procurement, copying, drawing, coloring, gilding, binding—not merely produce exotic synonyms for “artist.”

## Costume and material signatures: form, substance, and entitlement

| Garment/material family | Usable description | Inference restraint |
|---|---|---|
| *jama* | Robe/upper garment; wrap, fitted torso, and skirt configurations must be identified from the dated case | The word is broader than one stable cutting diagram; Persian and later vernacular uses need not have identical extension [AF, PL] |
| *takauchiyah* | [AF, pp. 88–89] describes an unlined Indian-form coat and an imperial change to skirt and tying arrangement | Preserve Blochmann’s spelling as an edition-specific handle; check the Persian before proposing an etymology |
| *peshwaz* | [AF, p. 89] lists a front-opening coat among imperial clothing | Do not project a later women’s garment category backward as an exclusively feminine sixteenth-century meaning |
| *qaba* and padded variants | Layering, lining, quilting, and cotton filling are meaningful distinctions in [AF, pp. 89–90] | “Long coat” alone loses season, construction, and price information |
| Trousers / *payjama* | Lower-body garment category; cut and ankle treatment need dated observation | The modern English association with sleepwear is not a historical classification [PL, ST] |
| *patka* / waist sash | A wrapped textile can secure the robe, carry equipment, and display costly workmanship | Fiber, weave, and exact court entitlement cannot be established from a painted hanging end alone [ST] |
| Turban and ornaments | Cloth wrapping, silhouette, plume, and jeweled ornament are separate components | Do not infer a wearer’s religion or precise numerical rank from one painted turban [ST] |
| Shawl | Kashmir supply, Lahore manufacture, altered wearing practices, and mixed-fiber products appear in [AF, pp. 91–92] | Neither every shawl nor every fine white cloth is demonstrably modern-category pashmina |
| Brocade, velvet, fine cotton, gold work | Separate fiber, weave, dye/print, embroidery, and surface finish | Visual sheen does not identify all these variables; a miniature alone cannot fix fiber or weave, which take surviving cloth, technical analysis, and textual supplements together [HO22] |

- Particularly useful counterexample: [AF, p. 88] describes Akbar changing a coat’s tying from left to right; Blochmann’s accompanying nineteenth-century note contrasts Hindu and Muslim practice “now-a-days.” The imperial instruction and the translator’s later generalization are different evidence.
- Therefore never use “right tie = Hindu, left tie = Muslim” as a timeless identification key; first establish whose right, the garment’s construction, the represented date, and the source’s voice.
- Paint transparency can suggest a fine fabric but cannot alone establish fiber, thread count, social permission, or whether the effect is a painterly convention.
- Elite male portraits are much better represented in this source set than securely identified women’s everyday clothing or laborers’ wardrobes; women’s images survive in fewer and less securely identified cases [WA17], so leave these extensions open rather than filling them with modern bridal fashion.

## Court dress hierarchy: investiture is an action, not just a silhouette

- **Khil‘at / robe of honor:** treat the gift as a performed relationship between donor and recipient; a particular investiture may involve multiple garments and associated gifts. Never assume one universal bundle or number of pieces. [GO; AF, Ain 74]
- Distinguish **office/rank**, **current favor**, **wealth**, **military obligation**, **audience privilege**, and **ceremonial occasion**; costly dress can intersect all six without encoding them identically.
- Mughal *mansab* and the distinctions involving *zat* and *sawar* concern ranked service and cavalry obligations; they are not a ready-made universal table of robe colors. Verify the institution for the selected reign before assigning exact numerical privileges.
- [AF, pp. 87–92] records attention to manufacture, purchase, gifts, prices, and rank-related clothing; this supports institutional ordering, not a complete surviving sumptuary code for the empire.
- [AF, pp. 91–92] ranks wardrobe entries through arrival time, auspicious day, price, weight, and color: a rare explicit **calendar → bureaucratic record → textile valuation** coupling.
- [AF, Ain 74, pp. 158–159] associates receipt of rank, gifts, and dresses of honor with prescribed salutation; dress, gesture, and political speech belong in one scene model.
- In an audience painting, triangulate garment quality with proximity to the ruler, platform, grouping, inscription, and documented identity; do not read scale or placement as unmediated reportage.
- Fictional design option: a robe grant changes permitted display and forms of address; subsequent confiscation or refusal becomes legible politically. This is a generative mechanism, not an assertion about a specific Mughal incident.

## Miniature costume evidence: a repeatable extraction protocol

1. Capture museum/repository, accession or shelfmark, folio side, attribution confidence, dimensions, medium, and catalog date before naming garments.
2. Separate the scene’s event date from painting date; then separate both from border, reverse, mounting, and album dates.
3. Describe visible features first: closure path, neckline, sleeves, skirt profile, layering, trouser ankle, sash placement, turban outline, jewelry, footwear.
4. Add historical garment terms only where text or a catalog supports the mapping; retain “wrap-front robe” beside the proposed specialist term.
5. Label each finding **visible**, **cataloged**, **textually prescribed**, or **inferred**. An image of cloth is not surviving cloth.
6. Compare related images within a patronage/date group; seek a material object or contemporary textile account before identifying a weave or dye.
7. Record whether the image is an allegory, idealized portrait, narrated past event, or observed ceremony; each permits different deductions.
8. Export an evidence packet and unresolved alternatives to lore-shokunin; do not select the fictional culture’s canon on its behalf.

- Worked anchor: [OBJ] securely supplies Chitarman, a recto dated 1627–28, lavish imperial adornment, halo, angels, and multiple represented crafts. It does **not** supply a robe entitlement for each *mansab*.
- Its verso is cataloged ca. 1530–50: one assembled album folio is not a single-date cultural snapshot. Reused prestigious writing can coexist with a newly painted royal body.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **A prestige network instead of a racial aesthetic:** several communities acquire the same court register while retaining different kinship, devotional, and household vocabularies.
- **A coupled promotion event:** title + address + robe grant + gesture + audience position; change one channel and explain which others follow or resist.
- **An atelier as a contact engine:** migrant teachers, local practitioners, imported images, and administrative review produce innovations without a mythical moment of total cultural fusion.
- **A wardrobe with a cosmology:** auspicious entry dates and ranked material inventories connect sacred time to seemingly practical storage decisions.
- **An archive with layered time:** earlier calligraphy, a current ruler’s portrait, and later mounting can encode possession, ancestry, and selective memory.
- Deliver a proposed system with named speakers, patrons, time windows, material constraints, source pointers, counterexamples, and alternatives; lore-shokunin alone records approved canon.

## Provenance cautions and known thin spots

- [AF] speaks for Akbar’s imperial project; its claims about perfected manufacture and royal insight are interested praise. Keep Abu’l-Fazl’s text, Blochmann’s notes, and OCR errors visibly separate.
- Blochmann’s colonial-era judgments about rationality, religion, and “Hindu/Muhammadan” custom are not transparent sixteenth-century categories; later dictionaries likewise cannot date earlier social rules by themselves.
