## Scope and calibration
- This is a method/reference case, not a fictional culture, canon proposal, or reconstruction of one homogeneous “Persian civilization.” Distinguish Persian dynastic identity from the empire's many populations.
- Evidence labels: **attested** = stated or depicted in a named ancient witness; **interpretive** = scholarly explanation; **contested** = materially different explanations remain; **design transfer** = fictional method, not historical assertion.
- Separate three scales: royal self-presentation; regional institutional practice; household/lived culture. Evidence at one scale does not automatically describe the others.
- The imperial center is not exclusively Persepolis: Susa, Babylon, Ecbatana, and other centers matter; the Fortification archive is a regional and chronological window, not an empire-wide census (Briant 2002; Kuhrt 2007; Hallock 1969).
- Thin areas here: ordinary speech, oral mythology, non-elite clothing, women's ritual experience, and eastern provinces outside the cited dossiers. Surviving court narratives and chronicles are uneven; do not fill gaps with later Iranian epic.

## Era model: stage institutions, not a rise–decadence script
| Stage, BCE | Evidence and coupling | Worldbuilding constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Formation, approximately 550–522 | Cyrus and Cambyses incorporate existing courts, scribal practices, and cult centers; Babylon conquered in 539, Egypt in 525 | Do not portray provincial cultures as blank before conquest or assume Cyrus already used every later royal formula |
| Darius I, 522–486 | Behistun legitimates a contested accession; monumental Old Persian becomes prominent; Susa/Persepolis projects materialize ordered empire | Separate the ruler's claim to restore order from an independent account of his legitimacy |
| Fortification dossier, approximately 509–493 | Elamite records document regional food administration and travelers within Darius's reign | A ration rule belongs first to this place, institution, and date, not automatically to all satrapies |
| Fifth-century successors | Royal visual formulas persist while local archives, revolts, diplomacy, and building histories vary | Keep administrative continuity compatible with political conflict; Greek wars are not the only timeline |
| Fourth century to 330 | Artaxerxes II's A2Sa explicitly invokes Ahuramazda, Anahita, and Mithra; later conquests and regional change precede Macedonian victory | A newly explicit invocation is not proof that a deity was newly invented; avoid automatic “oriental decadence” |
| Reception after 330 | Later Iranian, Zoroastrian, and modern national traditions reinterpret earlier forms | Store reception in a separate era layer; neither Sasanian dress nor modern Faravahar diagrams date themselves to Darius |
- This staging combines source windows and political phases, not a universal periodization law (Briant 2002; Kuhrt 2007; Metropolitan Museum 2004).

## Language family, writing, and functional distribution
| Language | Family/status | Writing and evidenced work | Incorrect inference to reject |
|---|---|---|---|
| Old Persian | Indo-European → Indo-Iranian → Iranian; Southwestern Iranian | Distinct cuneiform system, written left-to-right; royal inscriptions and elite objects dominate the familiar corpus | Not identical to Avestan, Middle Persian, or modern Persian; not the language of every imperial subject |
| Elamite | Conventionally treated as a language isolate; proposed genetic affiliations unestablished | Adapted cuneiform; monumental versions and extensive Persepolis administrative records | An inherited administrative language is not merely a translation code invented by Persian conquerors |
| Babylonian | Babylonian dialect of Akkadian, an East Semitic language | Mesopotamian cuneiform; royal versions, regional legal, scholarly, and economic traditions | “Babylonian” here is not a separate Iranian dialect or a synonym for all cuneiform |
| Aramaic | Northwest Semitic | Alphabetic writing; correspondence/administration across regions, including an Aramaic component at Persepolis | Monumental trilingualism does not exclude a different, widely used administrative medium |
- Old Persian script is conventionally described as semi-alphabetic or partially syllabic: phonetic signs, a limited logogram inventory, numerals, and a word divider. Shared wedge shapes do not mean interchangeable Elamite, Babylonian, and Old Persian sign values (Kent 1953).
- Treat Darius's role in the script's introduction as a historical problem, not an exact invented-on-one-day anecdote. Earlier royal names on inscriptions do not alone date the carving to their lifetime.
- Do not declare Old Persian exclusively monumental: Matthew W. Stolper and Jan Tavernier (2007), [“An Old Persian Administrative Tablet from the Persepolis Fortification,” ARTA 2007.001](http://www.achemenet.com/document/2007.001-Stolper-Tavernier.pdf), publishes an important counterexample, described in the project's own report (Stolper 2007) as an ordinary archive tablet carrying a text in Old Persian language and script and as the first “practical” Old Persian text known; the article's individual text readings are not restated here because its PDF was not inspected this pass.
- Survival is medium-biased: monumental stone, clay records, ink, and perishable writing supports preserve different activities. Archive language does not directly count spoken-language populations.
- Romanizations below are normalized reference forms/stems, not phonetic transcriptions; editions differ in diacritics and spelling conventions. An asterisk marks a reconstructed form, never a directly readable tablet spelling.

## Imperial trilingualism as a canon-language case study
| Surface | What authority does | Coupled design variable |
|---|---|---|
| Behistun: Old Persian, Elamite, Babylonian | Repeats the king's accession/victory claim across scribal traditions, with different composition histories and coverage | Which version counts as authoritative, who translates, and how additions propagate |
| Susa foundation account DSf | Connects divine favor, king, imported materials, and workers from different regions | A building inscription can be a political map of labor and resources, not a neutral procurement ledger |
| DNa: titulary, countries, throne-bearers | Joins verbal enumeration to sculpted peoples supporting kingship | Decide whether clothing, ethnonym, and visual position agree, and who gets classified by outsiders |
| Regional accounting and correspondence | Records entitlements, commodities, orders, and receipts through practical scribal conventions | Choose a work language by transaction and institution, not only by royal ancestry |
- “Canon-language” is an analogy for authorized versions of a ruling narrative, not evidence that royal inscriptions were one standardized sacred scripture.
- DB versions are not mechanically identical: the Old Persian fifth-column appendix has no equivalent in the principal Elamite/Babylonian versions. Do not make every historical discrepancy a translation mistake (Lecoq 1997; [Behistun overview](https://www.livius.org/articles/place/behistun/)).
- DB §70 claims inscription production, reading before the king, and circulation; translation of its script/material wording is itself debated. Use the [passage](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/behistun-persian-text/behistun-t-42/) to model mediated authority, not universal public literacy.
- Design transfer: distinguish **dynastic voice**, **authorized translation**, **provincial legal usage**, **oral proclamation**, and **archival record**. These can overlap without having equal social prestige or identical wording.
- Handoff a version matrix to lore-shokunin: proposition → witness language → date → medium → patron → audience → difference → confidence. A fictional discrepancy remains an option until lore accepts it as canon.

## Mythic and pantheon core: what the witnesses actually support
- **Attested, royal cosmogonic formula:** DNa and DSf begin with Ahuramazda creating earth, sky, humanity, and happiness, then making Darius king. Creation and appointment are rhetorically coupled; this is not a surviving narrative epic.
- **Attested, political morality:** DB associates the king's enemies with lying and rebellion; read the identification as royal legitimation. A defeated claimant's being called a liar does not independently prove his story false.
- **Attested, plurality:** A2Sa under Artaxerxes II names Ahuramazda, Anahita, and Mithra. Do not force every royal inscription into a fixed three-god pantheon or infer the absence of other cults from a short invocation.
- **Regional ritual evidence:** Persepolis records and Henkelman's analysis connect Iranian and Elamite divine recipients, notably Ahuramazda and Humban, with institutional provisioning. Shared support need not imply one merged god or uniform theology (Hallock 1969; Henkelman 2008).
- **Contested:** “Were the Achaemenids Zoroastrian?” depends on ruler, definition, and evidence. Ahuramazda devotion and Iranian truth/order language establish an Iranian religious setting; whether that demonstrates specifically Zoroastrian affiliation, and how it relates to later doctrine, remain debated.
- **Contested:** XPh describes suppression of daiva worship; its location, scope, and historical occasion are not securely identified. Neither a blanket empire-wide ban nor a story of unlimited religious tolerance follows automatically.
- **Chronological firewall:** the Avesta is in Avestan, not Old Persian; textual transmission and dating require their own analysis. Ferdowsi's much later Shahnameh is not an Achaemenid court chronicle.
- Design transfer: give royal theology, provincial festival practice, and household devotion separate evidence slots. Pantheon “archetypes” are comparison tools, not a license to reduce Mithra to a universal sun-god slot.

## Winged figure, winged disk, and “Faravahar”
| Claim or feature | Calibration | Safe use |
|---|---|---|
| Winged disk and anthropomorphic winged figure | Attested motifs; distinguish examples with and without a human upper body | Describe the particular relief/seal before assigning one identity to the whole motif family |
| Earlier Egyptian/Near Eastern and especially Assyrian comparanda | Established comparative setting; transmission and local remaking require analysis | Model borrowed visual grammar without claiming unchanged theology or one simple origin chain |
| “This is Ahuramazda” | Influential interpretation, not a universally accepted identification | Record as a named interpretation; proximity to an Ahuramazda inscription is not a direct caption |
| “This is royal glory,” farr/xvarənah | Important scholarly alternative, associated with the debate represented by Shahbazi and Boyce | Keep as an alternative explanation, not a replacement certainty |
| “This is a fravaši / guardian spirit” | Familiar later identification, disputed as an Achaemenid reading | Keep modern/traditional reception distinct from the ancient object's demonstrated meaning |
| Wing layers = good thoughts, good words, good deeds; tail = their opposites | Modern didactic interpretation, not established contemporary Achaemenid exegesis | Valid subject for reception history; do not print as an ancient sculptor's verified code |
| Ring = marriage fidelity; every feather has a fixed doctrine | Modern devotional reading, not ancient exegesis: a Zoroastrian explanatory essay reads the ring as a “ring of covenant, loyalty and faithfulness” and the feather groups as good and bad thoughts, words, and deeds while presenting them as what “some interpreters consider”; no ancient Achaemenid text glosses these elements, and Boyce (2000) records no certain ancient representation of the fravašis | Omit from historical reconstruction; a fictional later commentator could invent such an interpretation |
- “Faravahar” is a useful modern lookup label, not an attested Achaemenid caption identifying the winged figure. Cite Shahbazi (1974) and Boyce (2000) for the identification problem: Boyce's article reports Moulton's fravaši identification, notes that Zoroastrians still generally read a fravaši, and states that strong scholarly arguments have since been made for xvarənah.
- Design transfer: preserve **visible form → placement → patron → contemporary wording → later interpretation** as separate fields. A modern community's meaningful reading need not be either an ancient fact or a worthless “mistake.”

## Other symbolic systems and their media
| System | Evidence and coupling | Constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Throne supported by differentiated peoples | DNa explicitly directs viewers to the throne-bearing figures; text, costume, and political hierarchy cooperate | “Ordered diversity” is a royal claim, not proof of voluntary or equal participation |
| Processions and gifts | Persepolis reliefs stage groups, garments, animals, and valued goods within court architecture | Exact ritual occasion and the identity of every delegation remain interpretive; do not label the whole scene “Nowruz” as settled fact |
| Seals and seal impressions | Administrative authentication also circulates images of power, animals, encounter, and worship | A seal can belong to an office or individual; its use need not express every user's private belief |
| King's bow, elevated scale, attendants, and inscriptions | Court visual composition differentiates the ruler and his support structure | Describe the object and context; avoid a universal symbol dictionary detached from medium |
- Use Curtis and Tallis (2005), DNa, and the Hallock archive together: monument, portable image, and accounting record answer different questions.

## Satrapy and royal-administrative vocabulary
| Form and language | Working gloss and witness | Coupling/translation guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| xšāyaθiya, Old Persian | King; DNa, DB, DSf | A title, not a personal name; translate its position in a formula, not just its dictionary gloss |
| xšāyaθiya xšāyaθiyānām, Old Persian | King of kings; DNa | Nested authority can coexist with provincial or local rulers; it is not a modern federal constitution |
| xšaça-, Old Persian | Kingship, royal power, kingdom; DSf | Do not automatically capitalize it as the state's uniquely official proper name |
| xšaçapāvan-, Old Persian stem; Greek satrapēs | Satrap, conventionally “protector of the realm”; DB III.11–19 / §38 names Dādarši as satrap of Bactria | Governor, not simply “feudal duke”; responsibilities and subordination vary by place/date |
| dahyu-, Old Persian | Country/land/people, depending on context; DNa country list | A royal country-list item is not automatically one administrative satrapy |
| bandaka-, Old Persian normalized form | Royal servant/dependent; DB §38 applies it to the satrap | “Servant” here need not mean chattel slave or low-status domestic worker |
| bāji-, Old Persian | Tribute; DNa, XPh | Separate royal tribute claims from taxes, local dues, gifts, wages, and archive ration distributions |
| dāta-, Old Persian | Law/ordinance; DNa, XPh | Do not infer one surviving written imperial law code or identical provincial legal procedure |
| kurtaš, Elamite administrative usage | Workers/labor personnel; Hallock's labor dossiers | Institutional category alone does not settle every person's freedom, ethnicity, gender, or occupation |
- Direct lexical check: [DB §38, naming the satrap](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/behistun-persian-text/behistun-t-24/); use Kent (1953) for Old Persian morphology and Tavernier (2007) for Iranian names/loans in other languages.
- Design transfer: distinguish **territory**, **office**, **personal dependency**, **revenue**, and **legal act** before inventing words. A rigid office chart copied across all provinces would overstate the evidence (Briant 2002; Kuhrt 2007).

## Royal Road: logistics lexicon, not a speed legend
| Term or lexical field | Provenance and meaning | Practical coupling |
|---|---|---|
| “Royal Road” | Modern conventional label for the famous Sardis–Susa route described in Herodotus 5.52–54; other imperial routes also existed | A network of routes, stores, crossings, and authorities, not one paved transcontinental strip |
| stathmos / stathmoi, Greek | Station/stage in Herodotus's itinerary | Name the place, travel segment, and provisioning unit separately if the fictional system distinguishes them |
| parasangēs, Greek rendering of an Iranian distance term | Distance unit in the itinerary; conversion and actual routes need contextual treatment | Do not force one exact modern kilometer conversion onto every journey |
| angarēion, Greek reporting | Herodotus 8.98's term for the mounted relay system | It is a Greek textual witness; do not silently label the whole spelling an attested Old Persian word |
| pirradaziš, Elamite-recorded term | Hallock translates persons as “fast messenger,” horses as “express”; exact status as a title is uncertain, pp. 6, 42–43 | Separate courier, express-service affiliation, and designated horse; do not turn one word into a complete postal ministry |
| halmi, Elamite | “Sealed document”; authorizing document cited by ration memoranda, Hallock p. 6 | Credentials establish entitlement; the memorandum recording provision is not necessarily the credential itself |
| miyatukkam, Elamite | “Authorization,” Hallock p. 6 | Authority → entitlement → actual issue → accounting record are distinct actions |
| Grain/flour, wine/beer, livestock; supplier and recipient | Material and transactional vocabulary in Fortification records | Courier speed depends on feed, stocks, personnel, and accounting, not horses alone |
- Hallock p. 6 explicitly distinguishes mentioned authorizing documents from the surviving transaction records in his edition; the ration texts do not supply a complete specimen “passport” format.
- Hallock's discussion also cautions against imagining every traveler mounted: travel groups and pack animals must be distinguished from express riders. Exact speeds, relay spacing, and privilege classes require dossier-specific evidence.
- Herodotus's famous snow/rain/heat/night passage praises an organized service; it is not an empirical guarantee of delivery time. Do not equate ordinary travel estimates with uninterrupted relay speed.
- Operational design transfer: origin + destination + issuer + beneficiary + escort/animals + commodity entitlement + issue date + station + seal + acknowledgment. These are design fields distilled from records, not a verbatim universal ancient form.
- Failure scenarios worth modeling: valid seal but exhausted grain store; obsolete entitlement after an office change; courier priority over other travelers; translation mismatch in the recipient's name. These are fictional tests, not claims of recorded incidents.

## Costume and material signature: function before ethnic shorthand
| Reference signature | Evidence level | Coupled craft use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Long, richly folded court robe, headgear, and controlled gesture | Royal/court relief conventions; compare images in Curtis and Tallis (2005) | Couple silhouette to audience and office; stone folds alone do not identify fiber, dye, cut, or daily comfort |
| Sleeved tunic/jacket, trousers, soft cap, and riding-compatible footwear | Conventionally discussed as “Median” dress in court imagery and scholarship | Distinguish mobility, court convention, and depicted identity; no timeless rule that every Mede or rider wore one uniform |
| Delegation-specific caps, cloaks, belts, footwear, and carried goods | Differentiated procession/throne-bearer imagery | Build recognition through combinations, not one hat = one biological ethnicity; individual identifications may be contested |
| Textile color, weave, and precious trim | Must be established from an appropriate object or text, not an uncolored relief | Pigments survive on Persepolis sculpture and are recoverable by technical analysis (Egyptian blue, malachite, cinnabar, hematite, calcite; Ebbinghaus et al. 2023), but colour reconstructions rest on assumptions and have been revised; an exact dye recipe, shade, or empire-wide rank-color code is not established by the sources inspected |
| Animal-protome metalwork, jewelry, and engraved seals | Portable elite material repertoire in Curtis and Tallis (2005) | Rank and authorization may travel on bodies and objects; an elite assemblage is not average household dress |
| Susa's imported timber, stone, metals, and skilled labor | DSf's royal account names origins and workers | Couple a prestige object's lexicon to supply geography, craft specialists, and the ruler's claim to command resources |
- DSf is procurement-shaped royal rhetoric: its material and people lists are valuable, but not a complete audited budget; some ancient material identifications require philological checking.
- Do not paste later Sasanian crowns, medieval Persian robes, or contemporary Iranian garments into an Achaemenid reference without a dated comparison. A design may deliberately blend eras, but must label the blend fictional.
- Lexicon method: separate garment structure, textile, fastening, ornament, occasion, issuer/donor, and wearer rank. Borrowing a court look need not replace a provincial language or cult.

## Naming practices and cross-language identities
| Attested reference or pattern | Use | Caution |
|---|---|---|
| Dārayavauš → Greek-derived Darius; Xšayāršā → Xerxes | Track native reference form and conventional English/Greek-derived form as aliases | They are not separate people merely because sound shape or script differs |
| Kūruš → Cyrus; Artaxšaçā → Artaxerxes | Normalize only after checking ruler, genealogy, place, and date | Repeated royal names require identifiers; a normalized dictionary headword is not every witness's exact spelling |
| DNa: ruler + royal titles + father's name + dynastic/identity claims | Model formal genealogical self-presentation separately from everyday address | A royal lineage claim is political evidence, not automatically an independently verified family tree |
| Iranian name elements such as -aspa- “horse,” arta- truth/order, and baga- “god” | Build a bounded semantic repertoire from Kent/Tavernier's attested entries | Do not concatenate stems at random or infer the bearer’s private creed solely from a name |
| Elamite/Babylonian/Aramaic spellings of Iranian names | Use Tavernier (2007) to distinguish phonological adaptation, scribal convention, and reconstructed forms | Mark reconstruction with *; a scribe's spelling does not demonstrate a separate ethnicity |
| DNa's Sparda and Bābiruš, conventionally Lydia and Babylon/Babylonia | Keep place-name aliases linked to textual context | Countries, cities, peoples, and administrative units are different entity types even when translations look alike |
- Method: name record = witness spelling + language/script + normalized form + office/title + kin relation + date/place + confidence. Never derive a whole conlang from a few royal names.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **A division of linguistic labor:** ancestral/dynastic display, authorized monumental versions, practical administration, regional law, and household speech can be different systems; multilingualism is not universal fluency.
- **An authorization chain:** a distant ruler's legitimacy becomes locally legible through translated formulas, recognizable seals, named officers, commodity entitlements, and acknowledged deliveries.
- **A political theology with limits:** creation and moral order can legitimate kingship while regional cults continue; tolerance, patronage, coercion, and resistance need separate local histories.
- **A layered symbol biography:** record the winged motif's visible features, changing royal use, contested ancient referent, and later religious/national readings without collapsing them.
- **An era-linked costume grammar:** ceremony, riding, office, regional affiliation, and resource supply can cross-cut each other; a costume is not a language-family label.
- **A testable design packet for lore-shokunin:** era matrix + language/function matrix + title/name glossary + symbol evidence/interpretation split + costume/material constraints + one station-provisioning workflow. Submit options and uncertainties; do not record them as world-bible canon here.

## Provenance cautions and stop rules
- Royal inscriptions are commissioned assertions; Greek narratives are outsider accounts with literary and political agendas. Their agreement is useful, but disagreement must not be automatically resolved in either party's favor.
- “Greek bias” is not grounds for discarding Herodotus wholesale. Conversely, a Persian self-presentation is not neutral because it is indigenous; triangulate archaeological context, archives, and different-language witnesses.
- Nineteenth-/twentieth-century Orientalist and colonial-era habits often framed Persia as luxurious despotism opposed to Greek freedom. That contrast is not an explanatory law; neither is a compensatory picture of a universally tolerant, frictionless empire (Briant 2002; Kuhrt 2007).
- Modern religious and national readings have their own history and dignity, but are not automatically ancient exegesis. Especially reject an unqualified equation Faravahar = Ahuramazda = fravaši = royal glory.
- Sparse chronicles do not license importing the Shahnameh, Sasanian religious institutions, or modern ethical diagrams to fill Achaemenid gaps. Date each comparator explicitly — the Shahnameh as an epic completed in 1010 CE, Sasanian institutions as 3rd–7th-century CE, modern ethical diagrams as 19th–20th-century reception — and keep any uncertain connection open rather than settled.
- Reference limits remain explicit: detailed non-elite dress, regional oral myth cycles, and a complete office hierarchy are not reconstructed here. A specific garment name, divine identification, phonetic reconstruction, or route speed needs its own witness before being treated as historical fact.
- Stop at the evidence boundary: retain competing readings, give the relevant source/siglum, and hand the design choice to lore-shokunin. This entry supplies coupled method and reference knowledge only.
