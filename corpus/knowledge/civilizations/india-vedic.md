## Scope and evidence labels

- “Vedic” identifies interrelated language, text, teaching, and ritual traditions, not one unchanging civilization or all ancient South Asian populations.
- Separate Ṛgvedic hymnic composition, subsequent collection/transmission, later Saṃhitā ritual systems, Brāhmaṇa explanation, and Sūtra prescription; a later explanation cannot automatically reconstruct an earlier performance.
- **Attested** below means present in the cited textual witness, not archaeologically demonstrated everyday behavior; **inferred** names a reconstruction; **design** names a transferable fictional mechanism.
- **Contested** marks a live scholarly problem; **unsupported** rejects an evidential leap, not a community's right to religious belief; `(to-verify)` marks unconfirmed particulars.
- Chronological scaffolding is approximate. Hymns and ritual texts are not continuous annalistic chronicles; their selective testimony underrepresents ordinary clothing, nonelite speakers, and regional variation.

## Era staging: relative strata before absolute dates

| Stage | Working historical placement | Coupled systems | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ṛgvedic composition | Broadly second half of the second millennium BCE; exact bounds contested | Old Indo-Aryan poetry; patronage, cattle, chariots, divine praise | JB14 introduction; do not turn hymnic imagery into a complete economy or costume catalogue |
| Collection and later Saṃhitās | Late second / early first millennium BCE, overlapping regional developments | Reorganized priestly repertoires; Ṛc, Sāman, and Yajus performance relations | Systematized later śrauta arrangements are not all demonstrable in the earliest hymns |
| Brāhmaṇa / early Upaniṣadic explanation | First millennium BCE, with differing textual and regional histories | Expanded ritual correspondences, consecration theory, explicit phonetic instruction | Śatapatha and Taittirīya evidence comes from particular schools, not a uniform date |
| Sūtra and auxiliary disciplines | Later Vedic and subsequent first-millennium BCE developments | Compressed procedures; school-specific śrauta, domestic, phonetic, and calendrical expertise | Dates and dependencies of individual works require specialist verification |
| Later and living transmission | Many centuries, continuing into the present | Regional scripts, recitation lineages, adapted material supply and patronage | K15 is a modern Andhra study; modern Kerala or Andhra practice cannot stand for every ancient region |

- **Design:** stage changes independently: vernacular sound change, liturgical conservatism, ritual reform, textile supply, and writing need not advance together.
- “Bronze Age / Iron Age” is material chronology, not a ladder from primitive language to sophisticated religion; even a correct archaeological date does not identify a recitation school.

## Language family, registers, and writing

- Vedic Sanskrit is Old Indo-Aryan within Indo-Iranian within Indo-European; close comparison with Old Iranian is linguistic evidence, not a racial classification (JB14 introduction).
- Inflection, compounds, inherited poetic formulas, metrical alternations, and accent help distinguish the register from later standardized Classical Sanskrit; “Sanskrit” does not mean every attested stage is interchangeable.
- Language family, ritual affiliation, descent claim, and political membership must occupy separate fields. Specific substrate identifications and borrowing histories are contested; word-by-word etymologies need checking rather than ethnic assignment by sound.
- The Vedas were composed and transmitted orally; a manuscript's script and copying date do not date the hymn's composition (JB14; Sa98).
- Brāhmī is securely represented in Aśokan inscriptions of the third century BCE; earlier-writing proposals are separate archaeological questions. Those largely Prakrit inscriptions are not evidence that early Ṛgvedic poets wrote Sanskrit in Brāhmī.
- Devanāgarī is a later script, not “the original Vedic alphabet.” Different regional scripts can transmit Sanskrit; a change of script is not automatically a change of sacred language.
- Indus signs remain undeciphered: identifying them as demonstrated Vedic Sanskrit writing is **unsupported**, whatever hypothesis a fictional culture might itself claim.
- This entry uses normalized scholarly transliteration, not a fully accented edition or pronunciation score. Macrons, retroflex dots, and accent marks encode different information; removing diacritics is lossy.

## Śikṣā: an embodied analysis of sound

- Śikṣā is a Vedic auxiliary discipline of phonetic instruction; Prātiśākhyas regulate the phonetic transmission of particular textual branches, or śākhās (S77; WM11).
- *Taittirīya Upaniṣad* 1.2 explicitly lists the following six teaching concerns (O98). English glosses are aids, not exact modern technical equivalents.

| Concern | Operational reading | Coupling opportunity | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| varṇa | Distinguish speech sounds and their articulation | School training classifies bodily production of authoritative speech | Confusing an oral sound inventory with a particular written script |
| svara | Accent/pitch distinctions | Liturgical names retain distinctions lost in an everyday register | Treating pitch accent as ordinary English stress or one timeless chant melody |
| mātrā | Relative sound duration; vowel quantity matters | Metrical competence constrains correct formulas and names | Assigning a universal milliseconds-per-unit value |
| bala | Articulatory force/effort | Breath and articulatory discipline become teachable expertise | Equating ritual power with loudness |
| sāma | Evenness/modulation; translation varies | Aesthetic delivery participates in correctness | Automatically equating this item with the entire Sāmaveda musical system |
| santāna | Continuity and connection of sounds | Sandhi and phrasing determine how a sequence is transmitted | Treating a word-separated lexical list as the performed text |

- Traditional articulatory ordering distinguishes velar, palatal, retroflex, dental, and labial stop series, with voicing/aspiration distinctions; these offer a useful conlang teaching model, not proof that all languages need Sanskrit's inventory (S77; WM11).
- Accent terminology includes **udātta**, **anudātta**, and **svarita**; these are not three interchangeable names for stress. Distinguish linguistic accent from its school-specific recitational realization: living recitation is transmitted within named school traditions (Larios 2017), so a notation-to-audio reconstruction must name the tradition it follows (S77; WM11).
- Duration terminology distinguishes **hrasva** (short), **dīrgha** (long), and **pluta** (protracted). Relative quantity is a teachable contrast; do not turn it into a universal absolute speaking speed (S77; WM11).
- **Saṃhitāpāṭha:** connected recitation; **padapāṭha:** word-separated analysis; **kramapāṭha:** sequential paired recitation. Compare their different checking functions instead of calling every method “chanting.”
- Oral preservation can be highly exact while analysis, pronunciation traditions, and textual branches still differ. Claims of literally zero variation everywhere are **overstated**.
- Complex permutation recitations (**jaṭā**, **mālā**, **ghana**, and the other eventual *vikṛti* series) and the surviving works attributed to famous sages require their own dating; do not project every later technique into initial Ṛgvedic composition. Technique-by-technique chronology is not established here `(to-verify)`.

## Mantra phonology and the kotodama comparison

- A mantra's ritual placement can couple wording, accent, metre, speaker eligibility, gesture, and timing; correctness is not merely possession of a translated proposition (JB14; WM11).
- Ṛgvedic poetry nevertheless has semantics, argument, metaphor, and social address. “All mantras are meaningless syllables” is **false as a blanket description**; later seed-syllable traditions are not interchangeable with the Ṛgvedic hymn corpus.
- RV 10.71 concerns speech and its uneven recognition among participants; RV 10.125 presents a powerful divine first-person speaker, conventionally associated with Vāc. Use the specific hymns, not an invented universal theory of sonic magic.
- The **kotodama parallel is analytical analogy only**: compare traditions in which speaking is consequential. This entry establishes neither common origin nor identity between Japanese categories and Vedic categories.
- Comparative depth is thin on the Japanese side here: historical schools, periods, and indigenous definitions require a separately sourced dossier `(to-verify)`; do not backfill them from Sanskrit.
- Separate **emic claim** (“this properly performed utterance accomplishes X”) from **historical observation** (“a school taught these distinctions”) and **fictional option** (“speech literally changes matter”). Lore decides whether the last is true in-world.
- ŚB 3.2.1.36–38 distinguishes restrained speech, permitted ritual utterances, transgression, and repair: silence and authorized speech form one system, not opposites (E85).
- ŚB 3.2.1.23–24 stigmatizes opponents' speech as mleccha; this is evidence for elite boundary-making, not an objective linguistic ranking of peoples.

## Ṛgvedic divine core: relations before a fixed pantheon chart

| Figure / grouping | Textual anchor in JB14 | Coupling to speech, practice, or imagery | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Agni | RV 1.1 | Fire, divine priest, mediator of offerings; human performance addresses a priestly divine participant | Not merely an elemental “fire wizard” |
| Indra | RV 1.32; 2.12 | Vṛtra combat, released waters, victory, praise and Soma | Martial preeminence does not make every hymn a fixed monarchy of gods |
| Soma | RV 8.48; book 9 | Pressed substance, deity, praise, filtering/purification imagery | Botanical identification remains contested; do not give a confident modern drug recipe |
| Mitra–Varuṇa / Varuṇa | RV 5.63; 7.86 | Ordered relations, vigilance, obligation, fault and release | “Contract god” and “law god” are limited glosses, not exhaustive job descriptions |
| Uṣas | RV 1.113 | Dawn, recurring appearance, radiance, time passing | Poetic apparel imagery is not an excavated garment pattern |
| Aśvins | RV 1.116 | Divine pair, rescues, healing, movement | Pairing can organize names and performances without proving a universal twin archetype |
| Sūrya / Savitṛ | RV 1.50 / 3.62.10 | Solar vision / divine impulsion; distinguish the figures | Do not collapse every solar-associated name into one deity |
| Rudra / Viṣṇu | RV 2.33 / 1.154 | Dangerous and healing power / expansive strides | Later Śaiva and Vaiṣṇava theologies must be staged, not simply pasted backward |
| Sarasvatī / Vāc-associated speaker | RV 6.61 / 10.125 | River and divine power / powerful divine speech | Later identifications require a history; modern river geography is not settled by a name alone |

- Gods have overlapping powers and situational prominence; “henotheism” is an interpretive label for some praise strategies, not proof of a single successive religious stage.
- Asura is not uniformly equivalent to the later category “demon”; track the word's changing value rather than translating every occurrence through a later opposition (JB14).
- RV 10.90, 10.121, and 10.129 offer distinct cosmogonic materials; do not reconcile them into an allegedly original single creation story. Broad social order in 10.90 is not a census of actual caste practice.

## Symbolic system: contextual correspondences, not a symbol dictionary

- Use **bandhu-style correspondence** as a named analytical model: a ritual item, bodily condition, verbal feature, and cosmic relation can illuminate each other; the correspondence must retain its textual and ritual scope.
- ŚB 3.1.2.18 assigns parts of a consecration cloth to Agni, Vāyu, ancestors, snakes, All-gods, and asterisms. Its footnote records different assignments in the Black Yajurvedic witness: even a vivid correspondence is not universal.
- ŚB 3.2.1.3 explicitly allows the white and black portions of an antelope skin to exchange sky/earth correspondences. This directly defeats a universal “white always means heaven” lookup rule.
- ŚB 2.3.2.9–14 correlates changing fire states with Rudra, Varuṇa, Indra, Mitra, and brahman, and discusses consistent offering practice. One material process can support several divine relations (E82).
- ŚB 4.6.1.11–12 couples twelve months, twenty-four half-months, Prajāpati, cattle gifts, and sacrificial identity. These are ritual correspondences, not proof of identical astronomical durations.
- Symbolic etymologies in Brāhmaṇas explain ritual associations; they do not automatically establish historical word derivations. Keep indigenous explanation and comparative etymology in different columns.
- **Design:** represent a sign as `form → occasion → authorized interpretation → competing interpretation`; connect it to a particular school and era rather than a whole continent.

## Śrauta organization and the social body of performance

- Śrauta concerns ritual based on śruti and elaborated in school traditions; distinguish it from gṛhya domestic rites. Elaborate sponsorship is not a measure of everyone's daily religious life.
- A **yajamāna** is the sponsoring sacrificer, not simply the priest; the **patnī** has prescribed ritual participation. Do not erase her because many surviving technical voices are male.
- In developed Soma ritual, distinguish **hotṛ** recitation, **udgātṛ** Sāman singing, **adhvaryu** practical/formulaic work, and **brahman** supervisory functions; scale and staffing depend on rite (E85; K15).
- Metre, spoken formula, song, manipulation, and silence coordinate different offices. Give a fictional institution distinct competence tests rather than a generic class of interchangeable “chanting priests.”
- Fire layout and maintained relationships matter: gārhapatya, āhavanīya, and southern fire are differentiated, not interchangeable decorative braziers (E82, ŚB 2.1.1; 2.3.2).

## Fire-ritual dress: consecration creates a temporary person

- The following is **Soma-sacrifice dīkṣā evidence from the Mādhyandina Śatapatha**, not uniform dress for every fire rite or every Vedic person.
- Primary English witnesses: [ŚB 3.1.2](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/satapatha-brahmana-english/d/doc63182.html) and [ŚB 3.2.1](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/satapatha-brahmana-english/d/doc63185.html), E85.

| Material / act | Precise witness | Ritual coupling | Reconstruction limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hair/beard shaving, nail cutting, bathing | 3.1.2.1–12 | Purification precedes changed status; the text discusses alternatives | Not a universal Vedic hairstyle or ordinary bathing ethnography |
| Garment as replacement skin | 3.1.2.13–20 | Clothing restores bodily completeness; putting it on is verbally marked | Eggeling's “(linen)” is a parenthetical addition in the translation (inspected, ŚB 3.1.2.13); do not infer the fiber from this gloss alone |
| New or appropriately retained cloth | 3.1.2.19 | Freshness, cleansing, prior handling, and ritual fitness interact | Prescription does not establish the actual market supply or affordability |
| Black-antelope skins as consecration ground | 3.2.1.1–9 | Skin joins worlds; the consecrated becomes an embryo entering metres | These passages describe sitting surfaces: do not automatically draw them as a worn cloak |
| Hempen girdle with muñja | 3.2.1.10–14 | Strength, food, generation, inner membrane, and specific plaiting | This girdle is not automatically the later familiar sacred-thread arrangement |
| Head wrapping | 3.2.1.16–17 | Envelopment follows the embryo analogy | Upper cloth versus turban is discussed in commentary; a unique ancient cut is not established |
| Attached antelope horn | 3.2.1.18, 28–31 | Womb analogy; prescribed scratching implement | Attachment differs between Mādhyandina and Kāṇva witnesses noted by Eggeling |
| Udumbara staff | 3.2.1.32–35 | Protection, food, strength, bodily measure | Not generic equipment for all priests, students, or ascetics |

- The linked sequence is `material preparation → garment/formula → transformed status → constrained gesture/speech → completion`; the dress is procedural, not merely decorative.
- Textile production and women's labor enter 3.1.2.19 through a purity prescription. Report the source's exclusionary framing, not its assumptions as a neutral truth about workers.
- **Material signature:** woven cloth, plant-fiber cordage, animal skins/horn, wooden implements, cattle/dairy resources, and maintained fire can be described at bounded confidence. Exact dyes, drapes, loom configurations, and class-wide wardrobes need additional evidence `(to-verify)`.
- Do not back-project modern saffron ascetic uniforms, standardized white priestly outfits, a modern sari/dhoti tying convention, or later temple crowns merely because they look “Indian.” Their particular histories require separate sources.

## Śrauta calendar: nested obligations and negotiated timing

| Scale | Attested organizing practice | Coupling | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Day | Evening/morning Agnihotra and associated fire attendance: ŚB 2.3.2.4 | Household resources, trained attention, daily light cycle | Not a claim that every person maintained śrauta fires |
| Half-month | Darśapūrṇamāsa new/full-moon offerings: E82 book 1; 2.3.2.7 | Lunar phase, preparation/fasting, repeated sponsorship | Preparation day and offering day must not be collapsed |
| Seasonal clusters | Cāturmāsya discussions: ŚB 2.5–2.6 | Vaiśvadeva, Varuṇapraghāsa, Sākamedha relate ritual to seasonal recurrence | Four-month naming does not mean four equally spaced seasons |
| Supplement / closure | Śunāsīrīya: E82 introduction to 2.5.1 | Completion and alternative scheduling | Eggeling explicitly reports disagreement about its timing |
| Year | Twelve months / twenty-four half-months: ŚB 4.6.1.11–12 | Prajāpati, sacrificial identity, gifts | Schematic completeness is not an exact lunar–solar conversion algorithm |
| Star-linked inauguration | Alternative nakṣatras for establishing fires: ŚB 2.1.2 | Asterism, deity, desired outcome, wordplay | The same discussion ultimately permits solar/day-based priority over a chosen star |
| Commissioned extended rite | Soma rites and sessions | Patronal means, eligibility, personnel, duration | Not every elaborate rite belongs to a universally fixed annual festival date |

- Read [ŚB 2.5.1](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/satapatha-brahmana-english/d/doc63166.html) with its editorial introduction: the seasonal schedule is school-mediated, and the introduction is Eggeling's synthesis, not an ancient paragraph.
- ŚB 2.1.1.12–13 juxtaposes five-season and six-season reckonings; therefore even “the Vedic seasons” needs a textual locator, not an automatic universal six-season chart.
- [ŚB 2.1.2.1–19](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/book/satapatha-brahmana-english/d/doc63151.html) presents arguments both for and against particular inauguration stars. Ritual authorities reason and disagree; the calendar is not only a lookup table.
- A lunar month and solar year are not commensurate; intercalation is a design problem, not evidence that every Vedic school used one later standardized Hindu calendar. Select a recension before specifying the insertion rule.
- The *Vedāṅga Jyotiṣa* supplies a five-year yuga model — 62 lunar months, two of them intercalary, over 1,830 civil days — and survives in two recensions (Ṛgvedic and Yajurvedic), with composition dates argued from the later second millennium BCE to the last centuries BCE (P09, chapter 2; [overview and dating dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vedanga_Jyotisha)). The full equations and the differences between the recensions are not independently checked here `(to-verify)`.
- Do not confuse that short computational yuga with later cosmic world ages, or use an inferred stellar epoch to date the composition of every transmitted passage. Astronomical dating without textual-stratigraphic controls is **contested**.
- **Design:** record `observed event / computed rule / declaration authority / intercalation / weather exception / rite-specific obligation`; show who resolves a mismatch between lunar timing and seasonal resources.

## Naming practices: genealogy, attribution, and occasion

- Keep **personal name**, **patronymic/descent designation**, **poet-family attribution**, **ritual office**, and **divine epithet** distinct. A divine name in a hymn is not automatically a human naming template.
- Sudās and the Paijavana designation occur in RV 7.18; inspect JB14's translation/commentary before reducing the relationship to a modern surname. Vasiṣṭha material in RV 7.33 likewise belongs to a poet/genealogy tradition, not a civil registry.
- E85 ŚB 4.6.1.7–10 names Rāma Aupatasvini, Budila Āśvatarāśvi, and Yājñavalkya while reporting differing opinions: names can authorize competing technical judgments within one tradition.
- Do not identify this Rāma with an epic protagonist merely from the shared name, or derive a biography from a patronymic without further evidence.
- ŚB 2.1.2.11 discusses Indra's alternative name Arjuna in relation to the Phalgunīs/Arjunīs and guarded naming. This is a source-specific divine naming rule, not proof of a universal ban on speaking personal names.
- Gṛhya naming-rite prescriptions belong to another textual genre and vary by school: the Gobhila tradition gives the name at ten nights or later, prescribing for boys a name beginning with a sonant, containing a semivowel, ending in a long vowel or the visarga (with a kṛt suffix), and for girls an odd-syllable name ending in -dā, with offerings to the child's tithi and nakshatra (Gobhila Gṛhya Sūtra 2.8.8–17, in Oldenberg, SBE 30, [text](https://sacred-texts.com/hin/sbe30/sbe30020.htm)); Oldenberg's notes cross-reference the parallels (Śāṅkhāyana 1.24, Āśvalāyana 1.15, Pāraskara 1.17). Apply any such rule only with its school and passage; claims about separate “secret” names or nakshatra-name rules still need their own citations `(to-verify)`.
- **Design:** build separate slots for everyday address, descent claim, liturgical identifier, and earned office; specify who can pronounce each, in which register, and whether its older accent survives.

## Apply to a fictional culture: coupling drill, not a borrowed civilization

1. Choose a historical reference stratum and mark which mechanisms are invented; do not combine an early hymn, a later Sūtra, and modern dress as one eyewitness package.
2. Specify an oral register with an explicit inventory, quantity/accent system, and two checking recitations; give ordinary speech its own change history.
3. Define competing divine praise relations and speech-eligibility rules; keep attributed claims of efficacy distinct from the world's supernatural truth.
4. Construct one consecration sequence whose garment materials, handling, knot/plait, gesture restrictions, and accompanying formulas explain one another.
5. Nest daily, lunar, and seasonal obligations; specify correction authority and the consequence of cloth, fuel, milk, or trained performers being unavailable.
6. Generate names by role and register, then test ordinary address, ritual introduction, hostile mispronunciation, and a reform that changes only everyday pronunciation.
7. Hand lore a reference/invention claim ledger, era bounds, variants, and typed coupling edges. Lore chooses any fictional institutions, dates, deities, or costume rules; this entry establishes none.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- A culture can make authoritative sound a trained bodily craft, preserving old phonology without freezing its entire society.
- A garment can function as temporary cosmological anatomy: material, manufacture, speech, and wearer restrictions work together rather than as independent aesthetic choices.
- A pantheon can be organized through praise, transactions, paired relations, and ritual offices instead of one permanent chart of elemental portfolios.
- A calendar can be a contested institution coupling astronomy, seasonal ecology, labor, patronage, and expertise; disagreement is a design resource, not an error to erase.
- Take these mechanisms and their evidence boundaries, not a claim that a fictional society is “really Vedic,” nor permission to copy living sacred practice indiscriminately.

## Provenance cautions and thin coverage

- E82/E85 are colonial-era scholarly translations with valuable locators but dated terminology, editorial reconstructions, and outsider judgments. Distinguish ancient paragraphs, Sanskrit commentary, translator supplements, and modern website framing; compare Sanskrit and current scholarship for consequential wording.
- This pass is strongest in the inspected Mādhyandina consecration, star-selection, and seasonal passages; weakest in regional textile archaeology, women's lived experience, non-Brahmanical testimony, detailed calendar mathematics, and early śikṣā dating. Those gaps are not evidence of absence.
- Prescriptive texts disproportionately present learned elite ideals. Sparse chronicle evidence cannot justify an invented total wardrobe, demographic picture, or unanimous religious doctrine.
- Language-family evidence does not license colonial “Aryan race” categories; that racial equation is discredited. Migration and contact questions require linguistic, archaeological, and other evidence rather than either a racial invasion cartoon or an assumed timeless autochthony.
- Universal color keys, literal cosmic-frequency physics, and “all gods are secretly one pan-cultural archetype” are not established by the cited passages. Identify any such move as designer invention, not recovered indigenous science.
- No consultation with living practitioners is claimed. A public text or bibliography does not establish cultural permission; restrict imitation where sacred access or representation is at issue, and preserve regional/community attribution.
- Recheck against a named edition, school, section, and translation before using any `(to-verify)` detail; preserve disagreements rather than silently resolving them into lore canon.
