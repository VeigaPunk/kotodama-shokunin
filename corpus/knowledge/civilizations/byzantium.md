## 1. Scope and calibration

- “Byzantine” is a useful modern period label; the polity claimed Roman continuity, and **Rhōmaioi**, “Romans,” is a central self-designation. Greek speech did not cancel Roman political identity. [L; O]
- Do not equate this empire with a homogeneous Greek nation-state: language, confession, locality, descent, and imperial allegiance need separate fields. Armenian, Latin, Syriac, Slavic, and other contacts vary by place and century. [L; O]
- Use **attested** for an object, term, or statement actually witnessed; **interpretation** for its proposed social mechanism; **contested** for disputed chronology or explanation; **design heuristic** for the adaptation machinery below.
- A theological prescription is evidence for an argument and its authorized vocabulary, not automatic evidence of everyone's practice. A ceremonial manual describes an ordered court, not an unedited day in the street. [J; C; P]
- “Mythic core” below means sacred narratives and their social work, not a claim that believers regarded them as fiction. Orthodox Christianity remains a living tradition.

## 2. Era model — choose a court, region, and date before combining systems

| Working stage | Language and institutions | Symbol/material staging | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late Roman, fourth–seventh centuries | Greek and Latin occupy changing domains; Roman offices persist through reorganization | Christian imperial standards coexist with inherited Roman ceremonial forms | 330 is a convenient Constantinopolitan marker, not an instant cultural reset. [L; O] |
| Seventh–early ninth centuries | Territorial contraction, army reorganization, gradual thematic development | Religious images become a contested field; court and army signs do not all disappear | Neither one founding decree for Themes nor an empire-wide ban on every kind of image is a safe model. [A; I] |
| 843–eleventh century | Learned Greek, ecclesiastical usage, documentary registers, and speech coexist; mature thematic institutions | Restored icon veneration; tenth-century court compilation supplies detailed ceremonial evidence | Date individual chapters and objects; compilation can preserve older rites. [L; C; I] |
| Eleventh–twelfth centuries | Provincial and military structures change; aristocratic family identification becomes especially conspicuous | Court dress and donor portraits advertise office, kinship, and patronage | Do not freeze the tenth-century Theme map or rank ladder. [A; B; O] |
| 1204–1261 | Latin occupation of Constantinople; multiple Greek successor courts | Competing claims to Roman legitimacy and custody of sacred prestige | No single uninterrupted Constantinopolitan Orthodox court governs this interval. [O] |
| 1261–1453, restored Constantinopolitan empire | Reduced and changing political geography; learned and vernacular writing continue | Pseudo-Kodinos documents a later hierarchy and ceremonial wardrobe | Its hats, ranks, and ceremonies cannot be back-projected into Justinian's court. [L; P] |

- These are workflow bins, not native “ages” or a rise–peak–decay law; 1453 is not the end of Greek language or Orthodox cultural life.
- Treat political rupture, linguistic change, iconographic change, and textile change as **asynchronous clocks**. A renamed office can retain an old insignia; an old title can acquire a new job.

## 3. Language family, writing, and Medieval Greek diglossia

- **Family:** Greek is an independent branch of Indo-European; Medieval Greek continues earlier Greek through the Koine, with regional and social variation. It is not a Romance language because its speakers called themselves Romans. [L]
- **Writing:** Greek alphabet; literary manuscripts, inscriptions, seals, and practical documents differ in abbreviation and graphic conventions. Minuscule expands in book production from the ninth century; majuscule does not thereby vanish. [L; O]
- Historical spelling, accents, and breathings preserve distinctions no longer reliably audible. Script conservatism is not proof of unchanged pronunciation. Date a pronunciation model separately from its orthography. [L]
- **Atticizing** means selective imitation of prestigious ancient Attic models, especially in learned prose: acquired vocabulary, syntax, and rhetorical habits, not ordinary speakers spontaneously preserving classical Athens. [L]

| Functional register | Typical institutional work | Linguistic design lever | Wrong inference |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strongly Atticizing literary prose | Historiography, panegyric, educated display | Classical lexical choices, syntactic archaism, allusion | Every court order or sermon used this exact style. |
| Scriptural/liturgical and ecclesiastical Greek | Worship, exegesis, doctrinal argument | Inherited biblical wording and technical theological distinctions | Sacred language and Atticizing prose are identical. |
| Documentary/administrative range | Petitions, accounts, orders, notarial work | Formulae and office jargon mixed with varying degrees of learned usage | All writing belongs to one uniform “high” variety. |
| Vernacular-oriented speech and writing | Household and local interaction; selected literary genres | Continuing phonological and grammatical innovations | Vernacular means illogical, ungrammatical, or wholly unwritten. |

- **Design heuristic:** use Ferguson's (1959) H/L diglossia model [F] and `../linguistics/sociolinguistics-registers.md` as a first map, then replace two boxes with a register continuum indexed by genre, schooling, audience, and date. His modern Greek example is not medieval evidence; Horrocks [L] supplies the historical correction.
- Vernacular trajectories include replacement of the dative by other constructions and increasing use of finite complement constructions where older Greek used an infinitive; timing differs by construction and dialect. Do not apply a universal overnight grammar switch. [L]
- Do not call medieval learned Greek **katharevousa** without qualification: that label belongs to a much later language-political project. Continuities of prestige do not make the institutions identical. [L]
- Application: design the same request in household speech, a tax petition, and a coronation address. Specify who can translate between them, who pays for schooling, and which register mistakes expose an impostor.

## 4. Sacred narrative and Orthodox icon-theology vocabulary

- **Core:** Trinitarian Christianity, Incarnation, salvation, resurrection, saints, relics, and liturgical commemoration; not a pagan pantheon with Christian names substituted. The Trinity is not three independent gods; Mary and saints are not additional deities. [J; O]
- Constantinople's association with the Theotokos, protective sacred objects, and holy places couples civic identity to Christian sacred history. Claims of miraculous protection must remain attributed claims, not historical proof of supernatural intervention. [O; I]
- John of Damascus's defense links representability to **the Incarnation**: the invisible God is not simply drawn as invisible; Christ's becoming visible in the flesh grounds a particular argument for images. “All images are inherently divine” is not that argument. [J]

| Term | Working gloss | Coupling consequence / misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| **eikōn** | Image, likeness; in this setting an icon | Distinguish depicted person, material carrier, and authorized use; not every image is an idol. [J] |
| **prōtotypon** | Prototype/original to which an image refers | Honor directed through an image concerns its prototype; image and prototype are not materially identical. [J] |
| **proskynēsis** | Reverence, obeisance; meaning depends on recipient and context | Bowing can belong to religious veneration or courtly respect; gesture alone does not identify worship. [J; C] |
| **latreia** | Worship/service reserved to God in the iconophile distinction | Do not collapse it into every English use of “worship,” especially in older translations. [J] |
| **Theotokos** | “God-bearer,” title of Mary | A Christological claim about the one she bore, not evidence for a mother-goddess pantheon. [J; O] |
| **Logos** | “Word”; a title of Christ in Christian theology | Theological “Word” is not a native rule that any spoken syllable creates reality. [J; O] |
| **Pantokratōr** | Almighty / ruler of all; prominent title of Christ | Distinguish divine sovereignty from an emperor's claims to earthly rule; date the visual composition. [O] |

- Iconoclasm: distinguish the first imperial iconoclast period, conventionally c. 730–787, from the second, 815–843. Early measures, their dates, motives, and geographical implementation remain debated. [I]
- Nicaea II (787) and the restoration of 843 belong to the institutional history of icon veneration; they do not license an assumption of identical domestic practices everywhere. [I]
- **Calibration:** iconoclast positions survive heavily through hostile preservation and later iconophile narratives. Do not reduce the dispute to “art-loving Greeks versus art-hating outsiders,” or assume a simple Islamic cause. [I]
- Application: define what a representation may depict, how honor travels, who adjudicates misuse, and how that ruling changes processions, oath wording, workshops, and textile imagery. Adapt the relation, not a caricature of living worship.

## 5. Symbolic system and imperial regalia

| Sign/object | Attested referent or conventional interpretation | What to couple | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| **labarum** | Constantine's Christian imperial-military standard: Eusebius describes a gold-covered spear with crossbar, suspended cloth, and crowning wreath enclosing the Christogram | Military allegiance, imperial portraiture, sacred authorization | A panegyrical late-antique description, not one unchanging national flag for a millennium. [E, I.31; O] |
| **Chi-Rho** | Superimposed Greek chi and rho, the opening letters of *Christos* | Script → name/title → portable military or monumental sign | Letterform matters; it is not an arbitrary “rune of victory.” [O] |
| **akakia** | Imperial roll/pouch, conventionally described as containing earth or dust and reminding the ruler of mortality | Material humility held within conspicuous sovereignty | Identification in a portrait and claims about contents are different evidence; specific construction or contents for a given object are (to-verify). [O; B] |
| **loros** | Elaborate jewel-decorated ceremonial wrapping/strip worn in imperial representation and ritual | Rank, restricted access, bodily movement, ceremonial time | Do not confuse it with a military sash or make every era's wrapping identical. [B; C] |
| Purple and precious textile | Material and chromatic claims to high status and imperial prestige | Dye supply, technical skill, permitted wear, diplomatic gifts | Different hues, materials, and restrictions need dated evidence; “all purple was always forbidden” is false precision. [B; O] |
| Double-headed eagle | A later imperial/dynastic visual association, especially relevant to late Byzantine comparison | Dynasty, household affiliation, court object versus public emblem | Not an empire-wide flag attested unchanged from Constantine; check object, wearer, and date. [O; P] |

- A useful **semiotic tuple** is `{carrier, authorized user, occasion, audience, claimed authority, sanction}`. A cross on a standard, an icon, and a seal shares a shape without having the same institutional function.
- Neither regalia nor a pious title makes the emperor a Christian deity. “Caesaropapism” is a misleading shortcut when it erases bargaining and conflict between emperors, bishops, councils, and monasteries. [I; O]
- Application: give a ruler a paired display of magnificence and mortality; specify how each object is named in learned speech, workshop jargon, and popular mockery. This is a design option, not a proposed fictional dynasty.

## 6. Costume and material signature — separate worn, depicted, and prescribed

| Evidence/item | Useful historical anchor | Design use | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chlamys**, often with **tablion** | Mantle and contrasting panel in elite/official representation | Make appointment and access visible without inventing a full uniform code | Panel color, shape, and rank equivalence are date-sensitive. [B; O] |
| **Loros** and long imperial tunics | Formal imperial imagery and ceremonial ensembles | Restrict garments by occasion as well as wearer | A portrait is not proof the ruler wore it daily; exact cut reconstruction is (to-verify). [B; C] |
| Silks, embroidery, metalwork, gems | Concentrated skilled labor and costly display at elite levels | Tie ceremonial vocabulary to workshops, trade routes, gifts, and maintenance | “Byzantine” is not a synonym for every jeweled garment or every compound weave. [B; O] |
| Wool and linen; practical layered clothing | Less spectacular material foundations of clothing | Make region, season, work, and means change a wardrobe | Do not extrapolate ordinary households from imperial mosaics. [B; O] |
| Pseudo-Kodinos's ranked court dress | Later offices differentiated through ceremonial garments and headgear | Let a later regime redesign visible rank while retaining old titles | It describes a late court; individual prescriptions still need contextual reading. [P] |

- Run the **three-evidence comparison**: object or textile fragment ↔ dated depiction ↔ textual prescription. Agreement strengthens an identification; disagreement is a research question, not permission to merge all three. See `material-culture-inference-method.md`.
- Record garment term, proposed referent, date, region, wear occasion, and evidence medium separately. Translations such as “robe” or “tunic” hide distinctions; Greek-looking spelling does not authenticate an invented cut.
- Thin coverage: this entry does not establish ordinary women's wardrobes across provinces or century-by-century fiber proportions. Specific silhouettes, color rules, and household ownership rates need additional evidence (to-verify).

## 7. Theme administration — an institution with semantic history

- **Thēma** (plural **themata**) changes from an army/command designation toward a territorial military-administrative district. District names can preserve organizational history rather than an ethnicity or natural region. [A; O]
- **Contested:** the older picture of Heraclius creating a complete system in one reform, with uniform grants to hereditary soldier-farmers, should not be taught as settled fact. Haldon's reconstruction emphasizes longer reorganization and continuities. [A; I]
- Separate **army location**, **command territory**, **fiscal machinery**, **military obligations**, and **land tenure**; they need not emerge together. “Theme” is not a synonym for a feudal fief. [A]

| Term/institution | Working function | Coupling lever |
|---|---|---|
| **stratēgos** | General; in thematic contexts the appointed commander with developing provincial responsibilities | An old military word acquires jurisdictional weight; title longevity can mask job change. [A; O] |
| **tourma / tourmarchēs** | Military subdivision / its commander in thematic organization | Local military vocabulary creates intermediate identities; do not assume identical subdivision sizes. [A; O] |
| **tagmata** | In the middle Byzantine setting, centrally maintained regiments contrasted with regional thematic forces | Build overlapping loyalties and recruitment channels, not “the army” as one social block. [A] |
| Fiscal and judicial officials | Revenue and adjudication remain necessary, with changing relationships to command | Let a general, clerk, and judge describe the same district differently. [A; O] |
| Seals and titulature | Offices, dignities, names, and Christian formulas can authenticate administrative action | Link bureaucracy to writing and sacred invocation without treating every seal as a magical token. [O] |

- Ninth-/tenth-century thematic organization, later military commands, and late uses of *thema* are not interchangeable. Keeping an administrative word after restructuring is an excellent model of lexical inertia. [A; O]
- Application: build separate maps for levy/recruitment, taxes, episcopal jurisdiction, dialect, and textile supply. Explain their mismatches before making costume mark every border perfectly.
- Do not infer that an official's court dignity, salaried post, family name, regional command, and hereditary property are one transferable package. [A; O]

## 8. Naming practices — identities are layered, not a fixed modern form

- Personal names include Christian/biblical and inherited Greek or Roman stock: **Iōannēs**, **Michaēl**, **Kōnstantinos**, **Eirēnē**. Do not make every name a transparent contemporary noun or a secret profession. [L; O]
- Family names become conspicuous among middle-/late-Byzantine elites, but a complete hereditary surname system is not safely projected onto every earlier or non-elite person. [O]
- **Alexios Komnēnos / Anna Komnēnē** and **Michaēl Doukas / Eirēnē Doukaina** illustrate historically attested gendered family-name forms; these are examples, not a mechanical rule for every surname. [O]
- Distinguish family name, patronymic, place-based designation, office, monastic name, and epithet. **Porphyrogennētos**, “born in the purple,” stakes a birth-status claim, not a universal surname. [O]
- **Basileus**, “emperor” in the relevant imperial setting, is an office/title, not a personal name; *Rhōmaiōn*, “of the Romans,” in titulature claims political community. Exact formulas must be dated. [O; C]
- Choose a transliteration policy: *Ioannes* versus *Iōannēs*, *Constantine* versus *Kōnstantinos*. A reader-facing translation is not a separate native language, and scholarly long-vowel marks are not a complete medieval pronunciation guide. [L]
- Application: make a naming record with `{birth name, kin affiliation, office, ritual name, public epithet, documentary form, familiar form}`. Leave fields empty where the social institution does not supply them.

## 9. Greek fire secrecy case — technology, sacred origin, and the capital

| Evidence layer | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Narrative reports of naval incendiary use | A real Byzantine military capability; importance of delivery apparatus and trained operators | One invariant substance behind every medieval “Greek fire” reference. [R] |
| Theophanes's attribution to **Kallinikos** of Heliopolis | An invention/refugee-artificer narrative about late-seventh-century development | Contemporary eyewitness certification of an inventor, exact year, or personal background. [R] |
| Constantine VII, **DAI 13** | An instruction to deny outsiders the guarded fire; an origin story in which an angel reveals it to Constantine the first Christian emperor | A literal technical history proving fourth-century invention. [D; R] |
| DAI's punishment story | Sacred sanctions make leakage both political betrayal and impiety | An independently documented prosecution or proof that leakage was impossible. [D] |
| Modern reconstructions | Plausibility of particular materials and mechanisms | Recovery of a uniquely authenticated original formula. [R] |

- Keep the two Constantines distinct: the **tenth-century authorial court** deploys an origin story about **Constantine I**. The distance between them is part of the legitimating mechanism, not a chronology to flatten.
- Chapter 13 ties authorized production to Christians and the imperial city. That is evidence for a stated monopoly and diplomatic refusal script; actual workshop organization and enforcement are separate questions. [D]
- Greek terms translated “liquid fire” or “sea fire” should be recorded with their source context. The modern umbrella label is not proof that all authors meant the same device or mixture. [O; R]
- **Systems lens:** capability = material supply + apparatus + platform + trained crew + maintenance + command practice. Capturing a component need not transfer a functioning capability. This is a more useful model than “one recipe equals empire.” [R]
- Compartmentalization is a useful explanatory model, not a fully recovered organization chart. Exact divisions of knowledge, workshop succession, and oath wording remain (to-verify); do not invent an unbroken secret family or formal guild.
- Composition and mechanism remain disputed. A plausible petroleum-based reconstruction is not an authenticated recipe; no formula, proportion, or fabrication procedure is needed for this cultural reference. [R]
- Do not attribute every naval victory or the empire's longevity to this weapon, or confidently narrate precisely how its knowledge was finally “lost.” The record does not justify that tidy story. [R]
- Application: design a protected craft whose religious origin story, restricted technical register, capital workshop, and diplomatic taboo reinforce each other. Test what happens when materials, operators, and political legitimacy separate.

## 10. Coupling matrix — transform relationships, not a surface aesthetic

| Start with | Change this system | Then check | Failure signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| Learned/vernacular distance | Schooling and eligibility for office | Petition formulas, education costs, family advancement, public speech | Everyone speaks one archaic register while literacy supposedly matters. |
| Icon doctrine | Who or what may be represented | Paintings, textile motifs, procession scripts, accusations, adjudicators | “No images” in dialogue, unrestricted figurative rites everywhere else. |
| Imperial regalia | Permitted carriers and occasions | Workshop terminology, custody, rank recognition, damaged-object handling | Crown, standard, and akakia become interchangeable magic props. |
| Administrative reorganization | Command and fiscal boundaries | Office semantics, seals, local naming, insignia, dialect geography | A new district instantly creates a new language and hereditary nobility. |
| Technical monopoly | Access to materials and tacit knowledge | Sacred sanctions, specialist speech, maintenance, diplomatic refusals | Stealing one vial transfers the entire institution. |
| Dynastic rupture | Legitimacy and court membership | Titulature, family-name display, ceremonial dress, inherited ritual | A millennium of unchanged Byzantine costume stands in for history. |

- These are **design tests**, not claims that a particular Byzantine reform caused every listed effect. Make evidence-based analogies explicit before handing options to lore-shokunin.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take

1. A **register ecology**, not “two languages”: keep literary prestige, liturgy, bureaucracy, and household practice distinguishable, with named brokers between them.
2. A **theory of representation** that changes material practice: define image, recipient, permitted gesture, and competent authority before decorating garments with sacred signs.
3. A **paired insignia logic**: sovereign magnificence can coexist with an object reminding its bearer of mortality; the tension is more productive than generic gold ornament.
4. A **moving administrative lexicon**: preserve old words across reorganizations, but explicitly update their referents, appointment rules, and visual markers.
5. A **secret-capability ecology**: technical language, supply chains, trained bodies, sacred origin claims, and state secrecy can form one institution without requiring magical causation.
6. A **dated comparison packet**: supply source, century, region, register, object medium, institutional role, uncertainty, and several possible adaptations. Lore-shokunin selects canon; this entry selects none.

## 12. Provenance cautions and thin areas

- Edition metadata is checkable through the cited DOI/publisher records; catalogue verification is not a claim to have collated every manuscript or inspected every book page. The open John of Damascus text was spot-checked against the First Apology's argument; modern Louth is the preferred translation reference.
- [D] chapter 13 is the primary locator for the fire story; an openly accessible quotation cross-check is [“Greek fire”](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_fire), which points to Moravcsik/Jenkins and Roland. This discovery aid is not a critical edition or independent corroboration of the miracle.
- Akakia's standard description is indexed in [O]; the accessible [akakia overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akakia) points to its p. 42. That pointer does not replace checking a particular object or proving its contents.
- Imperial manuals and ethnographic descriptions are center-biased sources about ruled peoples and neighbors; their categories and claims of authority are not neutral descriptions of everyone's identity.
- Iconophile victory shaped the surviving controversy record. John of Damascus also uses supersessionist, anti-Jewish polemic: treat it as situated rhetoric, not valid comparative knowledge about Judaism. [J; I]
- Depictions idealize, identify, and commemorate; prescriptions authorize; excavated objects survive unevenly. None alone yields a census of actual dress, belief, or speech. [B; C; P]
- This entry is thinnest on rural spoken registers, ordinary women's provincial dress, non-Greek communities' own accounts, and technical workshop routines. Add local documentary/archaeological work before designing those specifics (to-verify).
- Avoid “decadent Byzantines,” an unchanging mystical East, universal emperor–priest control, and single-cause invention legends. These are stereotypes or overcompressed frameworks, not explanations; preserve disagreement and missing evidence in the lore handoff.
