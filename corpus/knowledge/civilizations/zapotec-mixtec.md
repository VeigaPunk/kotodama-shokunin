## Scope corrections and calibration
- **Established:** Zapotec and Mixtec are not language isolates. They belong to different branches within Oto-Manguean; “Mesoamerican” identifies an interaction area, not a single language family. [C97]
- **Established:** “Zapotec” and “Mixtec” each cover substantial linguistic and regional diversity. Neither label authorizes one timeless pronunciation, pantheon, national costume, or centralized kingdom.
- Zapotecan includes Zapotec and Chatino; Mixtecan includes Mixtec, Cuicatec, and Triqui. Do not confuse Mixtec with Mixe, which belongs to Mixe-Zoquean. [C97]
- **Strong but contextual:** calendrical names, dynastic descent, sacred places, and dress work together in elite records. They do not describe every person's daily experience. [U05; P94; JP11]
- **Interpretive:** “animated places” is useful for sacred geography and agency; it does not establish a universal grammatical rule that all place nouns are animate.
- **Thin here:** local oral traditions, commoner wardrobes, early Mixtec spoken registers, and exact preconquest pronunciations. Do not fill these gaps from a generic “Aztec” reference sheet.

## Language family and writing: choose a local system
| System | Reference anchor | Coupling consequence | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zapotec languages | Zapotecan, Oto-Manguean [C97] | Build related local languages, not independent exotic word lists | Modern varieties cannot automatically voice a Classic inscription |
| Mixtec languages | Mixtecan, Oto-Manguean [C97] | Let local forms, intermarriage, and scribal traditions interact | Do not treat one modern orthography as ancient pan-Mixtec spelling |
| Ancient Zapotec script | Early evidence around 600 BCE; Urcid argues for a logosyllabic system [U05] | Names and calendrics can legitimate corporate descent through monuments and tombs | Partial decipherment: a translated narrative is not a complete phonetic reading |
| Mixtec pictorial histories | Screenfold books combine people, dates, acts, genealogies, and place signs [PW; JP11] | A visual record can support recitation and multiple layers of historical memory | Not “primitive pictures”; not an alphabet in disguised form |
| Colonial alphabetic writing | Indigenous-language legal, religious, and community documents [T01; L17] | Add new names, offices, spellings, and genres to older claims | Alphabetization is transformation, not immediate replacement of every pictorial practice |

- Separate **genealogy of languages**, **spread of a graphic convention**, and **political allegiance**; one does not prove the other.
- Preserve tone and other contrasts when using a particular living language; exact inventories and notation are variety-specific. Historical sound values not established here are `(to-verify)`.
- Colonial Zapotec spellings vary substantially; Ticha documents alternate consonants, doubled letters, and vowel spellings. Do not turn every spelling variant into a separate fictional ethnic group. [L17]
- Avoid both extremes: “pictures readable identically in any language” and “every graphic detail corresponds to a spoken syllable.” Identify what the particular reading actually demonstrates. [U05; JP11]
- Useful design mechanism: a shared court iconography can cross language boundaries while oral performance, local toponyms, and diplomatic address remain language-specific.

## Personal naming and the coqui/cihua title audit
| Item | Attestation and meaning | Safe use | Unsafe substitution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number + day sign | Mixtec elite calendrical names; a 260-day cycle combines 13 coefficients and 20 signs [PW] | Track calendrical identity separately from epithets and office | Treat “Eight Deer” as a surname or proof of a deer clan |
| Epithet / additional identifier | “Jaguar Claw” in the conventional English name of Lord 8 Deer [W13] | Distinguish translated identifier from reconstructed spoken form | Assume an English gloss supplies original pronunciation |
| **coqui** | Colonial Valley Zapotec: “noble; lord, lady”; variants include Cooqui and coguj [L17, s.v. coqui] | A source-specific noble designation, potentially translated differently by context | Assert a universal male-only “king” title |
| **chana** | Colonial Valley Zapotec: “Sir; Lady; Lord” [L17, s.v. chana] | A reminder that address and noble status need not have one-to-one English equivalents | Import an English aristocratic rank ladder |
| **yya / iya** | Mixtec lord/divine designation; Pohl discusses rulers' divine ancestry [PW, gods and titles] | Couple rulership, ancestor claims, and ritual authority | Treat as Zapotec coqui with interchangeable pronunciation |
| **cihua / cihuā- / cihuātl** | Nahuatl, not an established Zapotec–Mixtec royal counterpart: “woman”; possessed, “wife” [K92] | Audit whether a text is Nahuatl, a translation, or evidence of contact | Manufacture a coqui/cihua = king/queen binary |

- The brief's “coqui/cihua titles” must therefore be retained as a **source-audit problem**, not accepted as a matched native title pair.
- A claimed independent local title spelled **cihua** remains `(to-verify)` until its language, community, date, and source passage are supplied; a resemblance to Nahuatl is not proof of borrowing either.
- Keep fields separate: `calendar-name | epithet | local-title | polity | kin-links | language | document-date | event-date`.
- Calendrical names recur: identify people through parents, spouses, places, and dated events rather than the numeral-sign combination alone. [PW; JP11]
- Women appear as dynastic actors, not merely costume variants of men; Lady 6 Monkey's history is an essential counterweight to an exclusively male ruler template. [PW]
- Do not publish invented Mixtec or Zapotec name forms as translations. For fiction, derive new forms within the fictional language and label the comparative mechanism.

## Ñuu / ñuù and places that act
- **Ñuu**, commonly translated “town,” is one of the geographical bases in Mixtec place signs. The brief's **ñuù** notation is not assumed to be a universal tone spelling. [S73; PW, places]
- Pohl's analytical model is **geographical substantive + qualifier(s)**: a limited set of landscape bases combines with colors, beings, plants, weapons, numbers, and other identifiers.
- His guide lists **yucu** “hill,” **yuta** “river,” **ñuu** “town,” and **yodzo** “plain.” These are cited forms, not a certified modern pronunciation guide. [PW]
- **Ñuhu** is discussed in scholarship on earth/sacred beings; exact historical lexical senses, tone contrasts, and its relation to a particular town-name spelling are `(to-verify)` here. Do not equate ñuu and ñuhu because their untoned romanizations resemble one another. [M95; JP11]
- “Animated” should mean that a locality can be an origin, interlocutor, ritual destination, or source of authority within a particular account—not that every mountain has the same personality.

| Place-reading layer | Example / evidence | Transferable question |
|---|---|---|
| Landscape base | Hill, river, town, plain signs [PW] | What classes of places are easy to name and depict? |
| Distinguishing qualifier | Objects, colors, beings, numerals [S73; PW] | Which distinctions are politically or ritually memorable? |
| Local and external names | Tilantongo is the Nahuatl-derived name; Pohl gives **Ñuu Tnoo–Huahi Andehui**, “Black Town–Temple of Heaven” [PW] | Do local speech, diplomatic records, and a later empire name the place differently? |
| Graphic composition | Black frieze plus temple with a star-marked roof in the Tilantongo sign [PW] | Which visible features encode locality rather than decorative atmosphere? |
| Founding and sacred agency | Ancestral origins, sacred trees, and the ordering of places in Vindobonensis [PW; JP11] | What obligations make a settlement more than occupied ground? |
| Political reuse | A place joins a marriage, conquest, or descent narrative [PW] | Does the event alter who may claim the place without changing its name? |

- Keep three readings distinct: **literal name components**, **identified archaeological location**, and **ritual interpretation**. Confidence in one is not confidence in all three.
- A sky or earth motif can both qualify a place and connect it with cosmology; do not force an exclusive choice between “map” and “myth.”
- Fictional application: define the place-name grammar first; then separately decide whether a charter personifies places, narrates their founding, or addresses their nonhuman custodians.
- Never infer a universal “animist noun class” from sacred-landscape interpretation; verify grammatical agreement and pronoun use in the chosen language independently.

## Mythic and pantheon core: complexes, not a universal roster
| Evidence complex | What the sources support | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Zapotec rain/lightning | Cocijo, rendered **Cociyo** by Urcid; masks, rainmaking imagery, and ritual impersonation [U05] | Strong iconographic complex; not every decorated vessel is simply a portrait of a god |
| Zapotec maize and ancestral continuity | Maize imagery, mortuary programs, named ancestors, and inherited offices combine [U05] | Contextual reading; deity, office-holder, and ancestor may overlap in representation |
| Mixtec rain/place identity | Terraciano renders **Tay Ñudzahui** “people of the rain place” [T01] | Establishes a meaningful self-designation, not a complete theology of rain |
| Mixtec origins | Vindobonensis includes Nine Wind, primordial acts, and ancestors emerging from sacred trees [PW; JP11] | A particular manuscript program, not one compulsory creation story for all communities |
| Divine dynastic ancestry | Pohl connects yya with lordship/divinity and discusses Seven Earthquake and Nine Reed [PW] | Distinguish calendrical names of sacred personages from generic job labels |
| Earth–rain relations | Exchange, sacrifice, and obligations in Monaghan's ethnography [M95] | Modern comparison; historical continuity must be argued, not presumed |

- A full entry on **Dzahui/Dzaui** as rain deity needs the selected community, orthography, and source passage; detailed attributes or equivalences not established here are `(to-verify)`.
- Do not collapse Cocijo, Dzahui, and the Nahua Tlaloc into one deity with three translations. Comparison of rain/lightning complexes does not establish identical myths or institutions.
- **Contested scholarship:** Urcid explicitly disputes readings by Marcus and collaborators that deny a Zapotec pantheon; divine ontology, ancestral authority, and the translation of colonial religious categories are interpretive issues. [U05, discussion of early colonial social organization]
- Record which model a reconstruction uses: named deities; ancestral embodiments; ritual offices; or a combination. Do not silently pass between them as settled facts.
- Colonial accusations of “idolatry” can preserve information about bundles and priests while distorting the terms and purpose of the practices. Pohl's 1544 trial example must be read through that filter. [PW, gods and titles]
- Craft leverage: a ruler's genealogy, sacred place, ritual garment, and inherited office can be different ways of asserting one claim—not four unrelated lore lists.

## Symbolic system: read relationships before isolated motifs
| Sign / visual convention | Operational reading | Wrong shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Numeral dots + day sign | Name or date, distinguished by narrative context [PW] | A number always encodes mystic rank |
| Year marker + coefficient | Year within a recurring cycle; absolute placement needs correlation [PW, chronology] | One sign gives a unique Gregorian year |
| Red guide lines | Help sequence Nuttall's narrative; follow the actual page [PW; W13] | Every codex obeys one universal left-to-right path |
| Facing pair and seat/throne | Can mark marriage and status in genealogical scenes [PW, reading] | Every seated woman is a daughter or attendant |
| Jaguar helmet / jaguar seat | Dress and authority are contextual; Eight Deer is associated with a jaguar helmet [PW, dress] | Every jaguar means the same deity, clan, or warrior order |
| Mask and headdress ensemble | May constitute ritual personification [U05] | A deity mask proves the pictured person is nonhuman |
| Sacred tree / origin place | Can anchor ancestry and legitimacy [PW; JP11] | A decorative “tree of life” interchangeable across civilizations |

- Pohl describes connections between codices, recitation, performance, feasting, costume, and decorated vessels. Use this as a reconstruction framework, not direct observation of every historical performance. [P94; PW]
- Build a motif ledger with `depicted-form | actor | action | place | medium | date | proposed-reading | alternatives`.
- Reject a universal color dictionary: a black element in a named place-sign can encode that place's name, not a general moral judgment about darkness.

## Era model: stage evidence, not ethnic replacement
These are broad working bands, not substitutes for local ceramic phases or community histories. [FM83; U05; T01]

| Working horizon | Evidence emphasis | Language–myth–dress consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Late Formative, including early writing around 600 BCE | San José Mogote and the development of the Valley writing tradition [U05] | Do not back-project a surviving Late Postclassic manuscript outfit into this horizon |
| Classic, broadly first millennium CE | Monte Albán and other centers; tombs, monuments, effigy vessels, corporate genealogies [U05] | Tie dress and masks to specific mortuary or architectural programs |
| Reorganization, approximately 800–1000 CE | Decline/reworking of central institutions and changing graphic conventions [U05] | Political and scribal changes need not mean that one population vanished |
| Postclassic, approximately 1000–early 1500s | Interlinked kingdoms, dynastic histories, ritual authority, warfare, marriage [PW; JP11] | Distinguish event dates from the later production dates of surviving codices |
| Colonial, from the sixteenth century | Conquest, evangelization, litigation, alphabetic documents, continuing indigenous agency [T01; L17] | Offices, names, genres, and wardrobes transform unevenly; avoid a single instant “conversion” |
| Modern and contemporary | Living languages, community knowledge, changing textile traditions | Consult living speakers and makers; contemporary dress is not an unchanged archaeological survival |

- Do not narrate “Zapotec civilization ends; Mixtec civilization begins.” The sources require interacting regions, continuing communities, and multiple political centers.
- Keep `event-period`, `artifact-production`, `copy/publication-date`, and `modern-interpretation-date` as separate fields.
- When choosing a fictional era analogue, choose its political scale, medium of record, textile evidence, and degree of language contact together—not a European period label plus an indigenous silhouette.

## Costume and material signature: what codices can actually prove
| Evidence | Positive inference | Limit / check |
|---|---|---|
| Nuttall's painted deerskin screenfold [W13; UC Davis guide] | An elite visual archive preserves ensembles, poses, accessories, and repeated associations | Pictures are not surviving garments or pattern-cutting diagrams |
| Eight Deer's jaguar helmet [PW, dress] | An identifying costume element can couple personal biography and public role | Do not make every inhabitant a jaguar warrior |
| Priests' black-and-white tunics in Pohl's analysis [PW, dress] | Dress can connect participants across scenes and distinguish ritual roles | Do not turn this into a universal priestly uniform without checking the corpus |
| Woman in Tomb 5, Cerro de la Campana [U05, interior vestibule] | Blouse, long skirt, Cociyo headdress, ear ornaments, necklace, incense pouch, and speech scroll combine in one high-status portrayal | One representation is not an inventory of all women's dress |
| Polychrome vessels and gifts of gold, shell, bone, turquoise [PW, reading] | Portable material culture can circulate ancestry, alliance, and ritual imagery | A pictured object does not establish its material, workshop, or availability to commoners |
| Throne and seating context, Nuttall 42–43 [PW, reading] | Status furniture helps identify a spouse and genealogical relationship | Clothing alone may not resolve who the person is |

- Read costume as an **ensemble attached to an event**: body garment + headdress + ornaments + implements + posture + setting + named participant.
- Separate four hypotheses: **rank display**, **ritual impersonation**, **personal identifier**, and **narrative convention**. More than one may apply; none should be assumed automatically.
- Log what is visible before naming materials: outline, fastening position, decoration, color, body coverage, and carried objects.
- Fiber, dye, weave, sewing, weight, and seasonal practicality need independent textile/material evidence; these are not established by a flat painted surface.
- Do not use modern festival dress or the contemporary Tehuana ensemble as a direct costume reconstruction for Classic Monte Albán or Lord 8 Deer.
- Reproductions matter: Nuttall's 1902 publication used commissioned copies. Check original-object photographs before relying on tiny motifs or exact colors. [N02; UC Davis guide]
- Responsible reconstruction label: “ensemble modeled on a named scene,” not “what all Mixtecs wore.”

## Lord 8 Deer Jaguar Claw: a coupling case study
- **Historical anchor:** the conventional correlation places his life in **1063–1115 CE**. Pohl explains the correlation through colonial-era synchronisms, genealogical order, and recurring 52-year cycles—not an isolated numeral's self-evident date. [PW, chronology]
- **Identity:** “8 Deer” is calendrical; “Jaguar Claw” is an additional conventional translated identifier; “Lord” is an English rendering, not the original name's first syllable. [W13; PW]
- **Place:** link the ruler to Tilantongo / Ñuu Tnoo rather than to an undifferentiated “Mixtec empire.” The place sign's black town and celestial temple join political geography to sacred authority. [PW, places]
- **Myth–politics:** encounters with oracles, ancestral connections, and ritual recognition are part of the political narrative, not detachable supernatural side quests. [PW, narrative comparison]
- **Costume:** his associated jaguar helmet makes the protagonist recognizable; other attributes must be read scene by scene rather than assembled into a permanent maximal outfit. [PW, dress]
- **Kinship:** marriages and descent connect kingdoms while warfare can divide those same families; the genealogical graph is also a map of conflicts. [PW; JP11]

| Manuscript comparison | What to inspect | Why it changes the method |
|---|---|---|
| Nuttall reverse, pp. 42–84 | Parentage, ascent, campaigns, and violence; the beginning is explained through pp. 42–43 [PW; W13] | A protagonist-centered account organizes political memory selectively |
| Selden, pp. 6–8 | Lady 6 Monkey and the Jaltepec dynasty [PW] | A rival dynasty's interests can change emphasis and omission |
| Bodley, including p. 14 | Pohl locates Eight Deer's death here; subsequent genealogies continue the history [PW] | Do not claim that Nuttall itself supplies every event in the reconstructed biography |
| Colombino–Becker | Related accounts of Eight Deer and Four Wind [PW] | Test a reconstruction across manuscripts rather than treating one as a complete transcript |

- **Chronological trap:** a later manuscript's portrayal of an eleventh-century person is evidence for remembered history and representational conventions; it is not automatically an eyewitness wardrobe record.
- **Political trap:** a spectacular career does not justify inventing a permanently unified national monarchy or an unbroken dynasty shared by every Mixtec-speaking community.
- **Transferable method:** construct `person × date × polity × kin-link × ritual-authorizer × ensemble × manuscript-viewpoint`; disagreements remain visible in the matrix.
- Fictional use stays conditional: one could design competing court archives whose shared signs identify the same actors while their genealogies and ceremonial dress frame incompatible legitimacy claims.

## What a worldbuilder can take
1. **Related languages without uniform culture:** choose a local linguistic model, then specify contact and divergence independently from political allegiance.
2. **Names as layered identifiers:** calendar-name, epithet, title, polity, and kinship each answer a different question; allow recurrence without making characters indistinguishable.
3. **Places as institutions and participants:** combine a landscape base with qualifiers, then define the stories and obligations that make the place authoritative; do not infer grammar from ontology.
4. **Costume as an argument:** make an ensemble indicate what its wearer claims to embody or perform in that scene, not simply which “culture skin” the character wears.
5. **An era-dependent archive:** monuments, tombs, screenfold histories, and alphabetic petitions give different forms to memory; changes in medium can alter who can contest a claim.
6. **Competing histories:** test a hero's biography against a rival lineage's account; retain omissions and alternative readings rather than forcing one omniscient chronicle.
7. **Handoff, not canon:** deliver candidate mechanisms, cited comparanda, era assumptions, costume evidence, uncertainties, and incompatible options to lore-shokunin; this entry authorizes no fictional historical fact.

## Provenance cautions
- **Colonial bias:** missionaries and courts classified practices through Christian and legal categories; indigenous alphabetic testimony also reflects negotiation, not transparent access to an untouched past. [T01; L17]
- **Elite bias:** genealogical books and monumental programs preferentially document rulers, ritual specialists, and claims to descent; everyday speech and clothing are much less securely represented. [U05; JP11]
- **Survival and reproduction bias:** loss, selection, copying, displaced manuscripts, and modern editions shape the archive. Catalogue date, event date, and codex date are not interchangeable. [N02; W13]
- **Interpretation versus decipherment:** named persons and narrative sequences can be persuasive without yielding every word once spoken at a performance; do not invent phonetic certainty. [U05; JP11]
- **Chronicle scarcity:** no continuous neutral chronicle resolves every kingdom's sequence, costume rule, or myth. This entry is especially thin on commoners, early spoken registers, and local variants of earth-being vocabulary.
- **No universal pantheon, animism, or costume code:** the deity/ancestor debate, lexical ambiguity, and regional diversity require local evidence; generic pan-Mesoamerican compilations cannot settle them.
- **Unresolved here:** ñuhu lexical history and tone; any independent local cihua title; a full sourced Dzahui attribute catalogue; ancient garment construction. These remain `(to-verify)`, not invitations to silently supply attractive guesses.
- **Living reference cultures:** work by Indigenous speakers and contemporary communities is scholarship and present agency, not decorative confirmation of an imagined timeless past. Borrow structural questions; avoid presenting copied sacred names, regalia, or practices as freely interchangeable fantasy stock.
