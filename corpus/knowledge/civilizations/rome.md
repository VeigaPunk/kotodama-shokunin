## 1. Frame the reference before borrowing
- **Working unit:** place × date × legal status × institution × medium; neither “Roman” nor “Latin” is a complete specification.
- The strongest coupling is **publicly legible standing**: a name establishes affiliation, a garment claims eligibility, a title supplies authority, and a dated inscription preserves that claim.
- Reference emphasis: late Republic and early Principate; archaic Rome and late antiquity require different evidentiary controls.
- **Calibration:** broad linguistic affiliations and many named offices are secure; exact garment cuts, regional observance, and the distribution of spoken features are often contextual or disputed.
- Early kings, foundation dates, and explanations attributing institutions to single founders come largely through much later literary traditions; they are not contemporary chronicles.
- “Romanization” as one-way progress from inferior local culture to superior Roman culture is an obsolete explanatory default; investigate coercion, selective adoption, bilingualism, and local initiative instead.

## 2. Era staging — do not assemble a timeless Roman kit
| Stage | Useful reference horizon | Coupled changes | Do not back-project |
|---|---|---|---|
| Archaic/regal Rome, conventionally eighth–sixth centuries BCE | archaeology, early inscriptions, later foundation narratives | Latin amid Italic/Etruscan contact; civic cult and insignia acquire retrospective origin stories | a fully documented royal wardrobe or standard classical prose |
| Republic, conventionally 509–27 BCE | expanding Italy and Mediterranean rule; evidence improves unevenly | magistracies, aristocratic ancestry, civic rites, increasingly extensive writing | a permanent emperor-centered cult or routine imperial portrait coinage |
| Late Republic, especially first century BCE | Cicero, Caesar, civil wars | rhetorical Latinity, competitive pedigrees, personal authority, expanding gold issues | proof that literary dialogue reproduces everyone's speech |
| Principate, conventionally 27 BCE–284 CE | emperor within inherited civic offices; diverse provincial institutions | imperial titulature, ruler cults, dynastic coinage, status dress | one identical cult protocol or costume across the empire |
| Later Roman world, especially third–sixth centuries CE | overlapping regional transitions | naming shifts, monetary reforms, Christian institutions, changing court dress | unchanged aureus tariffs, universal tria nomina, or the early imperial toga silhouette |
- These boundaries are modern analytical conveniences, not synchronized switches in language, clothing, and belief (Clackson & Horrocks 2007; Beard, North & Price 1998).
- Rome's traditional foundation date, 753 BCE, is an ancient chronological construction; “509 BCE” likewise does not make later annalistic detail eyewitness evidence.
- **Application:** use separate clocks for constitutional authority, spoken change, sacred institutions, textile display, and money; make the clocks interact rather than advance together automatically.

## 3. Language family, script, and latinitas
- Latin is Indo-European, within Italic, conventionally grouped with Faliscan as Latino-Faliscan; Etruscan is not an Italic language (Clackson & Horrocks 2007).
- The Latin alphabet belongs to a transmission history involving Greek alphabets and Etruscan mediation; borrowing a script does not imply borrowing a language family.
- Early Latin, classical literary standards, later Latin, and the ancestors of Romance are historical continua, not four mutually sealed languages.
- Distinguish monumental capitals, informal cursive, wax-tablet writing, painted notices, and literary book hands; an empire does not write everything in lapidary capitals.
- Classical I/J and U/V are not the modern letter distinctions; preserve `AVGVSTVS` in a transcription while normalizing *Augustus* in explanation. Modern macrons are editorial aids, not universal ancient practice.
- **Latinitas:** in rhetorical/grammatical discussion, acceptable or correct Latin, carrying judgments about authority and belonging; it is not simply the name of an upper-class dialect.
- Quintilian, *Institutio oratoria* 1.6.1–3, names reason, antiquity, authority, and usage as linguistic warrants; 1.6.43–45 restricts approved usage to educated speakers rather than a majority vote.
- **Named framework:** Quintilian's warrants expose a standard-language ideology; use them to design disputes among grammarians, antiquarians, successful speakers, and everyday users, not as modern linguistic truth.
- “Vulgar Latin” is a modern umbrella with substantial definitional problems; it must not become a single homogeneous language spoken by every non-elite person (Adams 2013).
- Greek remained a major language of education and administration, especially in the eastern Mediterranean; local languages persisted with very unequal documentation. Latin citizenship names do not demonstrate Latin home speech.

| Register/medium | Evidential anchor | Coupling lever | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Rhetorical/educated | Cicero; Quintilian; grammatical commentary | trained speech and controlled gesture authorize civic standing | prescriptions and self-presentation, not a population recording |
| Legal/administrative | documents, statutes, official inscriptions | offices, formulae, names, and dates make acts recognizable | formula and abbreviation vary with document and period |
| Ritual/antiquarian | transmitted prayers, vows, priestly terminology | prestigious old wording can outlive everyday comprehension | literary archaism is not automatically surviving primitive speech |
| Familiar correspondence | surviving letters and tablets | audience and relationship permit departures from public style | familiarity does not equal low social status |
| Informal/nonstandard | graffiti, private documents, reported speech | regional, educational, and situational differences | spelling alone cannot identify class, ethnicity, or pronunciation securely |
| Multilingual | bilingual texts and code-switching | language choice negotiates expertise, audience, and affiliation | “Latin public / Greek private” is not an empire-wide rule |
- **Application:** specify speaker, audience, medium, prestigious authority, and sanctions for each register; avoid making “low” speech grammatically defective.

## 4. Mythic core is not the whole religious system
| Cluster | Roman anchors | Institutional/material coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Civic sovereignty | Jupiter, Juno, Minerva of the Capitoline triad | public temple, vows, civic authority; not a universal household pantheon |
| Warfare and civic ancestry | Mars; Romulus and Remus in foundation traditions | military success and genealogical claims; Mars is not exhausted by a one-word “war god” label |
| Trojan ancestry | Aeneas, the Penates, Venus in Roman literary/genealogical traditions | migration narrative, household continuity, elite descent; Virgil's *Aeneid* is Augustan poetry, not a Bronze Age chronicle |
| Household and neighborhood | Lares, Penates, the household genius | domestic and local ritual; these categories are related but not interchangeable |
| Thresholds and civic continuity | Janus, Vesta | beginnings, hearth, priesthood, calendrical rites; avoid reducing each deity to a game-like portfolio |
- Beard, North & Price (1998): distinguish deity, local epithet, sanctuary, priesthood, festival, offering, and narrative; identical divine names can organize different cult relationships.
- “Orthopraxy rather than orthodoxy” is a useful contrast, not evidence that Romans had no beliefs, emotions, theology, or disagreements.
- Public, household, associational, and imperial practices overlap; “Roman religion” is not a single church with one scripture and a fixed twelve-deity membership list.
- **Application:** give a cult a calendar, authorized performers, locations, and material obligations before assigning a creation myth or a universal moral doctrine.

## 5. Interpretatio romana — translation with political consequences
- The textual anchor is Tacitus, *Germania* 43: he describes the Nahanarvali's divine brothers, named Alcis, through Castor and Pollux, using *interpretatione Romana*.
- This is evidence for Tacitus's explanatory mapping; it does not establish that local worshippers thought their gods were Roman gods under mistaken names.
- Separate **outsider identification**, **locally adopted paired naming**, **shared iconography**, and **institutional cult combination**; one does not prove all the others.
- Similar function is not identity: a historian's “Mars” may conceal multiple local names, rites, kin relations, and political meanings.
- Roman elite ethnography is an imperial filter, not neutral access to colonized or neighboring peoples; Tacitus also uses them to discuss Roman moral concerns.
- **Comparison grid:** local name | outsider equivalent | who asserts equivalence | shared attribute | incompatible rite | patron/funder | date | evidence type.
- **Application:** design rival translations by merchants, conquerors, priests, and local elites; preserve the possibility that locals reject the offered equivalence.
- Do not reconstruct an otherwise unattested local theology from a Roman label alone; names, ritual details, and language outside the surviving report are not attested elsewhere.

## 6. Imperial cult — institutions, not one compulsory creed
| Setting/object of honor | Working distinction | Coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Living ruler at Rome | honors to the ruler and to his genius/numen need contextual differentiation | public benefaction, household idiom, sacrifice, titles |
| Provincial/civic ruler cult | local and provincial institutions vary; Roma and Augustus can be honored together | Greek/Latin naming, priesthoods, city competition, festivals |
| Consecrated dead ruler | *divus* marks a recognized deified figure in Roman titulature | genealogy, succession, temples, coin legends |
| Imperial household | family members may receive honors, with different forms and histories | dynastic representation without identical status for every relative |
- Suetonius, *Augustus* 52, reports Augustus accepting provincial temples only jointly with Roma and refusing that honor in Rome; treat this as his biographical presentation of Augustus, not a universal law for every emperor.
- Posthumous deification was not automatic for every emperor; distinguish the date of death, recognition, and the resulting title (Beard, North & Price 1998).
- *Deus* and *divus* must not be treated as an exceptionless theological binary across languages, centuries, and genres; official titulature is more specific than everyday vocabulary.
- Price (1984), *Rituals and Power*: cult in Asia Minor helped articulate relations between ruler and communities; “mere cynical flattery” is an inadequate explanation, but Asia Minor is not the whole empire.
- **Application:** specify who proposes an honor, funds the building, officiates, attends, benefits, and can refuse; separate local competition from central commands.

## 8. Costume/material signature — toga grammar is a metaphor
- **Critical correction:** *latus clavus* is the broad purple stripe associated with a senatorial **tunic**, not the purple border of a toga. *Toga praetexta* names the bordered outer garment.
- Civic status dress is context-dependent signaling, not a literal language or a complete wardrobe census (Sebesta & Bonfante 1994; Edmondson & Keith 2008).

| Item | Principal distinction in the late Republican/early imperial reference frame | Coupled system / exception |
|---|---|---|
| Tunica with latus clavus; *tunica laticlavia* | broad stripe, senatorial association | rank eligibility; Augustus extended its use to senators' sons, so “wearer = serving senator” fails (*Aug.* 38.2) |
| Narrow clavus; *angustus clavus* | equestrian distinction in conventional descriptions | do not infer a precise stripe width or complete social biography from every image |
| Toga virilis / pura | plain toga associated with entry into adult male civic standing | lifecycle rite; do not fix one universal initiation age |
| Toga praetexta, childhood | purple-bordered garment associated with freeborn childhood, including girls in the literary tradition | age/protection does not confer magistracy |
| Toga praetexta, office/ritual | certain magistrates and priestly settings | office, occasion, and childhood may use a related visual device without identical rights |
| Toga candida | conspicuously whitened candidate's toga | electoral self-presentation, not an eternal rank color |
| Toga pulla | dark garment associated with mourning and distress | do not assume every dark garment is mourning or every mourner uses one uniform hue |
| Toga picta | decorated triumphal dress; later ceremonial adaptations | extraordinary honor and historical reuse, not routine senatorial clothing |
- Quintilian 11.3.137–149 links tunic length, stripe, folds, gesture, and the progress of a speech; his ideal orator is a normative elite case, not an average person.
- Suetonius, *Augustus* 40.5, reports toga enforcement near the Forum; a demand to restore a dress norm is evidence of tension, not proof of universal compliance.
- Woolen draped civic dress coexists with tunics, cloaks, linen textiles, regional fashions, and working clothes; fiber, weave, cleaning, repair, and season need independent specification.
- Women's stola/palla terminology and the literary association of some women with the toga require caution; “all respectable women wore X, all prostitutes wore Y” is an overconfident wardrobe census.
- Cloth survives unevenly; statues idealize bodies and often lose color. Exact early cuts, dye recipes, and province-specific access rules are not established here.
- **Application:** couple garment eligibility to age, office, wealth, and occasion; test an outfit whose rank signal passes while its wearer's speech or name reveals a mismatch.

## 9. Naming practices — status-bearing, historical, not a three-box generator
- Salway (1994) traces changes across roughly 700 BCE–700 CE; the familiar *tria nomina* is an important configuration, not every Roman's timeless identity system.

| Element/practice | Reference meaning | Design constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Praenomen | personal name from a restricted conventional repertoire; often abbreviated | `M.` commonly Marcus, `L.` Lucius; `C.` Gaius and `Cn.` Gnaeus need learned expansion |
| Nomen gentilicium | affiliation expressed through a gentile name, e.g. Tullius | not a modern hereditary surname with identical social behavior |
| Cognomen | additional identifying name, potentially inherited or accumulated | not necessarily a fresh descriptive nickname or transparent personality trait |
| Filiation / freed status | `f.` and `l.` can encode *filius/filia* and *libertus/liberta* in context | a letter alone is not a safe expansion without syntax and date |
| Tribal designation | civic enrollment category in appropriate records | not proof of ethnicity, place of birth, or a modern political party |
| Women | feminine gentile names, later and contextual additional names | “all daughters are only Julia” erases variation and change |
| Enslaved/freed persons | naming reflects ownership, manumission, and patronal relations | a freed person's citizen nomenclature can retain a previous personal name as cognomen; not a universal mechanical rule |
| Adoption / imperial naming | names may advertise new affiliation, descent, and political continuity | do not impose one suffix transformation or a maximum of three names |
- *Marcus Tullius Cicero* illustrates the familiar three elements; it is a grammatical example, not a template for every century, gender, or legal category.
- *Caesar* moves from a family cognomen into imperial naming and titulature; *Augustus* becomes an honorific/title. Their function must be dated.
- Caracalla's citizenship grant in 212 CE helps explain the spread of *Aurelius*; a common nomen is not proof of one bloodline or the disappearance of local identity.
- **Application:** store full formal name, spoken address, document abbreviation, inherited affiliations, acquired affiliations, legal standing, language, and era separately; never infer all of them from one field.

## 10. Consular dating — names and offices as chronological infrastructure
- A common Roman year identifier names the consuls; `X et Y consulibus` is an ablative construction, “when X and Y were consuls,” not a numbered regnal year (Cooley 2012).
- Constructed teaching example: *M. Tullio C. Antonio consulibus* identifies the consulship of Cicero and Gaius Antonius in 63 BCE; this is not presented as a transcript of a particular document.
- Distinguish ordinary consuls associated with the year's opening from replacement/suffect consuls; document type and date determine which pair matters.
- Repeated consulships, similar names, damaged letters, and rival recognitions demand a dated *fasti* reference; a plausible-looking pair is not verification.
- `COS III` means a third consulship in titulature, not “year three”; `TR P` counts tribunician power in its own renewal sequence. Cross-check both with other titles before assigning a year.
- *Kalendae* = the month's first day; Nones and Ides fall later in March, May, July, and October than in the other months. Count backward inclusively.
- Worked calculation: *ante diem III Idus Martias* = 13 March, counting 15, 14, 13; specify the calendar and year before converting into another chronology.
- Caesar's Julian reform took effect in 45 BCE; pre-reform intercalation and initial implementation problems prevent naive conversion by modern month/day arithmetic.
- *Ab urbe condita* is an ancient chronological convention, not the mandatory date on every letter, coin, or contract; different foundation reckonings also existed.
- **Application:** specify who maintains office lists, how disputed officeholders affect document validity, and whether local, civic, regnal, and sacred calendars coexist.

## 11. Aureus vocabulary and an object that couples the systems
| Term | Working sense | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| *Aureus*, plural *aurei* | gold denomination; adjectival “golden” used substantivally | not every gold object or every Roman gold coin |
| *Denarius*, plural *denarii* | silver denomination in the relevant early imperial system | metal content, tariff, and purchasing power are separate variables |
| *Sestertius*, plural *sestertii* | denomination and important money-of-account vocabulary | an amount in sesterces does not require a sack of those coins |
| *As*, plural *asses*; *aes* | denomination versus copper/bronze or monetary vocabulary | do not translate both as the same fixed unit or alloy |
| *Nummus*, *pecunia* | coin/money terminology dependent on context | *nummus* can become more specific; neither word alone fixes a denomination across eras |
| *Moneta* | Juno Moneta/mint-related vocabulary and its later semantic history | check a dated lexical source before assigning a particular derived sense |
- Under the Augustan/early imperial tariff, **1 aureus = 25 denarii = 100 sestertii**; this is not an eternal exchange rate or a measure of a stable household shopping basket (Burnett 1987; MoneyMuseum record 397).
- Gold's value makes the aureus unsuitable as a universal small-purchase token; distinguish account unit, actual tender, bullion, credit, and local/provincial coin systems.
- Third- and fourth-century reforms and the rise of the solidus require a new monetary model; do not retain the early imperial conversion table unchanged.
- **Checked object:** Sutherland (1984), *RIC* I², Augustus 206; [OCRE catalog record](https://numismatics.org/ocre/id/ric.1(2).aug.206) attributes this gold aureus to Lugdunum, 2 BCE–4 CE. These are catalog attributions, open to scholarly revision.
- Obverse legend as cataloged: `CAESAR AVGVSTVS DIVI F PATER PATRIAE`; portrait: laureate head of Augustus.
- Read `DIVI F` as *divi filius*, “son of the deified [Julius]”: the living ruler claims deified ancestry; this does not call Augustus himself *divus* on this issue.
- Reverse legend as cataloged: `C L CAESARES AVGVSTI F COS DESIG PRINC IVVENT`; the named Gaius and Lucius are represented togate with shields/spears, with a simpulum and lituus in the field.
- Here `COS DESIG` presents designated future consular office, not a year numeral; the youth/dynasty, civic dress, weapons, priestly objects, and abbreviated names perform different parts of one succession claim.
- **Application:** design an object's issuer, audience, portability, legend, image, dating claims, and ritual references together; an ideological coin is not necessarily a literal scene of a ceremony.

## 13. What a worldbuilder can take
1. **Eligibility across media:** couple schooling, public register, name, dress, office, and cult participation without pretending they identify the same thing perfectly.
2. **Imperial translation politics:** let administrative divine equivalents and local sacred identities disagree; distinguish an outsider's label from an adopted rite.
3. **A layered ruler:** separate living person, inherited names, acquired offices, sacred honors, dynastic ancestry, and posthumous status.
4. **Names as records of change:** let adoption, manumission, grants, marriage contexts, and political realignment leave historically uneven traces rather than universal name formulas.
5. **Objects as compact institutions:** combine coin legends, portraits, ritual implements, dress, and promised offices; specify what different readers can decode.
6. **A chronological audit:** test whether title, consul pair, naming pattern, garment, script, and denomination could coexist in the same reference horizon.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: offer alternatives with reference date/place, generative rule, expected exceptions, source pointer, confidence, and unresolved verification; do not declare a fictional culture's names, gods, costumes, or chronology canonical.

## 14. Provenance cautions and thin areas
- **Evidence asymmetry:** elite male literary authors, selective inscriptions, commissioned images, and archaeologically favored materials dominate; enslaved people, women, rural communities, and many local languages are unevenly recoverable.
- **Source bias:** Quintilian prescribes cultivated behavior; Suetonius constructs imperial character; Tacitus interprets outsiders through Roman categories. None is a neutral census or a transcript of collective belief.
- **Chronicle scarcity:** regal institutional origins and detailed archaic dress chronologies remain thin here; a later story that a king “invented” an insignia is not a secure event date. Such attributions belong to later literary tradition (Livy 1.8 on Romulus' twelve lictors and the Etruscan origin of curule chair and toga praetexta; Dionysius of Halicarnassus 3.61 on the insignia brought to Tarquinius Priscus), not to contemporary evidence.
- **Reconstruction limits:** exact dialect-to-class mappings, garment dimensions, dye identification from faded images, and region-specific ritual compliance need dedicated evidence; no universal recipe is supplied.
- **Verification scope:** linked ancient passages and the OCRE type were inspected; publisher front matter and selected catalog metadata establish bibliographic anchors, not a fresh full-text audit of every modern monograph. No uninspected page-specific quotation or precision measurement is asserted.
- **Misleading defaults to reject:** decadent Latin as a linguistic fact; Roman gods as merely renamed Greeks; universal emperor worship; latus clavus as a toga border; every Roman with three names; every year dated AUC; an aureus worth the same basket of goods for centuries.
- **Method boundary:** the matrices and application rules are analytical aids, not ancient native taxonomies. Reference does not authorize a single moral essence of “Roman culture,” and it does not settle fictional canon.
