## Scope and calibration

- “Inuit” is not one ancient state, uniform religion, or timeless costume; specify community, language variety, date, and source before combining details.
- This entry's strongest cultural evidence is eastern/central Canadian: nineteenth-century Baffin accounts, Igloolik fieldwork, and contemporary Sanikiluaq/Kivalliq naming testimony.
- Alaska, western Canada, and Greenland appear chiefly for comparison; their ritual vocabularies and naming restrictions are thinly covered here, not reconstructed from Canadian evidence.
- Inuit and Yupik are linguistically distinguishable even when “Inuit” is an inclusive political designation; do not transplant Yup'ik ritual practices as generic Inuktitut facts. [D; K]
- Retain obsolete ethnonyms only inside historical source titles or when explaining the literature; use communities' own contemporary names in working prose. [K]
- Calibration labels: **documented-local** = evidence for a stated place/time; **comparative** = scholarly/generalizing model; **design heuristic** = a worldbuilding operation, not an Inuit belief.
- `(to-verify)` identifies a claim or detail not established by this source bundle; it is not permission to turn a plausible inference into reference knowledge.

## Language family and writing: choose a locality first

- **Family:** Dorais uses *Eskaleut*; *Eskimo-Aleut* is the conventional historical linguistic label. Inuit varieties, Yupik languages, and Unangan must not be collapsed into one language. [D, chs. 1–2; K]
- The Inuit branch spans related varieties across Alaska, Canada, and Greenland; dialect-continuum language does not mean effortless comprehension between distant endpoints. [D]

| Reference area | Language label to retain | Writing orientation; coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Northern Alaska | Iñupiaq; speakers/people Iñupiat | Latin-based orthography; do not decorate an Alaskan reference with eastern Canadian syllabics. [K; T] |
| Western Canadian Arctic | Inuvialuktun and Inuinnaqtun, with finer community distinctions | Latin-based systems; broad administrative labels do not supply a single local pronunciation or glossary. [D; T] |
| Much of eastern Canadian Arctic | Inuktitut; Labrador also uses Inuttut | Syllabics and Latin orthographies vary by region; Labrador's Moravian writing tradition is distinct. [T] |
| Greenland | Kalaallisut for the West Greenlandic reference; distinguish other regional varieties | Latin-based writing; a Greenlandic lexical example is not automatically an Inuktitut example. [D; K; T] |

- Canadian syllabics were adapted from systems developed for Cree/Ojibwe and introduced through missionary activity; Inuit then circulated literacy within families. Not an immemorial sacred Arctic script. [T, Writing]
- Greenlandic religious literacy developed through eighteenth-century missions; the 1973 orthographic revision is a later institutional stage, not a change in the people's identity. [T]
- The Canadian ICI writing standard of 1976 paired syllabic and Roman forms and was ratified by Inuit organizations; distinguish this historical standard from later proposals and present uptake. [T; ITK]
- **Design heuristic:** attach script to transmission institutions—households, missions, schools, publishing, administration—rather than to “snow culture.”
- For a fictional script, invent forms and a transmission history; do not use living Inuit syllabics as interchangeable exotic runes.

## Polysynthetic morphology: a generative model, not long-word decoration

- **Framework:** distinguish root, derivational material/postbases, and grammatical ending; polysynthesis concerns how much grammatical/semantic structure a word can carry. [D; T]
- A teaching schematic is `ROOT → zero or more AFFIXES → appropriate GRAMMATICAL ENDING`; it is not a claim that all affixes are freely permutable. [T, Basic Structure]
- Agglutination, fusion, and polysynthesis are different dimensions: transparent suffix chains can coexist with sound changes and endings expressing several grammatical distinctions.

| Component | Documented Canadian teaching evidence | Fictional-language operation |
|---|---|---|
| Root | `niri-` “eat,” `aullaq-` “depart,” `nuna` “land” [T] | Define noun/verb bases separately; establish what can occur as a complete word. |
| Spatial inflection | `sijjami` “at the shoreline,” `sijjamut` “to the shoreline,” `sijjamit` “from the shoreline” [T] | Build a case system tied to travel and location, not a list of unconnected place words. |
| Derivation/time material | `aullalauqtunga` “I departed,” built with past `-lauq-` [T, Basic Structure] | Keep a segmentation/gloss and surface-form record; phonological combination is not simple string concatenation. |
| Participant endings | `takujunga` “I see”; `takujara` “I see him/her/it” [T, Double Verb Endings] | Decide whether predicates index only a subject or both subject and object. |
| Number | `matu`, `matuuk`, `matuit`: one, two, more than two doors [T, Dual] | Number distinctions must propagate into paradigms; do not infer every Inuit variety preserves the same dual. |
| Morphophonology | T's transitive-ending page documents changes conditioned by the root's final consonant | Specify productive boundary rules and exceptions; prohibit arbitrary “Arctic-sounding” spellings. |

- Long forms are useful diagnostics only when their constituent meanings and boundary changes are sourced; word length itself is not the design goal.
- Full Canadian Inuktitut receives a predominantly ergative–absolutive case analysis, though accounts differ on how the agreement system patterns; the linked introductory pages do not establish its complete case/voice system, so exact paradigms here still require a local grammar. [SP; C]
- Noun-to-verb derivation should not automatically be called unrestricted “noun incorporation”; the structural analysis and permitted base/affix combinations need a grammar, not a typological slogan.
- **Application:** choose a limited base inventory, ordered derivations, argument/number endings, and sound rules; generate a small set of checked paradigms before creating ritual or garment vocabulary.
- Couple clothing terms to actions—cutting, fitting, wearing, repairing, carrying—only where the fictional morphology licenses those derivations.
- Never claim polysynthesis prevents analysis, abstract thought, wordplay, or borrowing; it is a structural resource, not a cognitive limit.

## Snow vocabulary: Martin/Pullum without a reverse myth

- **Discredited popular claim:** an unspecified “Eskimo language” has a fixed spectacular number of snow words, proving its speakers inhabit an inaccessible conceptual world. [M; P]
- Martin traces the example's transmission; Pullum, pp. 276–278, shows claims inflated through repetition without a stable language, counting unit, or dataset.
- **Counting framework:** root ≠ lexeme ≠ inflected/derived surface form ≠ multiword description; comparing them as if equivalent makes the result uninterpretable.

| Claim or operation | Calibration | Better reference practice |
|---|---|---|
| “Inuktitut has hundreds of snow words” | Unsupported without variety, corpus, and counting rule | Name the community, source, period, and lexical unit; count reproducibly. |
| “Inuit languages have only two snow words” | An equally misleading universal | Pullum's two relevant roots were drawn from a particular West Greenlandic dictionary, not a census of Inuktitut. [P, p. 280] |
| `aput` “snow on the ground”; `qanik` “snow in the air/snowflake” | Pullum's report of Schultz-Lorentzen's 1927 West Greenlandic dictionary | Retain language/source labels; do not relabel this pair as exhaustive modern Inuktitut vocabulary. |
| A derived word glossed by an English sentence | Compatible with polysynthesis | Compare like units; a new surface form need not be a new lexical root. |
| Expertise distinguishes snow/ice conditions | Plausible, but specific inventories require evidence | Ask what distinctions change travel, building, clothing care, or hunting decisions. |
| Pullum's suggestion that hunters need not be interested in snow | Polemical speculation, not field evidence | Keep the citation critique; do not use that aside to dismiss Inuit environmental knowledge. |

- Snow and sea ice are related environmental domains, not one automatically merged lexical field; a snow-word list does not establish sea-ice terminology.
- **Application:** build an affordance matrix—condition, observation, consequential task, lexical status, register—instead of chasing an impressive word count.
- Example categories for elicitation, not asserted Inuit translations: wind transport; crust/support; melt/wetness; visibility; suitability for shelter; adhesion to clothing.
- A distinction may be expressed by a root, productive modifier, verb, or phrase; cultural importance does not require a separate noun.
- This debunking does not settle every version of linguistic relativity; avoid both strong language-determines-thought claims and unsupported denials of language/culture interaction.

## Mythic core and symbolic system: relations before pantheon slots

| Documented complex | Source and locality | Coupling to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Sea Woman/Sedna narratives | B, “Sedna and the fulmar,” beginning p. 583; Oqomiut/Akudnirmiut tellings and reported comparisons | Marine animals emerge from severed finger parts; betrayal, bodily transformation, subsistence, and ritual mediation intersect. |
| Multiple versions of that narrative | B gives differing prose/song accounts and compares Nuliajoq and Greenland traditions | Variant names/tellings are not automatically separate deities or one standardized biography. |
| Animal availability and powerful beings | B's comparisons; R's Igloolik account | The hunt is framed through relations and appropriate conduct, not only technical mastery. |
| Raven/fox and light | R, p. 3, adapting Rasmussen's 1930 account | Spoken wishes establish light and evening; cosmological speech is consequential, not merely descriptive. |
| Human beginnings and death through speech | R, pp. 2–3, citing Rasmussen 1929 | Words change bodily/social possibilities; retain these as attributed narrative claims, not literal historical reconstruction. |
| Animal personhood and reciprocity | R's 2004 Igloolik interviews, including Qulittalik and Ulaajuruluk | Animals receive human speech, intentions, and treatment; material use and moral address share referents. |

- **Comparative lens:** “animism” or relational personhood can describe an analytical pattern; neither licenses a universal Inuit creed or a copied deity roster.
- “Shaman” is a comparative gloss; `angakkuq` names a locally situated ritual specialist, not necessarily a sovereign, hereditary priest, or costume class.
- Broader Sila/weather cosmologies, lunar traditions, and Greenland/Alaska spirit classifications need local primary accounts `(to-verify)`; this bundle is not a full pantheon survey.
- Avoid “Sedna = ocean goddess = queen of all Inuit gods”; Boas's own comparisons contain uncertainties and externally imposed theological categories.
- **Symbolic operation:** map who can address whom, what substance carries a relation, and what breach changes access to food, health, or community—not merely animal-to-virtue equivalences.
- A caribou skin can be sheltering material and evidence of a human–animal relationship; do not reduce the animal to a heraldic mascot. [R; I]
- Garment cut, contrasting fur, tools, and adornment can communicate skilled production and local affiliation; a universal color/allegiance code is unsupported. [B, dress section; I]

## Angakkuq registers: substitution, metaphor, and speech consequences

- **Documented-local:** Igloolik elders call the ritual register `angakkuit uqausingit`, “shamans' words.” Randa argues “ritual language” may be more accurate because non-specialists also used it in particular circumstances. [R, p. 5]
- It is not simply “ordinary Inuktitut whispered”: R describes alternate lexical fields for animals, humans, anatomy, tools, phenomena, actions, and social categories.
- Some terminology was publicly known; some formulas/songs were secret. Do not make all Inuit speakers initiates or publish an invented “complete sacred dictionary.” [R]

| Register / circumstance | Documented distinction | Design inference, explicitly not canon |
|---|---|---|
| Everyday animal naming | Common species names support ordinary communication | Give practical specialists a precise everyday vocabulary. |
| Ritual naming | Substitution avoids inappropriate direct address and can please/attract animals | Define audience, permitted speakers, context, and substitution rule before inventing words. |
| Poetic/personal songs | `pisiit`; metaphor and pleasing expression; overlap with ritual terminology | Permit shared lexemes with different pragmatic effects rather than two wholly unrelated languages. |
| Mourning or other restricted states | Older reports quoted by R require some non-shamans to use substitutions | A temporary speaker-state restriction differs from permanent caste ownership of a register. |
| Contemporary Igloolik testimony | In 2004 the substitution register was described as largely forgotten, while respectful animal speech remained consequential | Historical change can preserve a speech ethic without preserving every inherited term. |

- Checkable example: R, p. 6, pairs everyday `nanuq` “polar bear” with ritual `pisukti` “the walker” and poetic `pisuqasiaq`, translated there “the ever-wandering one.”
- These are source-specific examples, not permission to generate Inuit ritual vocabulary by substituting any descriptive phrase for any animal.
- R also reports different registers can use the same designation for different animals; substitution is relational/contextual, not a one-to-one cipher.
- R notes that grammar/syntax was concerned in the ritual register and that research on it is still pending; R's overall account is explicitly preliminary, so no systematic alternate grammar, archaic tense system, or obligatory cryptic phonology is established. [R, pp. 1, 5]
- R's older menstruation/bereavement restrictions must retain their historical attribution; “unclean” is source terminology, not this entry's endorsement or a rule for contemporary Inuit.
- **Application:** derive fictional ritual/poetic registers from speech situations and social consequences; leave actual restricted formulas uncopied.

## Parka/anorak technology: vocabulary follows construction and use

- English “parka” and “anorak” are broad reference labels, not a single universal Inuit garment name; local cut, layer, material, wearer, and task matter. [I]
- B's dress section, pp. 554 onward, documents differences in hood shape, cuts, skin choices, ornaments, and layers; its aesthetic judgments are the observer's, not indigenous classification.

| Term or technical distinction | Reference meaning / calibration | Coupled systems |
|---|---|---|
| `amauti`; `amaut` | Canadian reference terms for a child-carrying parka and its carrying pouch; local forms/cuts vary [I; secondary cross-check] | Childcare, body movement, household skill, kinship; not a generic coat with a decorative hood. |
| `atigi`; `qulittaq` | Inner/outer parka distinction in Canadian clothing reference terminology and museum descriptions; regional forms and spellings vary (e.g. `qulittaujaq` in Tusaalanga) [I; PM; T] | Layer position and fur orientation become lexical distinctions, not social rank by default. |
| `kamik`, `kamiik` | Skin boot; dual “two skin boots” explicitly exemplified by T | Garment vocabulary and grammatical number interact; do not confuse paired-object forms with singular names. |
| `ivalu` | Sinew thread; dictionary-attested as “sinew (for sewing)” (Inuinnaqtun; also Inupiaq) and used in clothing literature [I; NED] | Anatomy, preparation, sewing, strength, and repair; not generic woven textile yarn. |
| `ulu` | Cutting/scraping tool associated with clothing production [I]; B describes use of the woman's knife | Tool affordance, skilled labor, hide geometry, and household transmission. |
| Caribou versus seal skin | Dry-cold insulation and wet-condition uses require different preparation/material choices [I; B] | Hunting season, animal relations, weather, maintenance, and lexicon. |
| Inner/outer layer and fur direction | A functional clothing system, not simply maximum bulk [I; B] | Fit, mobility, heat retention, ventilation, and task. |
| Cut, ruff, pouch, fastening, seam | Distinguish structural components and functions [I] | Regional style, wearer biography, climate, infant support, and workmanship. |

- **Operational chain:** acquisition → skin selection → scraping/softening → measuring/cutting → joining → fitting → drying/repair; this is an analytical production model, not an asserted indigenous taxonomy.
- Distinguish hide preparation from spinning/weaving: a skin garment need not imply a loom, sheep husbandry, or a cloth economy.
- Waterproofing depends on preparation, joining, thread, fit, and upkeep; “sealskin” is not a magic all-purpose waterproof adjective. Exact stitch procedures `(to-verify)` before technical reconstruction. [I]
- Snow removal and storage are part of the technology: B describes beating snow off outer garments before storage rather than treating the coat as inert scenery.
- The baby pouch is not simply the hood: nineteenth-century observers sometimes describe the carrying arrangement imprecisely; use construction drawings and wearer knowledge before modeling it. [B; I]
- Do not universalize historical gendered labor descriptions into a biological ban on other makers or wearers; distinguish documented convention, individual practice, and present design.
- **Application:** create a component/operation lexicon before ornamental labels; test whether a garment supports its wearer's actual work, weather, childcare, and repair access.

## Naming and kin-address: do not merge all “taboos”

- **Framework:** separate genealogy, namesake relationships, direct address, reference to absent people, temporary avoidance, and official registration.
- `atiq` is “name”; Dupré describes namesake relations using `sauniq`, while Sanikiluaq often prefers `atituqaq` for an inherited/traditional name. Retain locality rather than standardizing away variation. [U, pp. 1–4]
- Names link people through memory, obligations, authority, affection, and sometimes accounts of returning personhood; they are not just unique labels or a fixed Western surname system. [U]

| Phenomenon | Evidence and calibration | What must not be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Kin terms instead of given-name address | Kivalliq elders reported in N emphasize `tuq&urausiit` and name-linked kin address | “Nobody ever utters an Inuit person's name” is an unsupported universal. |
| Namesake-mediated kinship | N describes colleagues named after two brothers treating their relationship as sibling-like; U examines name-created relations | Genealogy alone does not determine every address term; a name is not merely an honorary nickname. |
| Deceased and living namesakes | U contrasts common deceased-namesake accounts with Sanikiluaq's documented living namesakes | A universal permanent ban on dead names is incompatible with transmission/reuse evidence. |
| Multiple names | U documents combinations of traditional, Christian, and newer names, with generational changes | Every name must not be flattened into one immutable first-name/last-name slot. |
| Names crossing the namesake's sex | Matilda Sulurayok in N names her daughter after her father | The donor's sex does not automatically determine the child's sex, clothing, or social role. |
| Name and skill expectation | Sulurayok's account connects her namesake's words to later sealskin sewing skill [N] | This is testimony about a particular relationship, not a universal hereditary occupational rule. |
| Mourner's animal-name substitutions | R quotes historical restrictions after particular bereavements | An animal-name restriction is not automatically a taboo on saying the deceased relative's personal name. |
| Illness-associated renaming | B's naming section reports angakut changing names, with regional/observer limits | Neither all sickness nor every contemporary name change should be explained by this practice. |

- N reproduces `&` in some kin-address spellings; it is an orthographic convention in that source, not conlang punctuation to copy without explanation.
- A sex/age-sensitive genealogical address term, an inherited namesake term, and an affectionate form can coexist; record who addresses whom rather than assigning each person one title.
- U's contemporary Sanikiluaq observations are especially useful against static models: parents, grandparents, adoption, media-derived names, and certificates interact.
- Chronological caution: B's claims about extensive deceased-name transmission are nineteenth-century reports, whereas U documents another locality and later changes; neither cancels the other.
- Exact in-law avoidance rules, restrictions on particular relatives' personal names, and mourning durations across regions are not established here `(to-verify)`; do not supply invented sanctions.
- The textbook category formerly called “Eskimo kinship” is an external comparative label, not a sufficient description of lived Inuit name-based relationships.
- **Application:** maintain separate fictional genealogy and namesake graphs; assign address forms to directed relationships and contexts, then test conversations across generations.
- Keep belief statements attributed: spiritual explanations of health or naming outcomes are testimony, not biomedical causal claims.

## Era staging: date institutions and evidence, not “primitive” peoples

| Stage for reference selection | Language / record changes | Myth, symbol, and clothing implications |
|---|---|---|
| Archaeological backstory | Thule/ancestral Inuit and earlier Paleo-Inuit/Dorset labels are conventional archaeological categories with regionally variable chronologies; dates require specialist local sources (NM) | Do not automatically project nineteenth-century names or ritual registers into every earlier archaeological culture. |
| Before regional missionary literacy | Oral narratives, remembered relationships, skilled demonstrations; no local written chronicle is consulted here | Historical knowledge exists without a written court chronicle; surviving material evidence does not fully specify a ritual lexicon. |
| Regional missions and trade | Greenland religious literacy from the eighteenth century; eastern Canadian syllabics introduced in the nineteenth [T] | Imported goods and religious institutions alter transmission; the dates and trajectories differ by community. |
| Late nineteenth-century Baffin reference | B's observations plus older traveler reports | Source already includes brass ornaments and outside contact; it is not an untouched pre-contact snapshot. |
| Twentieth-century standardization and institutional change | Orthographic reforms/standards documented by T; exact local administrative naming policies `(to-verify)` | Do not infer that a school, certificate, or Christian name immediately eliminates namesake relations or skin-clothing expertise. |
| Early twenty-first-century documented cases | R's 2004 Igloolik interviews; U's Sanikiluaq work; N's 2014 Kivalliq reporting | Respectful speech and naming relations persist/change selectively; literary records, modern names, and material skill coexist. |

- Use two clocks: historical/institutional change and recurring seasonal production/use; “winter” is not a historical era.
- **Application:** stage a change by its distribution—new spelling, material, name category, or ritual setting—rather than replacing all cultural systems at once.
- Exact Thule migration dates, mission dates outside the examples above, and current language-policy uptake are deliberate gaps, not inferred chronology.

## Provenance cautions and remaining thin ground

- Colonial-source bias is substantial: B's “priesthood,” “Supreme Being,” aesthetic rankings, and travelers' theories are interpretive layers, not transparent Inuit self-description.
- B sometimes explicitly expresses uncertainty; retain it. Collector comparison is not proof that all local figures or narratives were historically identical.
- R combines modern testimony with Rasmussen-era records; its 2004 claim about register loss is not a census of all Inuit communities or a statement about practice today.
- U is a local, explicitly preliminary study; its hypotheses about adoption/kin strategy must not become pan-Inuit laws.
- Inuit-authored pre-missionary written chronicles are not the evidentiary base here; oral narratives and Indigenous testimony are indispensable, and scarcity of written archives is not absence of history.
- Thin areas: Greenland/Alaska ritual registers; exact kin-name avoidance rules; garment-term distributions; ritual syntax; archaeological chronology; contemporary policy. These remain `(to-verify)` before detailed reconstruction.
- Bibliographic verification is not full-text verification: D's extract and I's publisher page do not certify unseen page-level claims; clothing-specific limits are identified above.
- Public availability does not authorize unrestricted reuse of community knowledge; consult living speakers/makers for representation, permissions, and contemporary terminology rather than turning a scholarly citation into cultural consent.
