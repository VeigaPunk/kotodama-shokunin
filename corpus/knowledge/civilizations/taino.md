# Taíno — loanword survival, zemí relations, and ritual landscapes
- topic: Arawakan language traces in the Caribbean; zemí cosmology; batey performance; exported hammock/canoa terminology; cave-art registers; era, naming, and material-culture coupling.

## Scope and evidence calibration
- Use “Taíno” as a qualified archaeological/historical umbrella, not a demonstrated single nation, uniform language territory, or self-declared island empire.
- Curet (2014) critiques the term's tendency to homogenize populations; Rouse's (1992) regional classifications are influential models, not timeless Indigenous identity boundaries.
- Geographic anchor: the Greater Antilles and Lucayan archipelago; the strongest colonial myth account here concerns particular Hispaniolan communities, not every island.
- Distinguish language-family affiliation, artifact tradition, political allegiance, and present-day identity: none automatically proves the other three.
- Indigenous descendants and contemporary Taíno identification must not be erased by describing a historical speech community as lost or incompletely documented (Forte 2006).

| Label | Evidence type | What it permits; what it does not |
|---|---|---|
| Material | Contextualized archaeology, manufacture, spatial relation | Establishes objects/practices; rarely an exact deity-name or spoken sentence. |
| Colonial | Pané and other European witnesses, mediated Indigenous testimony | Records particular claims and encounters; missionary categories and language limitations remain active biases. |
| Reconstruction | Comparative linguistics or contextual iconographic interpretation | Offers testable relationships; not recovered verbatim speech or a decoded visual dictionary. |
| Craft inference | Deliberate design extrapolation | Generates fictional systems only; must not be presented as a Taíno historical fact. |
| `(to-verify)` | Passage, derivation, or interpretation not securely checked | Exclude from authoritative naming rules or history until the indicated check is done. |

## Language family, transmission, and writing
- Taíno is conventionally classified as Arawakan; do not equate the entire Arawakan family with modern Lokono, or classify Taíno as Cariban merely because it is Caribbean.
- Granberry and Vescelius (2004) distinguish regional varieties and reconstruct language geography from sparse evidence; their fine-grained maps and etymologies are hypotheses, not census returns.
- Their proposed affiliations of poorly documented neighboring languages require independent review `(to-verify)`; do not make the whole archipelago linguistically uniform.
- The surviving record is disproportionately nouns, personal/place names, short utterances, and words filtered through European spelling; it is not a balanced conversational corpus.
- Spanish spellings do not give a phonemic transcription: retain an attested spelling beside any normalized form, and label phonological reconstruction explicitly.
- A family relationship licenses comparison with other Arawakan languages, not copying a modern mainland grammar wholesale and calling it recovered Taíno.
- No securely deciphered Taíno glottographic script is established in the sources used here; petroglyphs and pictographs must not be advertised as a translated alphabet.
- “Non-glottographic” does not mean meaningless: images, performed speech, genealogy, remembered place, and objects can organize knowledge without spelling sentences.
- Modern reclamation projects have contemporary community purposes; distinguish their new conventions from words independently documented in early records.
- Application: build separate historical-word, colonial-spelling, comparative-reconstruction, and modern-coinage columns before using this case to design a fictional language.

## Loanwords as an uneven archive of conquest
- Working framework: **contact pathway + semantic change + political asymmetry**; a borrowed noun establishes lexical transmission, not the speaker's whole cosmology.
- European borrowing of Indigenous ecological and technical terms coexisted with coercion, displacement, and language shift; lexical prestige can survive without political power.
- Conversely, an isolated loanword cannot prove that a specific borrowing transaction was violent: establish conquest from historical context, not phonology alone.

| Case | Secure working relationship | Coupled system | Calibration / trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Hurricane | English *hurricane* belongs to the Spanish *huracán* borrowing history, attributed to a Taíno source. | Regional weather knowledge → imperial navigation → exported hazard vocabulary. | Do not reconstruct a deity's identity from the modern English noun. Exact Indigenous phonemic form `(to-verify)`. |
| Cassava | Taíno-attributed *caçabi* underlies European *casabe/cassave/cassava* forms; *casabe* denotes prepared cassava bread. | Crop processing → durable food → colonial provisioning → botanical terminology. | English plant/root meaning must not be projected unchanged onto the bread term; precise intermediary route `(to-verify)`. |
| Hammock | Taíno-attributed *hamaca* entered Spanish; English *hammock* and French *hamac* belong to its later dispersal. | Cotton/fiber manufacture → domestic suspension → portable colonial equipment. | A loan preserves a technical label, not necessarily the original maker's social status or ritual use. |
| Canoa | Spanish *canoa*, attributed to Taíno, lies behind English *canoe* and related European forms. | Boatbuilding → inter-island mobility → colonial description and transport. | Do not assume every later “canoe” retains one Caribbean hull design; direct versus intermediary English route `(to-verify)`. |

- Lexical checks: Harper's *cassava* entry; Wiktionary's *hurricane*, *hamaca*, and *canoa* entries. These are accessible cross-checks, not substitutes for historical dictionaries or original attestations.
- Avoid invented “first used in 1492” dates: first European encounter, first surviving manuscript token, first printed token, and first English token are different events.
- Strong material/context bridge: Cooper et al. (2016: 1055–57) describe Mona residents supplying cassava bread, cotton shirts, and hammocks to early Spanish settlements and shipping.
- That bridge supports a provisioning/contact mechanism; it does not establish the first export date of each word or make every product a voluntary commodity.
- Craft inference: an empire can export a subordinated community's food and boat words while replacing its legal language; model who keeps the word, who makes the object, and who controls trade separately.

## Toponyms and naming practices
| Name layer | Reference example | Useful inference | Limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Island name retained beside colonial name | *Hayti/Haití* beside *Hispaniola*. | Multiple naming authorities can coexist. | Do not collapse the whole island, its communities, and the later Republic of Haiti into one historical political identity. |
| Indigenous name retained in cultural usage | *Borinquen/Boriquén* alongside *Puerto Rico*. | A subordinated place-name can remain an identity resource. | Modern *Borikén* is not proof that its spelling is an unmediated pre-contact transcription. |
| Small island in a transport network | *Amona* / Mona. | Small places can carry major navigational and ritual importance. | “Amona means X” requires a separately sourced derivation `(to-verify)`. |
| Personal name | Anacaona, Caonabó; Agüeybaná in Puerto Rican history. | Maintain person, island, period, and source together. | A handful of famous names does not establish productive name morphology. |
| Office or ritual category | *cacique*; *zemí/cemí*. | A borrowed title/category need not be a personal name. | Spanish later generalized *cacique* beyond its initial Antillean context; do not back-project all those institutions. |
| Baptismal/documentary layer | Juan Mateo in Pané's account. | Names can index conversion and entry into colonial records. | A recorded Christian name does not by itself disclose the person's language, private practice, or complete identity. |

- Cooper et al. (2016: 1055) explicitly juxtapose Hayti, Borinquen, and Amona with colonial/current island names; Pané's account supplies the Juan Mateo case.
- Naming ledger: `attested form | spelling witness | referent | locality | date | proposed segmentation | confidence | later reuse`.
- Do not promote attractive translations of Jamaica, Cuba, Bahamas, or personal names into grammatical rules without an identified historical-linguistic argument.
- Fictional application: generate an endonym, a shipping-chart exonym, and a bureaucratic spelling from distinct histories; do not merely respell real Taíno sacred or personal names.

## Zemí relations: more than an “idol pantheon”
- Spellings *zemí*, *cemí*, and unaccented forms vary across languages and editions; record the witness rather than treating each spelling as a different entity.
- “Idol” is a colonial classificatory word. *Zemí* can concern a powerful being, ancestor, and its material presence; translating it simply as “statue of a god” narrows the relation.
- Oliver's (2009) **web of rulers and cemí relations** is the productive framework: persons, inherited objects, places, ritual action, and authority act together.
- Keep **being / material embodiment / custodian / site / rite / reported efficacy** as separate fields; their relation is not reducible to a modern museum accession label.
- Three-pointed stones, wooden figures, and other embodiments are materially different; neither a common outline nor “three points” proves one deity, a trinity, or a single fertility doctrine.

| Named complex | Source-grounded core | Design use | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yúcahu and Atabey | Pané reports a principal being and his mother, with variant names; cassava-related readings are prominent in subsequent interpretation. | Tie food production, origin narrative, and custodial authority together. | A neat universal “sky father / earth mother” chart or exact epithet translations exceed this evidence `(to-verify)`. |
| Guabancex | Pané, ch. XXIII, reports a storm-associated cemí and associated beings Guataubá and Coatrisquie. | Distribute destructive weather among linked agents rather than one weather switch. | Do not quietly equate her with a separate god named “Hurricane,” or make her Atabey's dark aspect without checking that interpretation `(to-verify)`. |
| Cave emergence | Pané's origin traditions locate human emergence in caves; Cooper et al. (2016: 1058) connect this testimony to cave archaeology. | Give a landscape feature genealogical significance. | Not every cave has the same named origin story or access protocol. |
| Yaya / Yayael and the gourd | Pané, chs. IX–X, relates the gourd, fish, and the origin of the sea. | Connect a mundane container, ancestral events, and the maritime environment. | Do not universalize one narrated sequence across all Antillean communities. |
| Ancestral and localized cemís | Pané names individual cemís and custodians; Oliver situates cemís in political/ritual relationships. | Make heirlooms and local places participants in legitimacy. | “One god per job” is an inadequate inventory method. |

- *Cohoba* consultation involves a psychoactive ritual practice in colonial descriptions; distinguish reported ritual procedure from modern claims about a universal shamanic religion.
- *Buhuitihu/behique* spellings concern ritual/healing specialists in different transcriptions; do not automatically assign every rite to a separate hereditary priestly caste.
- *Areíto* is useful for thinking about collective performed memory; surviving colonial descriptions are not complete transcriptions of Indigenous song or choreography.
- Thin area: exact island-by-island deity distributions, complete liturgies, specialist training, and everyday non-elite explanations cannot be reconstructed confidently from Pané alone.

## Batey: architecture, bodily performance, and authority
- Separate the plaza/court, the ballgame, and other assemblies; a shared setting does not make every event the same rite.
- Stone-lined ceremonial spaces at Caguana and Tibes in Puerto Rico are material anchors; identify a site and phase instead of designing one pan-Caribbean standard court (Rouse 1992; Oliver 2009).
- Colonial descriptions of elastic-ball play and archaeology of plazas support bodily public performance; a complete standardized rulebook or island-wide tournament calendar is not recovered here.

| Layer | Observable or reported anchor | Coupled question for design |
|---|---|---|
| Spatial | Delimited court/plaza, surrounding settlement, sometimes carved boundary stones. | Who can approach, watch, cross, or claim the center? |
| Bodily | Ball play and coordinated movement. | Which trained movements display discipline and community membership? |
| Political | Public gatherings in chiefly social settings. | How could hosting, spectatorship, and ownership of the venue display authority? |
| Symbolic | Carved imagery associated with particular ceremonial spaces. | Does the site's imagery participate in ancestry, place, or legitimacy? Label any specific reading as interpretation. |
| Oral/performed | Accounts of dancing and collective performance, distinct from a recovered ballgame text. | What is transmitted by performance rather than by an inscription? |

- Diplomacy, rivalry management, wagering, and political integration are questions to investigate; do not assert that every match adjudicated a war or land claim `(to-verify)`.
- Do not import Maya/Aztec rules, masonry rings, calendrics, or automatic sacrifice of losing players into this case by visual association.
- Stone collars and “elbow stones” require object-specific contextual interpretation; calling one a ballplayer's belt does not prove routine wear during play `(to-verify)`.
- Craft inference: couple court access, body adornment, spoken titles, and ancestor imagery; keep the sports mechanics separate from an unsupported sacrificial spectacle.

## Cave-art registers: situated visibility, not a translated script
- “Register” below is an analytical analogy for audience, material, placement, and occasion—not proof of distinct Taíno linguistic registers encoded in marks.
- Cooper et al. (2016: 1058–61) document Mona's geometric/meandering marks, anthropomorphic/animal-composite imagery, finger-fluting, water associations, and later inscriptions.
- A cave is not just an image support: darkness, fresh water, acoustics, constrained movement, surface texture, and artificial light shape the encounter.

| Analytical register | Context / technique | What the evidence supports | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|---|
| Exposed/public comparison | Court stones and other visible carvings outside caves. | Compare audience access and repeated motifs against cave assemblages. | That a motif retained one meaning in every setting. |
| Cave interior | Finger or tool marks in soft cave surfaces; images on walls, ceilings, and recesses. | Deliberate placement and bodily engagement with subterranean space. | A syllabary, literal sentences, or a universal secret initiation code. |
| Water-associated | Mona concentrations near underground fresh water. | A local relationship between marking and an essential resource. | A universal “water goddess” identification for every figure. |
| Early colonial overlay | Latin/Spanish writing and Christian marks within an Indigenous-marked cave. | Repeated visits and encounters with an existing meaningful landscape. | Peaceful conversion, identical beliefs, or Indigenous authorship of each written phrase. |

- Cave 18 is a specific case, not a representative sample of all Caribbean caves: Cooper et al. distinguish earlier Indigenous marking from later historic inscriptions by technique, placement, and superposition.
- Associated charcoal dates and pottery constrain activity; an associated date is not automatically a direct date on every drawn figure.
- Keep `site → chamber → surface → technique → superposition → associated dating → motif description → interpretation` before assigning meaning.
- Do not label every human/animal composite a named zemí, every schematic face Atabey, or every repeated shape a translated word.
- Campbell-style monomyth and Jungian archetype charts are not archaeological decoding methods; use the local context, not a universal symbol key.
- Thin area: individual authors, narrated readings, gendered access rules, and exact speech accompanying most cave images remain poorly documented `(to-verify)`.
- Craft inference: the same fictional motif can change force between a bright public court and a water-filled dark chamber without becoming a separate alphabet.

## Era model: stage changes without an extinction endpoint
- Framework: **regional sequence + interaction history**, not “primitive → classical → vanished.” Dates below are broad working brackets, not simultaneous island-wide transitions.
- Rouse (1992) supplies an influential migration/ceramic framework; use Curet (2014) to prevent archaeological categories from becoming fixed ethnic essences.

| Stage | Working bracket | Language / myth / material coupling | Staging warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Diverse earlier settlements | Millennia before European arrival. | Multiple local histories precede the late-contact labels. | Do not call all earlier cave marks or inhabitants Taíno. |
| Ceramic-age networks | First millennium BCE through first millennium CE, varying by island. | Movement of people, pots, practices, and words need not share identical boundaries. | Saladoid and related labels classify archaeological patterns, not proven spoken languages. |
| Late pre-contact formations | Especially c.1000–1492 in the core area. | Elaborate local ceremonial spaces, chiefly relations, and cemí traditions become the main comparative reference. | Neither a single founding date nor a uniform “Taíno kingdom.” |
| Early colonial transformation | From 1492, with island-specific conquest histories. | Forced labor, resettlement, conversion, new goods, and export of Indigenous products and words. | A metal fastener or cotton shirt can index contact, not “inauthentic” identity. |
| Continued adaptation and resurgence | Subsequent centuries to the present. | Surviving terms, place-names, practices, descent, and renewed identification follow different trajectories. | Word survival, uninterrupted fluency, and modern identity are different claims requiring different evidence. |

- Mona provides a local hinge: Cooper et al. (2016) document late pre-contact cave use, sixteenth-century supply networks, and Indigenous/European material encounters in the same landscape.
- Application: place a garment, loanword, sacred object, and court in a dated local sequence; reject the timeless mood board that silently combines all phases.

## Costume and material signature
- Framework: **display is not proportional to covered skin**. Labor-intensive cotton, body treatment, ornaments, and ritual furnishings can communicate distinctions without layered robes.
- Chronicles' descriptions of bodies and dress are encounters filtered through European morality, gender assumptions, and selective attention; avoid treating them as an exhaustive dress census.

| Material / practice | Grounded reference | Coupled design lever | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cotton textiles | Hammocks, bands/belts, and reported clothing; colonial Mona also produced cotton shirts. | Connect fiber labor, exchange vocabulary, household use, and changing colonial demand. | A shirt in a contact setting is not proof of identical pre-contact tailoring. |
| *Nagua* | Cotton apron/skirt associated in colonial descriptions with married women. | Test how clothing indexes life stage rather than climate alone. | Exact island, rank, length, and marriage-rule generalizations `(to-verify)`; do not impose a universal uniform. |
| Body paint and adornment | Reported painted bodies and ornaments, alongside surviving shell/bone/stone objects. | Ask which treatments are daily, ceremonial, political, or biographical. | Exact pigment recipes and fixed color/rank dictionaries are not established here `(to-verify)`. |
| Metals and valued imports | Gold and gold-copper alloy ornaments belong to the wider Antillean material record. | Separate material, shine, origin, labor, and status value. | Assay and archaeological context are needed; do not call every yellow ornament pure gold or every copper alloy locally made. |
| Wood and worked stone | Cemí embodiments, carved seats commonly called *duhos*, and court/cave imagery. | Link authority to a person-object-place ensemble, not only to a garment silhouette. | Surviving stone is overrepresented relative to perishable wood and textiles. |
| Boats and suspended bedding | Canoa/hamaca technologies, not decorative tropical accessories. | Let mobility and domestic engineering generate exportable vocabulary. | A worldbuilder should design construction constraints, not merely retain the exotic-sounding noun. |

- General material context: Rouse (1992), Oliver (2009); the cotton-shirt/provisioning example is directly checkable in Cooper et al. (2016: 1056).
- Avoid a single “Taíno costume”: identify locality, activity, age/life stage, position, and contact phase before choosing dress and ornament.
- Practical record: `fiber/material | maker/labor | garment/object term | social context | source type | local date | uncertainty`.

## What a worldbuilder can take
1. Design unequal lexical survival: technical terms and place-names can outlast institutional autonomy, while grammar, ritual vocabulary, and everyday fluency have different histories.
2. Make a staple a system: crop → processing knowledge → bread term → provisioning network → status claims and sacred narratives; do not reduce it to a food-list entry.
3. Design relational sacred objects: identify custodian, biography, place, obligations, and reported effects before assigning a pantheon category.
4. Couple performance and space: a public court and a dark cave can organize different audiences and bodily experiences without one being “secular” and the other simply “religious.”
5. Stage contact materially: changed shirts, imported beads/metal, retained place-names, and exported hammock terminology can coexist; change is not proof of cultural disappearance.
6. Hand lore-shokunin alternatives, constraints, and confidence labels—not Taíno-derived canon, invented historical translations, or decisions written into a sibling's world bible.
