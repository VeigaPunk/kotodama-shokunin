## 1. Scope and calibration

- This is a reference-and-method entry, not a fictional population, religion, or world-bible decision.
- “Malagasy” is not synonymous with “Merina.” A language-family relationship, an island-wide identity, and a particular kingdom are different scales.
- Much detail below is **locally attested** in Imerina, especially Graeber’s Arivonimamo/Betafo fieldwork in 1989–1991; it is not an island-wide rule [G].
- **Interpretive** labels identify an author’s explanation; **(to-verify)** marks material needing a more specific source or practitioner check before use.
- Broad genealogy and precise voyage histories have different confidence levels: Austronesian affiliation is secure; an exact founding crew, route, or calendar year is not established by it [A, K].

| Reference scale | Good use | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Malagasy language history | Compare inherited structure with contact layers | Do not turn linguistic descent into biological purity |
| Merina/Imerina | Study royal and ancestral authority, tombs, public oratory | Highland institutions are not all Malagasy institutions |
| Antemoro/southeastern manuscript traditions | Compare restricted Arabic-script literacy with later print literacy | Manuscript genres, ownership, and dates require individual dossiers |
| Betsileo and other regional traditions | Identify comparison questions | Their mortuary speech and clothing rules are thinly covered here (to-verify) |
| Present-day Madagascar | Observe heritage institutions and changing participation | Neither a nineteenth-century court nor an unchanged ethnographic present |

## 2. Language family, migration, and writing

**Framework: comparative reconstruction + contact stratigraphy + independent historical evidence.** Do not make the lexicon narrate more than it can support [A, K].

| Evidence | Supported inference | Unsupported shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| Malagasy’s Southeast Barito affinities within Austronesian/Malayo-Polynesian; close relationship with Ma’anyan of Borneo | A deep linguistic connection across the Indian Ocean | Present-day Ma’anyan people are an unchanged image of the migrants |
| Malay and Javanese contact evidence discussed in the migration literature | A multilingual Southeast Asian background is plausible | Every Asian-looking word arrived on the same boat |
| African/Bantu contact and African ancestry | Madagascar developed through African as well as Asian connections | Malagasy is a Bantu language, or African components are superficial additions |
| Genetic versus linguistic comparisons | Population histories and language histories can diverge | A genetic percentage determines language, social rank, dress, or belief |
| Maritime geography | Long-distance networks can carry languages, people, instruments, and crops differently | An instrument silhouette proves a particular voyage |

- Kusuma et al. found that their Malagasy samples’ Asian genetic affinities did not simply reproduce the close Ma’anyan linguistic relationship [K].
- Treat their proposed source-population scenarios as models with sampling limits, not the final settlement narrative; do not export sample proportions to every Malagasy community.
- Fictional application: maintain separate histories for inherited grammar, trade vocabulary, prestige terms, and population movement. Require a contact event before assigning a loanword layer.

| Writing regime | Historical/reference anchor | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Sorabe, Arabic-derived writing for Malagasy | Especially associated with Antemoro manuscript traditions; [D] identifies traditional history/custom texts | Script literacy can belong to specialists without being the everyday script of the whole island |
| Royal/missionary Latin standardization | Radama I’s 1823 adoption; [N] gives the alphabet without c, q, u, w, x | A court decision, schooling, and religious translation alter access to written authority together |
| Subsequent print and institutional use | Merina-based standardization must be distinguished from regional speech [A, N] | The national written norm need not erase dialect identities or older textual practices |

- Sorabe does **not** mean the Malagasy language was formerly Arabic; script ancestry and language ancestry are separate.
- Do not describe missionaries as introducing writing to an entirely unwritten island, or describe restricted manuscript literacy as universal literacy.
- Exact Sorabe manuscript hands, pronunciation rules, and specialist titles are outside this dossier’s checked detail (to-verify).

## 3. Mythic core without an invented “national pantheon”

| Category | Reference anchor | Craft distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Creator/divine designations | *Zanahary* and *Andriamanitra* occur in Malagasy religious vocabulary; [D] cross-references them | Do not force dictionary equivalents of “God” into one timeless doctrinal system |
| Ancestors and deceased persons | *Razana* can designate ancestors and the deceased/remains [D, G] | A socially consequential dead person is not automatically a named deity |
| Ancestral efficacy and standing | *Hasina*: sacred efficacy, sanctity, dignity/authority; also a ceremonial gift in relevant contexts [D, T] | Translate according to transaction and speaker, not as a universal “mana meter” |
| Prohibition | *Fady*: particular forbidden acts and relations; claims can vary between families, tombs, and speakers [D, G] | Identify who prohibits what, for whom, where, and under what circumstances |
| Heroic narrative | *Ibonia*: extraordinary birth, naming refusals, verbal contests, marriage, and royal authority [H] | An epic’s cosmology and hero are not a census of everybody’s ritual beliefs |

- Richardson’s historical *Andriamanitra* entry includes sovereigns, charms, and other extraordinary things alongside the Christian gloss “God”; its dismissive language about non-Christian users reveals missionary hierarchy, not their inferiority [D].
- Haring treats *Ibonia* as texts and performances in the plural: the familiar long Merina version must not swallow Antandroy, Tanala, Bara, Sakalava, or other variants [H].
- Haring’s **folkloric restatement** asks the reader to imagine performer, audience, and setting; it is an interpretive aid, not a recovered recording of a lost performance [H, ch. 2].
- For fiction, define the relation between creator stories, household ancestors, royal legitimacy, and performed tales before inventing divine offices.

## 4. Razana and registers of ancestral authority

**Register is a configuration of occasion, speaker entitlement, audience, and genre—not proof of a separate “ancestor language.”**

| Speech situation | Attested linguistic/social resource | What the resource does |
|---|---|---|
| Identifying the dead | Personal names, *razana*, *razambe* (“great ancestor”) [G] | Distinguishes remembered persons, prominent ancestors, and less individually remembered remains |
| Claiming a place of belonging | *Tanindrazana*, ancestral land/home territory [B via Molet; G] | Couples mobility and residence to tomb affiliations rather than equating home with current address |
| Requesting blessing | *Fangatahana tsodrano*, request for blessing; *mangataka tsodrano sy ranombavaka*, formal request expression [G, pp. 145, 163–165] | Establishes a relationship with a giver who has standing; it is not merely naming a desired result |
| Asserting seniority | *Ray aman-dreny*, literally father-and-mother, used for elders [G, pp. 157–158] | Parenthood language indexes authority, but age alone does not guarantee recognized elder status |
| Public ceremonial speech | *Kabary*, performed by *mpikabary*, using proverbs, maxims, figures, and wordplay [U] | Makes collective relationships and values publicly recognizable |
| Household narration | Stories of demanding, frightening, or protective ancestors [G] | Can differ sharply from formal speeches celebrating ancestral benevolence |

- Do not present household criticism as the one “real belief” hidden underneath empty public rhetoric; register, audience, and personal position are part of the evidence.
- Dictionary forms such as *razantsika* and *razanay* make a useful research prompt: which “our ancestors” includes the addressee? Verify inclusive/exclusive usage in the actual utterance rather than inventing ceremonial formulas [D].
- Keenan’s study concerns situated gendered speech norms, not an eternal law that Malagasy men are indirect and women direct [E].
- UNESCO describes increasing participation by women and younger orators in kabary; an older elite-male ideal is not a present-day exclusion rule [U].
- Fictional application: write one inheritance dispute in household conversation, public oratory, and a written administrative record; vary permissions and references, not just vocabulary difficulty.

## 5. Famadihana: ritual linguistics, material action, and time

- The stock English label “turning of the bones” is incomplete: historical uses include transferring remains between places of burial; rewrapping is not the only possible operation [D; G, pp. 142–143].
- Graeber describes rewrapping becoming an increasingly independent purpose around the late nineteenth century in western Imerina. This is a local historical argument, not a securely dated island-wide invention [G].
- Avoid “every seven years” as a law: schedules depend on tomb/family circumstances and ritual decisions. Dreams of cold ancestors are reported explanations, not compulsory triggers [G].

| Event position in Graeber’s account | Speech/material coupling | Fictional design question |
|---|---|---|
| Preparations | Kin are notified; funds, musicians, and shrouds are organized; government authorization also enters the process | Can absent kin contribute without attending, and who controls the list? |
| Eve of opening | Close kin call the relevant ancestors’ names, asking them to return [G, p. 144] | Who is entitled to summon, and whose name is omitted? |
| Emergence from the tomb | Bearers call the ancestor’s name as the wrapped remains emerge | Does a name attach to a person, a bundle, a tomb position, or several at once? |
| Requests for blessing | Gifts and verbal requests accompany contact with the remains | What obligation does a “gift” acknowledge or ask to release? |
| Rewrapping | New cloth, handling, binding, and differentiated resources make kin obligations material | Who supplies cloth, who handles remains, and whose advice counts? |
| Closing | Formal speeches summarize and thank; ancestral names can be recited again | Does the public account agree with what participants experienced? |

- *Mamadika* has senses including turning/reversing and betraying; *famadihana* is its nominalization. Lexical polysemy does not prove that participants understand the rite as betrayal [G, p. 142 n. 3].
- Graeber notes an active-form homophony: *mamono* “wrap” from *fono* versus *mamono* “strike/kill” from *vono*. He explicitly distinguishes the roots and does not report participants making his proposed connection [G, pp. 163–164, n. 41].
- This is a strong calibration example: an analyst can notice a language/material parallel without discovering an emic ritual explanation.
- Cloth is not scenery: *lambamena* supplies warmth/care imagery, durable enclosure, expense, and obligations to particular dead persons [G, T].
- Do not script a universal stripping of old cloth: Graeber specifically describes old wrappings remaining beneath new ones [G, p. 145].
- “Celebration, not mourning” is a voiced norm in his account, alongside tears, fear, and affection. Joy does not exclude grief [G].
- Bloch’s reconstruction of ancestral order and Graeber’s account of constraint, memory, gender, and forgetting are analytical perspectives with different field settings—not interchangeable statements of doctrine [B via G].
- A scene specification should separate **participant explanation**, **observer description**, and **interpretation**; exact liturgical wording and current access rules require local verification (to-verify).

## 6. Valiha: instrument vocabulary as a technology history

| Term or analytical label | Checked minimum | Coupling use |
|---|---|---|
| *Valiha* | Bamboo tube zither, plucked strings; [D] distinguishes construction types | Classify by resonator and string arrangement, not an exotic silhouette |
| *Valiha tory tenany* | Listed construction variety associated with the instrument’s own-body strings [D]; precise maker usage/gloss (to-verify) | Let a craft term encode how sound-producing material is made |
| *Valiha jihy vy* | Listed metal-string variety [D]; regional terminology and technical specifications (to-verify) | Imported wire can change repair work and sound without requiring replacement of the whole tradition |
| Idiochord | Analyst’s organological term: string raised from the instrument body itself | Distinguish a manufacturing relation from a Malagasy social category |
| Heterochord | Analyst’s organological term: separately supplied string material | Ask about procurement, replacement, tension, and access to materials |

- The online dictionary preserves an older English gloss “lute” beside the more precise “tube zither”; do not reproduce incompatible organological labels uncritically [D].
- Do not assign a fixed string count or tuning to all valiha: the dictionary describes substantial variety. Exact historical tuning, repertoire, and terminology remain thin here (to-verify).
- A Borneo-related language and Southeast Asian-looking instrument invite comparison, but neither demonstrates uninterrupted ritual use or a single migration episode.
- Do not assume that every kabary or famadihana is accompanied by valiha: [U] describes oratory, and [G] describes musicians without establishing a universal valiha ensemble.
- Fictional application: build a parts/materials/performers lexicon alongside the instrument’s prestige vocabulary; distinguish court fashion, household music, and contemporary heritage presentation where evidence permits.

## 7. Era staging and highland kingdom registers

**Periodize by institutions and connections, not “tribal → civilized.”** The units below are reference lenses, not a universal sequence [R; G; T; N].

| Historical layer | Language/authority relation | Material and era guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Oceanic settlement and later contacts | Inherited language and contact histories develop together; exact routes/dates remain debated [A, K] | Do not project modern ethnic borders or court dress onto initial migrants |
| Coastal/manuscript networks | Arabic-derived literacy has distinct custodians and genres [D, N] | Do not make southeast manuscript expertise simply an early version of Merina print schooling |
| Eighteenth-/nineteenth-century Merina expansion | Royal power and ranked affiliation provide settings for public authority [R, T] | Imerina’s history is not the complete history of the island |
| Nineteenth-century court, schooling, diplomacy | Latin standardization, religious translation, and international diplomacy intersect [N, T] | A court can deploy locally woven silk alongside imported objects; “traditional” and “foreign” are not separate eras |
| French colonial rule, beginning in 1896 | New administrative controls interact with older ritual authorities [R; G’s tomb-opening requirements] | Date actual regulations before borrowing them; do not back-project a colonial permit procedure into ancient rites |
| Post-independence and contemporary heritage | Oratorical participation and public framing change [U] | A heritage description is not direct evidence of medieval practice |

- *Andriana* denotes noble/royal standing in the relevant highland contexts; *hova* commonly denotes free commoner standing. Neither should become an unqualified island-wide caste label [B via Molet; G].
- Royal acknowledgment can be material as well as verbal: [D] records *hasina* presentations at the *fandroana* festival, including offerings of cattle and, in later practice, coin substitutes. This is a specific court sense, not the meaning of every use of *hasina*; the exact “unbroken coin” wording, period, officials, and accompanying formulas require checking against a clean edition (to-verify).
- Distinguish *andevo* (enslaved status) from historically changing uses of *mainty* (“black” social designations); do not reduce status histories to skin colour or translate every category into a biological population [G; R].
- Graeber’s case shows that access to public voice, ancestral land, and prestigious tombs is unequal. Ancestor idioms can sustain exclusion as well as kin solidarity.
- A “royal register” may involve who can address whom, speak for a group, distribute blessings, or claim an ancestor; it need not be a complete honorific grammar comparable to Javanese speech levels.
- Exact nineteenth-century forms of royal address, rank-specific lexical substitutions, and regional contrasts are insufficiently checked here (to-verify); do not fabricate a court phrasebook.

## 8. Costume and material signature: cloth as social action

| Item/material system | Supported function | Inference to refuse |
|---|---|---|
| *Lamba*, cloth/wrapper/mantle | Worn, gifted, invoked in speech, and used in relations between living and dead [T] | One standardized “national costume” worn identically by every group |
| *Lambamena*, often reddish-brown burial cloth with contrasting stripes | Shroud, costly obligation, and ancestor connection [T, G] | The name alone proves every shroud is red or has the same fibre composition |
| *Akotofahana*, elaborate patterned silk | Merina court/noble prestige and diplomatic gift; geometric weft-float work [T] | Every geometric motif has a recoverable religious translation |
| Cotton, raffia, bark, hemp, silk | Different materials appear in the textile record [T] | Surviving luxury silk represents everybody’s wardrobe |
| Durable tomb versus dwelling | Graeber contrasts stone tomb permanence with local mud-brick houses [G] | One construction contrast describes all regions and all centuries |

- The Smithsonian explicitly says origins and meanings of many historic akotofahana motifs are poorly known; “unknown” is better than an invented symbol dictionary [T, Queen’s Room].
- Ranavalona III’s silk gifts to Grover Cleveland in 1886 coupled textile skill, sovereignty, and diplomacy; imported sewing machines and revolvers also circulated as diplomatic objects [T].
- Haring discusses cloth and broken-thread imagery in hainteny: material vocabulary can carry relational metaphor without establishing a universal code for actual woven patterns [H, ch. 4].
- Stage a garment by fibre supply, weaving technique, patron, occasion, wearer, and afterlife; a cloth may change function as it passes from dress to gift to mortuary obligation.
- Exact sumptuary rules, colour restrictions, and period-specific cuts require garment/object dossiers rather than free inference from rank (to-verify).

## 9. Naming practices: persons, power, and remembered property

- Do not impose a universal inherited Western surname pattern. Graeber’s discussion of *anaran-dray* and *anaran-dreny* links a father’s/mother’s “name” with ancestral property while explicitly contrasting this with inherited patronymics/matronymics in his setting [G, p. 162].
- That ethnographic observation is not a complete account of contemporary civil registration, passports, or all regional naming practices; those remain (to-verify).
- In Haring’s *Ibonia*, refusing proposed names and performing self-praise establish the hero’s exceptional standing. Name acceptance can be an action in a story, not an exposition label [H, ch. 4].
- Haring relates praise-name performance to name changes at life stages; do not assume every community uses the same sequence or entitlement [H].
- In Graeber’s account, names called during famadihana connect bodily remains, memory, land obligations, and selective forgetting. Memorial practice need not preserve every ancestor’s individuality forever [G, pp. 149–150, 161–162].
- Fictional application: distinguish birth/personal name, praise or public designation, office, and remembered ancestral name as design slots—not as a claimed universal Malagasy inventory.
- Generate a fictional naming morphology in its own language; do not concatenate Malagasy name fragments or assume a familiar initial element proves noble descent.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Split migration evidence:** create separate ledgers for language inheritance, contact lexicon, people, and material techniques; let their histories intersect without making them identical.
2. **Make authority relational:** specify who may name an ancestor, request a blessing, open a tomb, supply cloth, or speak in public—and who disputes the claim.
3. **Make ritual language do work:** notifications, summons, requests, acknowledgements, and closing thanks have different audiences and consequences; do not replace them with generic mystical chanting.
4. **Give cloth a biography:** production cost → wear → gift → rewrapping obligation can couple economy, rank, naming, and memory without copying one ritual wholesale.
5. **Stage change internally:** instrument materials, scripts, public speakers, and diplomatic wardrobes can change while participants still claim continuity.
6. **Keep voices plural:** public solidarity, intimate affection, ancestral fear, and unequal access may coexist; none alone is “the culture.”
7. **Handoff, not canon:** send lore-shokunin a source-tagged option set, region/era assumptions, dependencies, and unresolved questions. Only lore determines fictional facts and world-bible consistency.

## 11. Provenance cautions and remaining gaps

- **Source status:** [K], [G]’s reprint, the cited [H] chapters, [T], [U], [N], and the named online dictionary entries were inspected. [A], [B], [E], [R], and Richardson’s edition have bibliographic verification here, not a claim that every page was newly read; detailed ritual descriptions above follow [G], not an uninspected passage of [B].
- **Colonial filtering:** missionary dictionaries and transcriptions can preserve valuable vocabulary while Christianizing divine terms, ranking beliefs, and privileging elite speakers. Dictionary date, region, and quoted authority matter.
- **Chronicle scarcity:** early migration has no voyage chronicle established in this dossier. Later chronicles, oral histories, linguistic reconstructions, and genetic models are different evidence classes; do not write reconstructed dialogues as ancient testimony.
- **Contested interpretation:** Graeber labels aspects of his reversal/violence reading as interpretations participants might reject. Do not silently convert that reading into communal doctrine or the meaning of the word *famadihana*.
- **Heritage compression:** UNESCO and museum summaries are valuable, but may compress regional differences and historical chronology; object labels are not complete legal or ritual histories.
- **Thin coverage:** modern practitioner-checked formulas, Sorabe manuscript detail, non-Merina mortuary registers, historical valiha tunings, and exact court-address etiquette require further research (to-verify).
- **Stop rule:** when a proposed word, garment rule, myth variant, or ceremony cannot be tied to a dated regional source, keep it an explicit design proposal or mark it (to-verify); never repair the gap by inventing provenance.
