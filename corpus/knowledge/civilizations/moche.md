## 1. Evidence model: do not collapse the channels

Moche is an archaeological designation for related north-coast societies, approximately the first through ninth centuries CE, not a recovered people's self-name, a securely bounded language community, or necessarily one empire (Quilter and Koons 2012).

| Channel | What it establishes relatively well | What it does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Excavated architecture and deposits | Local building sequences, associations, repeated practices | An empire-wide calendar or a complete theology |
| Fineline painting and relief | Recurrent actors, attributes, actions, and patterned relationships | Spoken wording, named gods, or literal eyewitness reportage |
| Sipán burial assemblages | Particular elite bodies buried with particular regalia | The deceased's personal name or every garment worn in life |
| Osteology at Huaca de la Luna | Violent death, processing, trauma, and deposition patterns | The victims' consent, prayers, or a unique ritual motive |
| Colonial Mochica documentation | A much later language and its recorded speakers | Direct recovery of Moche-period speech |
| Colonial coastal traditions | Later remembered origins and religious descriptions | Unchanged transmission from the Moche centuries |

- Calibration labels used here: **attested** = documented object, image, text, or physical context; **supported interpretation** = several channels converge; **contested** = plausible readings compete; **unknown** = the available channel cannot supply it.
- The labels are designer bookkeeping, not an indigenous Moche classification. Preserve locality, date, and medium with every inference.

## 2. Language family, language loss, and writing

- **Attested later language:** Mochica, also called Muchic/Muchik and, in some sources, Yunga; treated as a language isolate. The [Glottolog 5.3 Mochica record](https://glottolog.org/resource/languoid/id/moch1259), checked 2026-10-17, corroborates the isolate classification and extinct status.
- **Unknown Moche-period language assignment:** Eloranta-Barrera Virhuez (2020:36) explicitly warns that associating Mochica with ancient Moche is very hard to prove. Shared geography and similar labels are not a linguistic genealogy.
- **Yunga is an unstable label:** it can be geographical or ethnic, and in particular documents linguistic. Read Carrera's *lengua yunga* as Mochica in that source; do not turn every occurrence of “Yunga” into one language family (Eloranta-Barrera Virhuez 2020:23–35).
- **Quingnam is not a free synonym:** colonial evidence distinguishes it from Mochica, with contact and bilingualism. Projecting either language's colonial distribution backward onto every Moche valley is a hypothesis, not a settled map (2020:36).
- **No Quechua default:** an Andean location does not make a language Quechuan. Proposed distant relatives of Mochica do not override its current isolate classification.
- **Loss was not the Moche political collapse:** historical Mochica documentation continues into colonial and republican times. Declining community transmission, later rememberers, and present revitalization are different stages, not one datable disappearance event.
- **Documentation filter:** Carrera's Latin-oriented grammar and missionary translations select particular categories and religious registers. Later transcriptions differ; reconstruction of sounds and meanings is not equivalent to recovering a Moche voice.
- **Thin knowledge:** precise original pronunciation, everyday Moche vocabulary, formal addresses, and ritual chants are not supplied by the archaeological corpus. No Moche-period translation can be checked against a contemporary witness — even tying Mochica to the Moche is “very hard to prove,” and it is “impossible to establish which language was spoken by the ancient Moches” (Eloranta-Barrera Virhuez 2020: 36) — so treat any purported Moche-period translation, including attractive deity-name glosses (Gayoso Rullier 2014), as unsupported.
- **Writing status:** fineline scenes support rich visual communication, but not a generally accepted decipherment into Moche spoken sentences. Do not label a rollout a translated manuscript (Donnan 1978; Donnan and McClelland 1999).
- **Fictional application:** an invented culture may share a visual liturgy across several languages. Specify who teaches the pictures, who narrates them, and how translations vary; do not claim this multilingual arrangement is established Moche fact.

## 3. Era model: stage regions, not a single costume ladder

The ranges below are approximate design-facing orientation, not an agreed succession of dynasties. Larco's Moche I–V pottery sequence is a stylistic framework, especially useful in the south; northern sequences require their own anchors (Donnan and McClelland 1999; Quilter and Koons 2012).

| Working stage | Approximate placement | Coupling question | Anachronism guard |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early formations | c. 100–300 CE | Which inherited visual motifs and local institutions become recognizably Moche? | Do not give every early settlement the later fineline repertory |
| Middle developments | c. 300–600 CE | How do monumental centers, elite burials, and differentiated offices reinforce each other? | Date each Sipán tomb separately; “Middle” is not its precise burial date |
| Late regional transformations | c. 600–850 CE, varying locally | Which centers, painters, funerary practices, and ritual networks change or persist? | San José de Moro is not an unchanged copy of earlier Sipán |
| Later north-coast societies | After local Moche transformations | Which techniques and motifs are inherited or reworked? | Sicán/Lambayeque and Chimú evidence is not automatically Moche evidence |
| Colonial and modern records | Sixteenth century onward | Which names, words, and narratives survive through new institutions? | Carrera's grammar is not contemporary with Moche tombs |

- **Political calibration:** shared imagery can cross autonomous or competing polities; unity of style does not establish unity of government. Northern/southern divisions are useful comparisons, not proof of exactly two unchanging states.
- **Collapse calibration:** floods, drought, political conflict, population movement, and institutional reorganization require local evidence. Avoid a single El Niño that simultaneously erases language, people, religion, and every settlement (Quilter and Koons 2012).
- **Material substrate:** irrigation agriculture in desert river valleys, marine resources, cotton, adobe monuments, and specialist craft production give connected constraints, not an ecological explanation of every belief.

## 4. Mythic and pantheon core: personages before proper names

- **Attested:** repeated supernatural-looking figures, composite bodies, fangs, animal attributes, and interactions with human-looking actors. These establish a mythic repertory; they do not yield a securely named family tree of gods.
- **Analytical labels:** “Wrinkle Face,” “Bird Priest,” “Priestess,” “Decapitator,” and “Mountain God” belong to modern interpretive vocabularies. Different authors' labels need not divide the imagery identically (Donnan 1978; Gayoso Rullier 2014).
- **Major warning — Ai Apaec:** Gayoso Rullier (2014) traces *aiapæc* and *chicopæc* to colonial Christian descriptive language and challenges their reassignment as ancient divine proper names. Do not present “Ai Apaec, supreme Moche creator” as an established recovered pantheon entry.
- The article's linguistic critique does not invalidate the excavated images. It separates the visible personage from an unsupported ancient name; the person's identity and rank remain further questions.
- **Mountain relationship:** images can integrate a fanged personage with a mountain rather than merely place a humanlike god on it. “God of the Mountains” remains an analytical nickname, and Gayoso explicitly calls that designation selective and arbitrary.
- **Human/divine interface:** costume similarities between painted actors and buried elites support ritual embodiment or role enactment. They do not settle whether an actor is a god, impersonator, ancestor, officeholder, or several of these in different settings.
- **Narrative limit:** no verbatim Moche creation myth is reconstructed here. Do not import later Inca Viracocha stories or the later Lambayeque Naimlap tradition as the missing Moche scripture.
- **Framework choice:** build a personage matrix — attributes, partners, actions, places, era, exceptions — before offering domains such as fertility or sovereignty. A universal “war god / mother goddess / trickster” grid would obscure the evidence.

## 5. Fineline pottery as a narrative system

**Donnan's thematic approach:** compare recurring constellations of actors and actions across vessels, rather than assign one permanent meaning to each isolated animal or ornament (Donnan 1978). Fineline painting's stylistic development and artist attributions supply another axis of comparison (Donnan and McClelland 1999).

- **Graphic/material signature:** red-to-brown painting on cream surfaces, including stirrup-spout bottles, makes complex scenes portable; modeled and painted pottery coexist. A restricted ceramic palette does not establish a restricted spoken color vocabulary or a universal red/white cosmology.
- **Symbolic syntax:** fangs, snake-associated attributes, bird/human combinations, headdresses, and implements work in configurations. A bird-headed officiant is not interchangeable with every painted bird; distinguish depicted fauna, costume attributes, and composite personages (Donnan 1978).

| Analytical unit | Record first | Use for a fictional visual grammar |
|---|---|---|
| Personage signature | Headdress, facial treatment, belt, held objects, bodily attributes | Identify a role through redundant cues, not one emblem |
| Action | Combat, capture, procession, presentation, sacrifice, transformation | Build a limited vocabulary of recognizable events |
| Relationship | Who approaches, receives, carries, restrains, or stands above whom | Encode authority through interaction, not only crowns |
| Setting | Architecture, stepped or jagged terrain, sea, plants, animals | Give scenes contextual anchors; test whether motifs change role by setting |
| Composition | Registers, relative size, orientation, repetition, vessel surface | Permit non-linear arrangements without claiming a universal reading direction |
| Variation | Date, provenance, painterly manner, omissions and substitutions | Version the grammar across places and eras |

- **Sacrifice Ceremony / Presentation Theme:** conventional scholarly names for a complex involving distinct costumed actors, captive treatment, and a cup presentation. Interpretive reconstructions connect the cup to sacrificial blood; retain the argument, not the illusion of a translated caption (Alva and Donnan 1993; Bourget 2006).
- **Not every vessel tells this story:** hunting, fishing, erotic encounters, animals, and other subjects must not be forced into one master ritual. A theme can have variants without every variation becoming a new deity.
- **Rollout discipline:** a modern flattened drawing makes comparison possible but removes curvature, handling, and viewing conditions. Inspect photographs as well as drawings before asserting which actor is encountered first.
- **Narrative calibration:** a recurrent co-occurrence supports a conventional scene; assembling scenes into an exact mythic chronology is a stronger, contestable inference. Separate “seen together” from “therefore happens next.”
- **Local test:** use the San José de Moro fineline corpus as a dated regional comparison, not a random source of interchangeable motifs (McClelland, McClelland, and Donnan 2007).
- **Worldbuilding procedure:** design a motif inventory, three distinguishable offices, several actions, and locally meaningful settings; generate variant scenes; then check whether a reader can recognize the roles without prose labels.
- The invented count and procedure above are a craft exercise, not a claim about how Moche artists were trained. Add oral narrators and disagreeing exegeses only as explicitly fictional choices.

## 6. Warrior-priest costume: what Sipán can and cannot prove

**Alva and Donnan's evidentiary bridge:** excavated elite burial regalia resemble attributes previously recognized in ceremonial imagery. This supports an association between material office and depicted ritual actors; it is stronger than an attractive costume reconstruction based on unprovenanced pottery alone (1993).

- **Concrete material anchor:** gold and silver peanut-form beads in the Lord of Sipán's adornment bring a recognizable crop form into elite display (Alva and Donnan 1993). The physical form and material contrast are firmer evidence than a proposed sun/moon or male/female explanation of the contrast.

| Evidence cluster | Supported observation | Keep out of the inference |
|---|---|---|
| Headdress ornaments and face/ear ornaments | Elite display emphasized head and face; forms help distinguish actors | “Every large headdress means king” |
| Necklaces, pectoral elements, and metal adornments | Labor-intensive assemblages made the body a surface of rank display | One complete everyday outfit assembled from all tombs |
| Gold, silver, copper, shell, and inlays | Material contrasts and skilled workmanship were conspicuous | A proven universal gold = sun/male, silver = moon/female code |
| Weapons and martial attributes | Martial imagery could participate in elite authority | Proof that the buried individual personally fought every depicted battle |
| Placement with a buried body | A local, recoverable association between person and objects | Exact life-use sequence or a universal dressing manual |
| Recurrent painted costume bundles | Conventionally recognizable ceremonial identities | An attested indigenous title or recovered personal name |

- **“Warrior-priest” is interpretive:** useful for the coupling of martial display and ceremonial action, not a translation of a surviving Moche job title. “Priest-king” would add a further constitutional claim.
- **Do not merge tombs:** keep the Lord of Sipán, the Old Lord, and other burials' assemblages separate. A museum reconstruction can silently combine conservation, inference, and analogy; distinguish each from excavated position.
- **Garment grammar:** imagery differentiates headgear, tunic-like garments, waist coverings, belts, and ornaments. Name the observed form descriptively rather than importing later Inca garment terms as Moche words.
- **Preservation asymmetry:** surviving metal and stone do not establish that textiles, feathers, paint, or other perishable elements were unimportant. Conversely, a painted feather shape does not prove the species or manufacture of a lost object.
- **Gender and locality:** Sipán's male elites do not exhaust Moche offices. Compare the priestess-associated imagery and burials at San José de Moro without asserting identical institutions across regions and centuries (McClelland, McClelland, and Donnan 2007).
- **Specimen gate:** exact bead counts, metal compositions, headdress dimensions, textile fibers, and wearable layer orders must come from the individual object and tomb records (Alva and Donnan 1993 and the Sipán catalogues); none are supplied as invented measurements here.
- **Fictional application:** make office legible through a bundle — head treatment + fastening + emblem placement + carried implement — and decide who manufactures, stores, repairs, inherits, or removes that bundle.

## 7. Mountain sacrifice: triangulate landscape, image, and bodies

Do not conflate every “mountain-sacrifice” image, the Presentation Theme, and every excavated killing into one photographed event. They are related research problems with different evidentiary resolutions (Bourget 1998, 2006).

| Evidence or proposal | Calibration | Worldbuilding consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Captives and violent acts in mountain-associated imagery | Attested visual associations; narrative reconstruction remains interpretive | A landscape can be an actor, not a scenic backdrop |
| Sacrificial deposits at Huaca de la Luna beside Cerro Blanco | Excavated monumental/rocky setting on the north coast | Built ritual space can incorporate a natural outcrop |
| Predominantly adolescent/adult male victims in studied sacrifice contexts | Osteological assessment of particular samples | Specify who is selected; never universalize one deposit |
| Neck cuts, other trauma, and body processing | Physical evidence supports killing and differentiated treatment | Distinguish capture, death, processing, display, and burial |
| Wet depositional conditions in some episodes | Association with rain is supported locally | Weather can alter ritual circumstances without explaining all sacrifice |
| El Niño as the cause of sacrifice | A stronger causal interpretation, not established by mud alone | Separate ordinary rites from possible crisis intensification |
| Blood presentation or consumption | Iconographic/ritual interpretation; skeletal cuts alone cannot prove drinking | Do not use one evidence type to certify an entire ritual script |

- **Mountain is not altitude:** Cerro Blanco and the Huaca de la Luna setting are not evidence for later Inca-style high-altitude child *capacocha*. Different populations, periods, selection regimes, and depositional practices require different models.
- **Origin of victims is contested:** ritual combats among related groups and warfare against outsiders are competing explanations. Sutter and Cortez (2005) use comparative biological evidence to examine affiliation; that method is not a direct ethnic self-identification or a transcript of battle.
- **Do not universalize decapitation:** neck cutting, dismemberment, and other treatments need separate observations. A knife-and-head image does not establish that every sacrifice followed that exact sequence.
- **Do not invent consent:** sacrificial victims' willing participation, moral status, and spoken responses are unknown here. “Ritual” does not mean voluntary, harmless, or merely theatrical.
- **What remains thin:** precise prayers, calendar dates, indigenous explanations of mountain agency, and the relation between each painted scene and each deposit. General Andean analogies can suggest questions, not fill those gaps as facts.
- **Fictional application:** specify the causal belief separately from the physical procedure: what the mountain does, what an offering changes, who controls access, who bears the cost, and what happens when the rite appears to fail.

## 8. Naming practices: maintain four separate name layers

| Layer | Moche calibration | Safe design use |
|---|---|---|
| Ancient personal or office names | Not recovered from Sipán's regalia or fineline scenes | Generate fictional names from an explicitly invented linguistic system |
| Modern archaeological labels | “Lord of Sipán,” “Priestess,” and named personages are analytical identifiers | Use stable catalogue IDs while keeping native titles undecided |
| Historical Mochica names | Colonial anthroponyms and toponyms exist, but their etymologies vary in security | Study transmission and spelling change; do not backdate the sample automatically |
| Colonial deity-language and modern reuse | Christian descriptive terms were later reapplied to ancient images | Track translation, reinterpretation, and modern naming as separate events |

- Eloranta-Barrera Virhuez (2020:79–94) examines Naimlap/Ñaimlap and Lambayeque with explicit etymological uncertainty. The later dynastic origin account is not a list of Sipán-era rulers or a recovered Moche creation story.
- Her discussion (2020:81–83) specifically questions easy bird-based etymologies and shows why sound resemblance plus a winged mythic figure is insufficient. Do not translate every name as “water-bird,” “osprey,” or another attractive compound.
- “Huaca del Sol” and “Huaca de la Luna” are Spanish designations, not recovered Moche dedications. Their names alone cannot prove the original temples' solar/lunar theology.
- **Naming worksheet:** original attestation → recorder/date → spelling variants → proposed morphology → competing etymologies → chronological reach. If any step is missing, mark the translation (to-verify).

## 9. What a worldbuilder can take

- **Portable visual theology:** shared scenes and regalia can make religious offices recognizable without a shared script. Fictional speech, the visual code, and the oral exposition should be designed as interacting systems, not collapsed into one.
- **An office made wearable:** a ceremonial identity can reside in a coordinated assemblage rather than in one crown. Version its materials and access rules across eras; specify whether a successor inherits objects, forms, or privileges.
- **Landscape agency:** mountains, outcrops, water, and built platforms can share a ritual geography. Invent a specific relationship rather than importing a generic “Andean mountain cult.”
- **Uneven cultural continuity:** an institution can fail while populations, craft techniques, words, or ritual forms persist differently. A fictional historian should not equate political transformation with total cultural extinction.
- **Language-loss design:** let later scholars reconstruct some sounds and misread other categories; preserve the distinction between a community's modern revival and an impossible claim to unbroken ancient speech.
- **Handoff to lore-shokunin:** offer alternative language assignments, era-tagged motif/office matrices, costume assemblage rules, and confidence-tagged naming options. Lore chooses and records fictional canon; this entry does not.

## 10. Provenance cautions and stop rules

- The linguistic documentation and colonial religion vocabulary are separated from Moche archaeology by centuries. Missionary purpose and later antiquarian expectations can manufacture apparent continuity.
- Fineline objects without secure findspots cannot supply their own precise ritual, social audience, or date. Looting also destroys the costume associations most needed for reconstruction.
- Royal burial wealth overrepresents elite display; repeated images overrepresent selected themes. Neither is an adequate sample of ordinary speech, everyday dress, or all local beliefs.
- “Ai Apaec = ancient supreme creator,” a single unchanging Moche empire, and a pan-Andean costume/ritual package must not be presented as uncontested facts. Some remain popular despite serious scholarly objections.
- Full-text checks were uneven: the linguistic dissertation and Gayoso Rullier article were directly inspected; several archaeological works were checked bibliographically rather than collated page by page. Exact scene-to-plate and object-to-tomb assertions remain (to-verify) until that check is performed.
- Stop rather than fabricate when asked for a Moche personal-name list, a translated ancient prayer, a complete divine genealogy, a universal costume pattern, or an exact sacrifice calendar. Supply the evidence boundary and a separately labeled fictional design option instead.
