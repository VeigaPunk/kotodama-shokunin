## 1. Scope and calibration

- “Newar” names a historically constituted community with internal linguistic, religious, status, occupational and regional differences; it is not a synonym for every inhabitant of Nepal.
- Kathmandu, Lalitpur/Patan and Bhaktapur are related urban traditions, not interchangeable examples of one standardized city.
- The surrounding farmland, water systems, villages and trade connections are part of this urban culture's conditions, not a blank backdrop (Levy 1991, chs. 3–4).
- **Secure:** Sino-Tibetan affiliation, multiple writing practices, differentiated ritual specialists, and the institutional significance of guthi.
- **Situated:** Levy's respect-level descriptions and civic symbolism concern Bhaktapur, principally fieldwork in 1973–1976, with strong Brahman collaboration.
- **Thin here:** early vernacular speech, changing everyday garment cuts, women's institutional perspectives beyond the cited account, and present-day practice outside the valley.
- **Design inference** below means a transferable relationship, not an assertion that all Newars enact it identically.

## 2. Language family and writing: “isolate-within-region” is not isolate status

| Question | Attested base | Worldbuilding consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Nepal Bhasa/Newar, often called “Newari” in older scholarship, belongs to Sino-Tibetan; “Tibeto-Burman” is a common classificatory label | A Sanskrit-rich vocabulary does not turn it into an Indo-Aryan language |
| Internal placement | WALS uses “Himalayish”; low-level family trees are model-dependent | Do not present “Himalayish,” “Newaric,” or an isolate-like branch position as universally settled genealogy |
| Regional distinctiveness | A Sino-Tibetan vernacular has a long relationship with Sanskritic institutions and Indo-Aryan languages | Use “relatively distinct local branch/contact setting,” not “a language with no relatives” |
| Geographic isolation | Valley cities participated in agricultural and trans-Himalayan exchange networks | A mountain basin need not produce either a sealed language or an unchanging culture |
| Varieties | Levy distinguishes Bhaktapur and Kathmandu speech; Genetti's grammar concerns Dolakha | Choose a location before borrowing a form; never assemble a paradigm from unrelated descriptions |
| Language versus script | Nepal Bhasa, Sanskrit and Nepali are languages; Newa and Devanagari are scripts | A change of script is not automatically a change of language |

- Newa is a Brahmi-derived, left-to-right **abugida**, with dependent vowel signs, virama and consonant conjuncts (Unicode Consortium 2024, §13.3).
- The same standard documents Newa use for Nepal Bhasa and Sanskrit, among other languages, and predominant modern Devanagari use for Nepal Bhasa.
- Rañjanā is another important historical Newar script tradition; Levy, ch. 3, discusses its religious transmission to Tibet. Do not identify it automatically with the Unicode Newa repertoire.
- A specific inscription's hand, language, date and reading must be established separately; ornate writing is not proof of secret language.
- Borrowing method: stage inherited domestic vocabulary, learned religious terminology, trade loans and later administrative vocabulary separately; the actual etymology of any proposed example remains **(to-verify)** until checked in a linguistic source.
- Avoid a stock “Sino-Tibetan sound”: this entry supplies no verified phoneme inventory or tone system. Use Malla/Hargreaves for Kathmandu data and Genetti only for the stated Dolakha comparison.

## 3. Nepal Bhasa registers: respect is a relational system

Levy (1991, ch. 5, p. 103) describes three ordinary respect levels, realized in pronouns and a small set of salient lexical items, especially coming/going and eating/drinking verbs.

| Respect level in that account | Typical relationship/context | Coupled visible or institutional cue |
|---|---|---|
| Familiar | Intimate equals; also address toward lower-status people | Domestic intimacy and social domination can select superficially similar forms |
| Formal | Higher-status people; equals in formal situations | A meeting or public duty can alter speech without changing ancestry |
| High respect | Royalty, Brahmans, usually Buddhist priests, and deities | Sacred office and royal authority intersect with address conventions |

- This is a situated sociolinguistic account, not permission to assign every modern speaker an immutable caste-coded speech level.
- **Respect level ≠ register ≠ language:** a formal Nepal Bhasa conversation, specialized ritual vocabulary and a Sanskrit liturgy are different analytical objects.
- Levy's introduction records distinctive pronunciation, vocabulary and usage across Bhaktapur's social and geographic divisions; do not reduce variation to a single vertical ladder.
- A useful designer matrix crosses **speaker–addressee relation × occasion × audience × ritual office × intimacy**; record who can change the convention and what a misstep communicates.
- Model a priest outside ritual and the same person officiating separately; an office can matter without exhausting someone's identity.
- Tie a costume change to a speech change only where the institution warrants it: regalia may signal a role, but clothes cannot mechanically determine grammar.
- Exact pronouns, honorific verb stems, hymn diction, historical court usage and local guthi formulas are **(to-verify)**; none are invented here.
- For fictional dialogue, first define the social contrast in a gloss; create original language forms only after its phonology and grammar are specified.

## 4. Mythic and pantheon core: multiple ritual jurisdictions

| Complex | Reference anchor | Coupling to track |
|---|---|---|
| Hindu household and neighborhood worship | Śiva, Viṣṇu and Gaṇeśa appear in Levy's Bhaktapur pantheon | Domestic rites, local shrine names and routine worship |
| Royal and powerful protective deities | Taleju, Bhairava and goddess complexes; Levy, chs. 8–9, 14–15 | Palace authority, initiation, sacrifice, public appearances and protected access |
| Newar Buddhist institutions | Levy, ch. 5, distinguishes Śākya and Vajrācārya groups and priestly initiation | Households, hereditary affiliation and specialist ritual competence; not a generic Tibetan monastery model |
| Buddhist image worlds | Buddha preaching; Chakrasamvara/Vajravarahi mandala; Vasudhara, in the cited museum records | Teaching, meditation, offerings, prosperity and the material work of image-making |
| Household renewal | Mha Puja in the Swanti sequence; Levy, ch. 13 | Body/self, household membership, senior women's ritual agency and annual time |

- Hindu and Buddhist traditions have substantial interaction without becoming one undifferentiated pantheon; ask whose rite, priest and explanation a particular scene uses.
- Reject the colonial claim, reproduced historically in Levy's discussion, that Nepalese Buddhism is merely a “branch of Hinduism.” Shared forms do not settle religious identity.
- Taleju's status as a Malla lineage goddess and civic focus shows how a deity can retain political significance after the dynasty loses power (Levy, ch. 8).
- **Mythic-origin lead (to-verify against a named Svayambhūpurāṇa recension):** Mañjuśrī opens the valley lake to make it habitable. Do not turn a familiar retelling into a dated settlement chronicle.
- Levy, ch. 7, independently records a Kathmandu sword-shape/founder association with Mañjuśrī; that does not verify every version of the lake narrative.
- Do not make “Hindu = public” and “Buddhist = private,” or “Tantric = sinister,” into organizing laws.

## 5. Symbolic system: map relations, not an emblem dictionary

- Levy's **mesocosm** is an analytical model of a city-scale symbolic world between individual life and encompassing cosmos; it is not an indigenous name for all Newar culture.
- His account links neighborhood, lineage, city and household through different shrines and ritual scales; boundaries and centers matter in particular contexts (chs. 7–9).
- Bhaktapur's peripheral goddess shrines and central Tripurasundari provide a situated spatial configuration, not a universal blueprint for every settlement.
- A mandala painting, a ritual floor diagram and an interpretation of urban space are related forms, not automatically the same diagram reproduced at different sizes.
- The museum's Chakrasamvara mandala description explains a palace-like abode, gates and a cremation-ground ring in a meditational setting; it is not simply a decorative town plan.
- A deity may have a Sanskrit name, a local Nepal Bhasa name and a shrine-specific manifestation: classify the relation instead of multiplying gods whenever a name changes.
- In Levy's Mha Puja account, a floor mandala corresponds to each household member; a body/self can therefore become a ritual focus without being a royal emblem.
- Design check: for every symbol specify **user, medium, location, occasion, authorized reading and possible alternative reading**.
- Do not assign all red borders, lotus forms, swords or serpents a single Nepal-wide meaning. Object context and named testimony outrank free association.

## 6. Guthi: association, endowment, obligation

Levy (1991, ch. 6, pp. 144–145) describes special-purpose associations, some supported by designated agricultural land and some by member payments and fines. “Community trust” is a useful partial gloss, not a complete legal translation.

| Institutional component | Attested range in that account | Designer's question |
|---|---|---|
| Resource base | Assigned land yield, income, member levies, fines | What keeps the recurring obligation funded after its founder dies? |
| Purpose | Shrines/buildings, public ceremonies, water provision or maintenance, particular forms of worship | What exact service is owed, to whom, and when? |
| Membership | Some associations follow a thar/profession; others include multiple thar groups | Who qualifies, who is excluded, and who supplies work without equal authority? |
| Organization | Leaders, rules, tutelary deity and periodic feasts | Who accounts for resources and sanctions a default? |
| Funeral provision | Death guthi are especially persistent in Levy's description | How are urgent duties fulfilled when a household cannot act alone? |
| Change | Government administration and newer associations compete with or replace some functions | Which obligation survives when its revenue or membership changes? |

- Keep **guthi as a corporate association**, **guthi land as a tenure category**, and a particular public administrative body analytically separate.
- Not every guthi is a craft guild, monastery, charitable foundation, municipal agency or democratic commons; choose the actual institution before using an analogy.
- Neither *thar* affiliation nor *phuki* kin grouping is a synonym for guthi membership. Institutions can intersect without being identical.
- Coupling chain for fiction: **endowment/dues → recurring labor and feast → calendrical obligation → procession or maintenance → public recognition and disputes**.
- Name resources and unpaid work as carefully as spectacular ceremonies. A city cannot sustain ritual density on beautiful costumes alone.
- Do not romanticize exclusion or caste hierarchy as necessary social harmony. Specify whose authority the record expresses and whose burden is less visible.
- Claims about current land law, present membership rules or a specific guthi's constitutional history remain **(to-verify)**; this is not a legal reference.

## 7. Era model: parallel tracks rather than one timeless “medieval Nepal”

| Staging band | Evidence and institutional emphasis | Costume/language guard |
|---|---|---|
| Early inscriptional/Licchavi horizon | Levy, ch. 3, discusses inscriptions and Sanskritic courts | Later chronicles and later paintings do not directly record early everyday dress or spoken registers |
| Post-Licchavi and early manuscript horizons | The Newa script history reaches into the first millennium; surviving manuscripts provide object-specific anchors | Date language, script and illustration separately; do not infer all vernacular use from a Sanskrit manuscript |
| Malla courts and urban competition, broadly 13th–18th centuries | Court patronage, local institutions and royal ritual; the cited cloth paintings supply late-1400s and 1648 anchors | One painting is not a complete wardrobe, and elite donors are not the whole population |
| Gorkhali/Shah conquest, 1768–1769, and afterward | Levy, ch. 3, distinguishes conquest from the survival and modification of local structures | A political rupture need not instantly erase local ritual, speech or craft practice |
| Rana-era and 20th-century transitions | Language/script constraints, administrative changes and later ethnography belong to distinct documentary horizons | Do not project a photographed or remembered ensemble into every earlier century |
| Contemporary performance and heritage | Requires present-day evidence, not extrapolation from the 1970s | Revival, adaptation, ordinary wear and staged identity are different uses, none automatically inauthentic |

- Use separate tracks for **political sovereignty, language prestige, script practice, ritual organization, garment silhouette and textile supply**.
- Their change-points need not coincide. A dynasty, a trust's property and a ritual costume can each outlast another's replacement.
- Dates of chronicles, the events they narrate, a surviving manuscript copy and its illustrations are separate dates.
- The earlier the vernacular or clothing claim, the greater this entry's evidential thinness; flag gaps rather than supplying a seamless dynastic wardrobe.

## 8. Paubha, costume and material signature: an evidence protocol

- Treat **paubha** as a Newar devotional painting tradition, not “a photograph of ancient Nepal” or a synonym for every Himalayan painting.
- Use object-specific cataloguing: a museum may call a work “painting,” “mandala,” “thangka” or another term; retain that label before assigning a comparative tradition.
- Secure material anchors: *Bejeweled Buddha Preaching* (1648) is catalogued as gum tempera, ink and gold on cloth; the late-1400s Chakrasamvara mandala as gum tempera on cloth (Cleveland Museum of Art, cited records).
- Vasudhara 1947.493 is catalogued as gilt bronze and semiprecious stones, with abundance-associated attributes; divine ornament and a metal image are not direct evidence of lay jewelry ownership.

| Evidence layer | What to record | What it cannot establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Divine figures | Crown, ornaments, drapery, attributes, posture and iconographic role | Everyday local dress, fabric actually worn, or universal color rules |
| Human donor or ritual registers, where identified | Date, inscription, figure identity, garment closure, hem, drape, headwear and accessories | Population-wide prevalence or a garment's original invention date |
| Processional embodiment | Mask/regalia, performer, makers, custodians, repair and activation/use context | That an ensemble is ordinary clothing, or freely available to everyone |
| Chronicle dress regulations | Wording, attributed ruler, compilation date and corroboration | Actual compliance across all neighborhoods and periods |
| Photographs/current ensembles | Date, place, wearer, task and whether staged | An unchanged Malla or Licchavi wardrobe |

- The dated paintings above are **catalogue anchors**, not an image-based reconstruction completed here; no precise donor garment cutting pattern is claimed from them.
- Levy, chs. 5 and 15, links painters/mask makers and deity-possessed Nine Durgas performers to differentiated social roles: making, keeping and wearing ritual equipment are not the same office.
- His ch. 5, p. 103, reports chronicle-attributed restrictions on caps, shoes, gold ornaments and jackets for particular subordinated groups; treat them as mediated, coercive status claims, not a neutral costume chart.
- Modern garment leads — **hāku patāsi**, commonly described as a black sari with a red border, and **tapālan**, a men's upper garment — are **(to-verify)** here for community-specific terminology, construction and earliest secure attestation.
- Do not back-date a modern heritage ensemble, or treat a Nepal-wide national costume as the uniform of every Newar era and status group.
- Extraction sheet: **object ID → inscription/date basis → human/divine/performer → visible feature → functional hypothesis → corroborating source → confidence**.
- Distinguish “painted as gold” from gold thread, dyed yellow, gilt metal or actual ownership of precious materials; medium and represented substance differ.
- Fictional application: let ritual regalia preserve an older silhouette while work clothes change, but supply an institution that stores, repairs and authorizes the regalia.

## 9. Jatra density: calendars are a labor-and-route system

- *Jatra/yatra* names festival/processional practices, but not every calendrical observance is a street procession.
- Levy (1991, chs. 12–16) distinguishes predominantly lunar scheduling from the solar Biska/Bisket sequence; do not flatten the year into twelve Gregorian festival dates.
- Lunar fortnight, lunar day (*tithi*), solar date and agricultural season are different coordinates; astronomical dates need a local almanac and year-specific conversion.
- His catalogue explicitly discusses inclusion and event-splitting decisions. Avoid unsupported slogans such as “a festival every day” or a supposedly canonical festival count.

| Situated example | Calendar/social scale | Coupled system |
|---|---|---|
| Biska/Bisket, Bhaktapur | Solar New Year sequence | Bhairava/Bhadrakali, chariots, city divisions, routes and negotiated public order |
| Mohani, Bhaktapur | Autumn goddess/harvest sequence | Royal/temple institutions, agricultural time and the Nine Durgas cycle |
| Swanti and Mha Puja | Lunar New Year and household renewal | Body/self, household membership, offerings, feasting and senior women's ritual action |
| Sithi Nakha in Levy's account | Seasonal threshold toward the rains | Household/courtyard deity relations and the changing agricultural year |

- Density comes from **overlapping households, lineages, neighborhoods, associations and city-scale obligations**, not simply a long list of gods.
- For each event assign funder, preparatory work, storage, route, authorized participants, food, music, dress, speech and aftermath; distinguish attested features from design choices.
- Test collisions: agricultural labor, funerals, garment repair, shared streets and the same specialists cannot all be infinitely available.
- Compare other cities only through their own sources; a Patan or Kathmandu calendar must not be filled with renamed Bhaktapur events.

## 10. Naming practices: keep persons, groups, offices and sacred aliases apart

- A working record separates **given name, family/thar name, kin affiliation, occupation, ritual office, honorific and name used in address**.
- Levy, ch. 5, supplies Śākya, Vajrācārya and Rājopādhyāya as situated group names; they are not interchangeable personal-name suffixes or complete descriptions of a living person's occupation.
- He also notes city-specific usage around Śreṣṭha/Sesya labels; do not make a surname-to-rank decoder valid everywhere.
- *Phuki* refers to a kin/ritual grouping in his account, not simply everyone with the same surname.
- Sacred alias example: Bhairava/Bhaila Dya in Levy, ch. 8. Track language, shrine and manifestation before deciding whether two names denote the same referent.
- Place-name example: Bhaktapur/Khopa in Levy, ch. 3; preserve local and Sanskritic naming layers without repeating a proposed etymology as settled fact.
- Levy, ch. 6, pp. 115–116, records spousal name avoidance and circumlocution in strict upper-status households; this is a situated address practice, not a universal ban on given names.
- A fictional naming grammar can therefore distinguish written affiliation from spoken relationship. Any real-world syllables, gender patterns or derivational endings need **(to-verify)** dictionary/grammar evidence before reuse.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take

- **An enduring coupling, not a costume pack:** a distinctive vernacular can coexist with transregional sacred vocabularies, mobile craftspeople and several writing practices.
- **Institutional memory:** recurring obligations and resource arrangements can preserve ritual names, specialist knowledge and regalia across political change.
- **Register as social action:** the same participants may speak differently at home, in an association's meeting and before a deity; define the relations, not a fixed “politeness personality.”
- **Calendar as infrastructure:** derive events from institutions, routes, seasons and household obligations before decorating them with symbols.
- **Visual time-depth:** give workwear, ceremonial equipment and sacred images different replacement rates; identify who pays for their continuity.
- **Plural explanations:** keep practitioner accounts, court legitimations, local histories and external scholarship distinct; no single narrator owns every meaning.
- Send lore-shokunin a reference packet containing **source, city/community, period, observed relationship, confidence, fictional transformation and unresolved question**. These are design options, never canon assertions.

## 12. Provenance cautions and explicit limits

- Levy's primary fieldwork and collaborators make the account unusually detailed but not socially exhaustive; his city-integration model is an interpretation, not a consensus description of all Newar life.
- His text includes obsolete racial categories and speculative early population narratives. Such categories are discredited as explanations linking language, ancestry and culture; do not reuse them as factual typology.
- Colonial observers and court-centered chronicles can preserve observations while also reproducing elite, patriarchal, religious or administrative prejudice; attribute statements and seek independent corroboration.
- Chronicle evidence is especially inadequate for uninterrupted early costume sequences, exact origins of institutions, and the speech of people who left few records. A famous ruler's attributed reform is not proof that a practice began with him.
- Museum attribution is not infallible: [Cleveland 1991.1081](https://www.clevelandart.org/art/1991.1081) is dated “1600s” in its record while its description associates it with Gorkha rule; resolving that chronological tension is **(to-verify)**, not a license to date donor clothes from the description.
- No direct garment-image analysis, full grammar collation, critical Svayambhū text comparison, current guthi legal survey or contemporary community validation was completed here. The inline **(to-verify)** flags preserve those limits.
- Do not copy restricted ritual procedures or claim authority to perform them. Transfer institutional and symbolic relationships into original fiction, credit the reference tradition, and leave world-bible decisions to lore-shokunin.
