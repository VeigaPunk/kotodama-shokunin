## Evidence model: distinguish a witnessed form from its interpretation

- **M — medieval anchor:** dated inscription, poem, excavated context, or dated monument; composition date, surviving-copy date, and restoration date are different fields.
- **L — later documented practice:** courtly, village, religious, or performance evidence from a specified later period/locality; useful comparison, not automatic medieval survival.
- **I — interpretation:** a scholar's explanation of ritual function, character identity, or symbolic meaning; preserve attribution and competing readings.
- **D — design inference:** a fictional mechanism abstracted from the evidence; never present it as a Javanese fact.

| Coupling mechanism | Evidence-bearing relation | Productive design question |
|---|---|---|
| Prestige language ↔ ritual authority | Sanskrit vocabulary and Old Javanese literary expertise mediate kingship and sacred narrative | Who can compose, translate, or challenge an authoritative utterance? |
| Myth ↔ performance ↔ social voice | A dalang can move between epic characters, poetic passages, and comic commentary | Which voices may criticize power under a performance license? |
| Weapon ↔ ancestry ↔ costume | A keris can be worn, inherited, maintained, named, and venerated | Does legitimate possession require genealogy, care, office, or public recognition? |
| Narrative ↔ architecture ↔ movement | Relief sequences and sacred spaces organize encounters with stories | What changes when a story is approached through a gate rather than read on a page? |

## Era staging: Java is not one timeless “Hindu kingdom”

| Stage | Useful anchors | Do not import without separate evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Central Javanese monumental florescence, broadly eighth–tenth centuries | Borobudur and Prambanan; Buddhist and Śaiva monumental patronage | Calling these Majapahit foundations; assuming their plans characterize every later sanctuary |
| Earlier East Javanese courts, tenth–thirteenth centuries | Literary and political settings preceding Majapahit, including Kadiri and Singhasari | Treating every Old Javanese poem as composed at the Majapahit court |
| Majapahit foundation and fourteenth-century court | Conventional foundation 1293; Hayam Wuruk/Rājasanagara; Prapañca's 1365 poem | A modern nation-state map inferred directly from a court poem's territorial claims |
| Fifteenth–early sixteenth-century transformations | Continuing East Javanese patronage alongside dynastic conflict and expanding Muslim coastal networks | One instantaneous island-wide collapse, conversion, or replacement of all earlier culture |
| Later Javanese courts and communities | Mataram-successor court traditions; rich later evidence for speech etiquette, macapat, dress, and wayang | Projecting a modern Surakarta/Yogyakarta ensemble unchanged into 1365 |
| Balinese transmission and later reception | Copying, performing, and interpreting Old Javanese works in changing Balinese settings | Treating Bali as a frozen medieval Java or a manuscript's copying date as its composition date |

- Use Robson (1995), Zoetmulder (1974), Kinney et al. (2003), and Kieven (2013) together: political, literary, and architectural periodizations do not align exactly.
- The *Deśawarṇana* is exceptionally rich but court-centered and encomiastic: claims of subordination are not uniform evidence of taxation, administration, or everyday identity.
- A single “last date” for Majapahit depends on the event adopted: later Javanese tradition dates the fall to 1478 (śaka 1400), but the Pëtak and Trailokyapuri charters of 1486 and Tomé Pires (1513) show Hindu-Javanese kings still reigning after the dynastic war in the capital, and the literature places the final end c. 1527 (Noorduyn 1978; Herho et al. 2023). Do not offer one collapse date without naming the event and its source.

## Language family, Kawi register, and writing

- Javanese is **Austronesian, Malayo-Polynesian**; substantial Sanskrit borrowing does not make Old Javanese an Indo-European language or merely “corrupt Sanskrit” (Zoetmulder 1974).
- **Old Javanese** is a historical language/literary corpus; **Kawi** can designate that literary language, a learned poetic vocabulary in later usage, or a historical script. Label which sense is intended.
- Sanskrit-derived lexicon supplies resources for courtly, religious, aesthetic, and technical expression; it coexists with Javanese grammar and inherited vocabulary rather than replacing them wholesale.
- Kawi script is a **Brahmi-derived abugida**, written left to right: consonant signs carry an inherent vowel, modified or suppressed by additional signs; conjunct mechanisms handle clusters (Unicode Consortium 2022, §17.9).
- Kawi was used for more than Old Javanese, including Sanskrit, Old Malay, Old Balinese, and Old Sundanese; language identity cannot be read from script identity alone.
- Letterforms vary across centuries, region, and support. A Unicode font supports encoding; it does not certify a glyph style as fourteenth-century Majapahit.
- Distinguish inscriptional stone/copper from manuscript supports and later copies; surviving literature owes much to transmission outside its original court setting (Zoetmulder 1974; Christie 1993).

| Register problem | Attested resource or distinction | Fictional adaptation, explicitly D |
|---|---|---|
| An ancient text must remain authoritative | Learned Kawi vocabulary and inherited verse can differ from surrounding speech | Preserve archaic lexical layers, then decide who explains them to listeners |
| Rank changes an encounter | Later Javanese etiquette includes ngoko/krama resources and honorific choices (Errington 1988) | Model speaker–addressee–referent relationships, not a fixed “noble language” spoken by one caste |
| A performer speaks for several beings | Narrative, dialogue, song, and comic voice can differ within one event (Keeler 1987) | Assign code-switch triggers by role, scene, and audience, not just birth rank |
| Orthography appears more conservative than speech | Literary spelling and manuscript transmission are not phonetic field recordings | Keep historical spelling, current pronunciation, and ritual recitation as separate specifications |

- **Calibration:** the full later ngoko/krama system cannot be reconstructed for Majapahit merely by citing a modern etiquette study; street speech and regional social registers are thinly evidenced here.

## Tembang and poetic registers: form is not the same as politeness level

| Term/tradition | Formal or performance principle | Historical control |
|---|---|---|
| **kakawin** | Old Javanese poetry using Sanskrit-derived quantitative metrical models; syllable weight matters | Major compositions predate and accompany Majapahit; do not equate “kakawin” with one dynasty |
| **kidung** | Javanese metrical song/poetry associated with a different literary and metrical tradition, often labeled Middle Javanese | “Middle” is not a clean date stamp; genre, language, and manuscript history need separate dating |
| **tembang / sekar** | Terms for song/poetic singing in Javanese usage; textual interpretation occurs through performance | A broad practice/category, not one meter or a synonym for Sanskrit |
| **macapat** | Later Javanese verse traditions regulate stanza lines (**guru gatra**), syllable counts (**guru wilangan**), and line-final vowels (**guru lagu**) | Origins are not securely traced: Purbatjaraka links macapat to Middle Javanese verse, Pigeaud places early written use at the beginning of the Islamic period, and estimates run from a traditional 1279 attribution to a 1541 anchor; stanza inventories vary by authority (8–15), so use named traditions, not one medieval canon (Sahlan and Mulyono 2012) |
| **sulukan** in wayang | Dalang's sung passages help frame atmosphere and dramatic transition | A performance category; not interchangeable with every literary meter |

- Genre distinctions: Zoetmulder (1974); performed textuality: Arps (1992); wayang context: Keeler (1987).
- Names such as **Dhandhanggula** and **Pangkur** identify particular macapat forms; names, melodic realization, and associated affect require tradition-specific checking before imitation.
- Do not treat a popular “human life stages” ordering of macapat as proof of its original creation or one timeless Javanese doctrine.
- **D workflow:** choose verse constraints → trained singers and copying institutions → occasions and audiences → permitted paraphrase → register shifts when an archaic verse is glossed. Do not add ornament without deciding who understands it.

## Mythic and pantheon core: adaptation rather than a single imported package

| Narrative/religious layer | Named anchors | Coupling and calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Śaiva cult and iconography | Śiva/Siwa, Durgā, Gaṇeśa; related local titles and representations | Identify deity through attributes, inscription, and setting; do not infer a complete theology from one image |
| Buddhist establishments and texts | Buddhas, bodhisattvas, and Buddhist literary production | Co-presence with Śaiva institutions is not proof that every practitioner held an identical combined creed |
| Epic reworking | Rāma/Rama, Arjuna, Bhīma/Bima, Kṛṣṇa/Kresna; *Rāmāyaṇa* and *Mahābhārata* narrative resources | Javanese works and performances select, expand, and reinterpret; an Indian summary does not establish a local episode |
| Courtly ethical-religious poetry | Mpu Tantular's *Sutasoma* | Its Śiva–Buddha formulations need textual context; later national uses of a phrase are not medieval nationalism |
| Panji narrative worlds | Prince Panji and associated quest/romance traditions | Distinguish these from Indic epic cycles; “cap-wearing male” is not alone a conclusive Panji identification |
| Place, ancestry, and agrarian sacrality | Sacred landscapes and ancestors alongside textual religious institutions | Do not label every local being a subsidiary Hindu god; later rice-goddess identifications require their own evidence |

- Literary basis: Zoetmulder (1974); architectural/iconographic basis: Kinney et al. (2003); cap-figure readings: Kieven (2013).
- **I:** “Śiva–Buddha” is useful shorthand for particular connections, not a sufficient population-wide religion label. Specify court, foundation, text, and ritual before describing synthesis.
- **D:** make religious plurality institutional: different custodians, endowments, teaching lineages, ritual expertise, and access rights can overlap without merging into a tidy universal pantheon.

## Wayang kulit: myth becomes an event, not only a story

- **wayang** is a wider performance field; **wayang kulit** specifies leather shadow puppetry. **wayang purwa** commonly concerns older epic repertoires in Javanese usage; these labels are not interchangeable with every regional wayang form.
- The **dalang** manipulates puppets and supplies differentiated voices, narration, and song in relation to musicians, patrons, and audiences; authorship is distributed, even when the dalang is central (Keeler 1987; UNESCO 2008).

| Element | Working vocabulary | What it couples |
|---|---|---|
| Light and screen | **kelir**, screen; **blencong**, traditional lamp | Material visibility, shadow, and the staging of a narrated world; modern lighting may differ |
| Musical environment | **gamelan**, ensemble; **sulukan**, dalang's singing | Timing, atmosphere, character entrance, and the social organization of performance |
| Play/story realization | **lakon** | A named narrative is realized through choices, not reproduced as one immutable script |
| Comic mediation | **punakawan**; Semar and, in familiar later Central Javanese sets, Gareng, Petruk, Bagong | Social commentary and vernacular address within epic worlds; membership varies across traditions |
| Character coding | Refined/coarse contrasts, posture, eyes, voice, dress, gesture | Several sign systems converge; never infer an invariant moral alignment from one color |
| Opening/transition figure | **gunungan / kayon**, tree/mountain-shaped puppet in familiar traditions | Boundary and transition work; individual cosmological readings need a named interpretation |

- Do not attribute the full modern puppet silhouette, comic cast, all-night sequence, or musical ensemble unchanged to Majapahit. Medieval performance references do not establish every later technical detail.
- **D:** design a performance grammar: who sponsors it, who can interrupt it, how a sacred character speaks, and which comic utterances remain deniable. Costume and language can mark roles without making social order uncontested.
- UNESCO's general overview is useful for safeguarding scope but groups unlike puppet forms; its wording must not replace a specialist regional typology.

## Keris pusaka: an heirloom vocabulary, not a generic enchanted sword

| Term | Usable sense, mainly L unless independently dated | Coupled design variable |
|---|---|---|
| **keris / kris** | Asymmetrical dagger; blades may be straight or waved | Weapon, dress accessory, rank display, and ritual object can coincide |
| **pusaka** | Valued inherited/sanctified heirloom; not a word restricted to keris | Custody, descent, office, and obligations of preservation |
| **empu / mpu** | Honorific associated with mastery; **empu** is used for a keris smith | Specialist authority; the same title elsewhere does not prove an individual was a smith |
| **wilah** | Blade | Separate an inherited blade's history from replaceable fittings |
| **dhapur / dapur; luk** | Blade form/design; undulations on a waved blade | An object taxonomy and its social interpreters; no requirement that every blade be waved |
| **pamor** | Visible contrasting metal pattern and associated classifications | Material process plus connoisseurship; an auspicious interpretation is not a laboratory result |
| **warangka / wrangka; pendok** | Sheath; metal sheath covering/fitting | Dress silhouette, materials, maintenance, and changes of presentation |
| **tangguh** | Connoisseurial attribution of age/origin/style | A situated expert judgment, not an excavated date or proof of royal ownership |

- Main vocabulary/reference: Solyom and Solyom (1978); UNESCO (2008) supplies a contemporary heritage synopsis. Classifications and spellings vary regionally.
- **Evidence discipline:** a blade, hilt, sheath, and claimed genealogy may have different dates. “Majapahit keris” in a sales description is not archaeological provenance.
- The heritage synopsis says blades are made “in layers of different iron ores and meteorite nickel”; do **not** generalize that material claim to every keris or infer meteorite origin from pamor alone. A 2025 study of five nickel-rich krises found only one with a possible Prambanan meteoritic admixture, and high nickel and cobalt content by itself did not imply meteoritic metal (Brandstätter et al. 2025): treat meteorite material as an object-specific analytical finding.
- In later court contexts, heirlooms can receive honorific names such as **Kyai**; that title also has human uses. A named object is not automatically a literal inhabited spirit or an independent deity.
- **D:** specify acquisition → recognition → inheritance → maintenance → alienation disputes. Supernatural efficacy can remain a character's belief unless lore-shokunin explicitly chooses otherwise.

## Candi and symbolic space: architecture is another narrative medium

| Term/form | Working sense | Avoid this shortcut |
|---|---|---|
| **candi** | Conventional term for ancient sacred monuments; investigate each site's cult and use | “All candi are royal tombs containing ashes” — the generic tomb model is rejected by Soekmono (1995) |
| **candi bentar** | Split gateway, recognizable at Wringin Lawang in the Trowulan area | Assuming every modern split gate has identical medieval meaning |
| **paduraksa** | Roofed gateway; Bajang Ratu is a useful Trowulan comparison | Treating roofed and split gates as synonyms or claiming their patrons from form alone |
| **pendapa / pendopo** | Pavilion term useful in architectural description | Reconstructing a lost roof or exact ceremony from a stone base alone |
| **petirtaan** | Sacred bathing/water installation | Reducing water infrastructure either to plumbing alone or to a universal “purification cult” |
| **kāla / kala** | Fierce mask/face motif often marking an architectural threshold | Reading every mask as a portrait of one demon with one fixed story |
| **makara** | Composite aquatic creature used in architectural ornament | Assuming every East Javanese doorway preserves the same kala–makara arrangement |
| **liṅga–yoni** | Śaiva cultic forms, identified in ritual and architectural context | Exhausting their meaning with a generic “fertility symbol” label |
| Relief sequence | Episodes distributed across built surfaces and viewing routes | Assuming literary episode order, clockwise reading, or one interpretation without checking the monument |

- Anchors: Kinney et al. (2003), Soekmono (1995), Kieven (2013). Present-day descriptive terms need not be the original builders' terminology.
- Red brick is prominent at Trowulan, but East Javanese sacred architecture also uses stone; Penataran/Panataran is a layered sanctuary, not a uniform single-date “Majapahit style.”
- **I:** Kieven's cap-figure study offers readings of guidance and religious progression through temple space; these are argued interpretations, not inscriptions labeling every figure's function.
- **D:** couple access vocabulary to gate form, dress requirements, purification, route, and narrative knowledge. Decide which signs an initiate understands that a visitor only sees.

## Costume and material signature: reconstruct ensembles, not a mood board

| Evidence/material | What it can support | What it cannot establish unaided |
|---|---|---|
| Reliefs and sculpture | Wrapped lower-body garments, drapery, belts/sashes, jewelry, and headgear in represented roles | Everyday dress across classes; exact dyes, fiber, or the absence of garments not shown |
| Patterned cloth in images | Motif placement, visual density, status differentiation within the image | Batik technique rather than weaving, painting, or another pattern-making process |
| Charter textile gifts | Textile valuation and differentiated social/ritual exchange (Christie 1993) | A complete Majapahit wardrobe; much of the article's securely dated material is earlier |
| Gold ornaments, terracotta, brick, stone | Contrasts among portable prestige, modeled objects, and durable sacred construction | One material assigned to an entire class, religion, or era without context |
| Later court dress and keris fittings | Concrete models of ensemble, comportment, status, and inherited objects | A medieval uniform of kebaya, beskap, blangkon, or modern court batik restrictions |

- **makuṭa / makuta** supplies a crown/headgear lexical anchor; **wastra** a Sanskrit-derived cloth/garment term. Verify each historical sense in Zoetmulder and Robson (1982), not a modern costume-shop glossary.
- Use **kain** cautiously as a modern general cloth label; do not let one convenient noun erase older terms or differences among wrap, sash, headcloth, and shoulder covering.
- **D worksheet:** support and climate → fiber/production/trade → cut or wrapping operation → fastening → body coverage → rank/occasion → permitted ornament → weapon carriage → spoken address appropriate to that encounter.
- A refined puppet's silhouette is evidence for a theatrical code, not proof that historical nobles always dressed or behaved that way.

## Naming practices: separate person, office, regnal style, and textual persona

| Layer | Grounded example/resource | Adaptation control |
|---|---|---|
| Personal/regnal reference | Hayam Wuruk and Rājasanagara refer to the same fourteenth-century ruler | Store aliases and contexts; do not generate two people from two source names |
| Sovereign titulature | **Śrī Mahārāja** and extended Sanskritic styles | A title is not a hereditary surname; verify the actual inscriptional string before quotation |
| Territorial/dynastic designation | **Bhre** plus a place-associated designation in Majapahit references | Office, relation, place, and individual must not collapse into a single “family name” field |
| Learned attribution | **Mpu Prapañca**, **Mpu Tantular** | Mpu is not a surname; literary attribution or persona does not guarantee a recoverable birth identity |
| Divine names across media | Śiva/Siwa; Kṛṣṇa/Kresna; related local titles | Preserve source-language and performance variants instead of treating all variation as spelling error |
| Named heirloom | Later honorific naming of pusaka | Give objects a separate naming/custody system; do not automatically share human descent rules |

- Medieval anchors: Robson (1995), Zoetmulder (1974); lexical checks: Zoetmulder and Robson (1982); later object comparison: Solyom and Solyom (1978).
- Spellings with **ś/s**, **v/w**, **ṛ/r**, or omitted diacritics may reflect transliteration policy, adaptation, or different source forms; decide which before positing a sound change.
- Do not infer the complete naming practice of villagers, women, or non-court communities from royal and poet names alone; this entry does not supply that missing evidence.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **A layered authority system:** inherited language, performed myth, ancestral objects, and built thresholds can authorize one another without becoming a single centralized religion.
- **A coupling graph, not a shopping list:** an archaic verse needs a performer and interpreters; a pusaka needs custodians; a temple route needs access rules; a costume code needs observers who can read it.
- **An era transformation mechanism:** keep a prestigious older text while changing its audience, recitation language, costume presentation, or sponsoring institution; continuity of a name does not imply continuity of function.
- **A design comparison:** contrast the same epic episode as court kakawin, a later puppet lakon, and a relief sequence. Record additions, omissions, register, bodily coding, and who controls interpretation rather than assuming one original “canon.”
- **A handoff schema for lore-shokunin:** reference place/date → source and evidence type → observed coupling → proposed fictional mechanism → alternatives → unresolved claims. Supply no accepted world-bible facts from this entry alone.

## Provenance cautions and limits

- **Secure distinctions:** Austronesian language versus Sanskrit loans; language versus script; medieval composition versus later copy; weapon versus heirloom status; observable form versus symbolic explanation.
- **Contested/interpretive:** scope of Śiva–Buddha synthesis, territorial control inferred from praise poetry, particular relief identities, and ritual itineraries. Keep each claim attached to the scholar, text, and site that support it.
- **Source bias:** elite inscriptions, court poetry, monuments, later collections, and colonial-era philological categories disproportionately shape the archive. “Indianization” used as passive transplantation obscures local selection and transformation; inherited “Hindu-Javanese” labels need definition.
- **Disallowed shortcuts:** pan-Asian color dictionaries, universal archetype/hero-journey decoding, keris advertisements as dating evidence, and modern costume plates as direct medieval documentation. These are not substitutes for regional evidence.
- **Thin coverage here:** precise medieval pronunciation and conversation, regional dalang lineages outside the familiar Central Javanese literature, women's everyday ensembles, perishable textiles, and non-elite naming. The surviving archive is courtly, monumental, and later-collected, so these domains cannot be reconstructed from the sources used here — silence in the record is not permission to invent.
- **Chronicle scarcity:** this entry does not treat the *Pararaton* or later *babad* as independently verified eyewitness records. Dynasty anecdotes, keris origin tales, and exact collapse chronologies require a named edition plus comparison with inscriptions and archaeology before use.
- **Verification scope:** URLs and bibliographic records checked 2026-10-17; accessible extracts and heritage pages are not a page-by-page re-verification of every cited monograph. Exact quotations, lexical derivations, object dates, and borrowed sacred motifs need a source-location check before production use.
- **Living traditions:** Java and Bali contain diverse, changing communities; neither a court ideal nor an old monument defines all their people. Adapt relationships and mechanisms, retain attribution, and seek tradition-specific advice before presenting copied ritual or sacred imagery as authentic.
