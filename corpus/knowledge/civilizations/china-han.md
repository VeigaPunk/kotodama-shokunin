## Era staging: select a historical slice before borrowing

| Slice | Institutional/cultural change to retain | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Qin antecedent, 221–206 BCE | Imperial unification promotes a common written standard; administrative handwriting has its own development. | A new state inherits both official graphic authority and working scribal habits. [QW; LE] |
| Early Western Han, especially 202–141 BCE | Han retains substantial Qin administrative machinery while legitimating a new ruling house; regional cultures persist. | Continuity in documents can coexist with repudiation of the predecessor's moral reputation. [LE; SJ 28] |
| Emperor Wu, reign 141–87 BCE | Classical learning, expanding court cults, auspicious signs, named periods, and calendar reform are politically active. | Do not reduce this court to a single doctrine established by one decree. [HS 6; SJ 28; BU] |
| Later Western Han, 87 BCE–9 CE; Xin, 9–23 CE | Classical interpretation, portents, antiquarian reform, and competing succession arguments are available political resources. | Archaism and graphic interpretation can authorize a rupture rather than preserve continuity. [HS 24; BU] |
| Eastern Han, 25–220 CE | A restored dynasty governs from Luoyang; elite scholarship and material practices continue to change. | A Western Han tomb is not a complete template for an Eastern Han court. [LE] |

- **Chronology:** Western/Former Han is conventionally dated 206 BCE–9 CE; Liu Bang's imperial accession is 202 BCE. Xin interrupts Han; restoration in 25 follows the fall of Wang Mang in 23. Keep the intervening wars visible. [LE]
- “Western/Eastern” and “Former/Later” are historical period labels, not era titles used interchangeably with a sovereign's personal name.
- **Design method:** date each borrowed practice by court, region, status, and occasion; “Han-inspired” alone is insufficient metadata.

## Language family and writing: the standardization after-shock

- **Family:** Chinese belongs to the Sinitic branch of Sino-Tibetan; Han speech was neither modern Mandarin nor a single recoverable empire-wide pronunciation. Reconstruction is a model constrained by evidence, not an ancient audio recording. [SC; LE]
- **Diachrony:** distinguish Old Chinese reconstructions from Schuessler's Later Han layer; do not mix reconstructed forms from different systems without labeling the source and period. Pinyin in this entry is a modern reading aid. [SC]
- **Script type:** morphosyllabic writing, with phonetic and semantic components central to character formation; reject the popular “each sign directly depicts a timeless idea” model. Graphic continuity does not imply unchanged sound or meaning. [QW]
- **What Qin standardized:** authoritative character forms within a Qin-derived written tradition, not instant uniformity of pronunciation, literacy, handwriting, or local culture. [QW; LE]
- **What Han inherited:** the prestige of seal forms alongside clerical development and everyday documentary practice. Clerical script, *lishu* 隸書, has pre-Han antecedents; Han did not invent it from nothing after the Qin fell. [QW]
- **After-shock mechanism:** an empire can enforce readable documents while court scholars debate the correct ancient graph, textual lineage, or ritual precedent. Functional convergence and antiquarian disagreement are compatible. [QW; LE]
- **Material coupling:** brush-written strips and silk manuscripts constrain layout and copying labor; do not design an early Han book as a modern printed codex or assume paper immediately replaced other supports. [QW]

| Written register | Historical anchor | Fictional design question — inference |
|---|---|---|
| Seal/inscribed authority | Seal forms retain formal prestige alongside other scripts. [QW] | Does an archaic graphic style authenticate an office, object, or ancestral claim? |
| Administrative hand | Clerical development serves documentary writing. [QW] | Which abbreviated or conventional forms must a clerk learn to be employable? |
| Classical/interpretive learning | Textual learning can support imperial and elite authority. [LE; BU] | Who can turn a disputed reading into a political obligation? |
| Regional speech versus shared documents | Imperial integration does not eliminate regional difference. [LE] | Can two officials exchange records more readily than converse without accommodation? |

- Do not relabel the Qin/Han transition “simplified versus traditional Chinese”: that imports modern categories into a different graphic history.
- For a fictional language, model sound change, character change, and register change separately, then specify which institution keeps them partially aligned.

## Dong Zhongshu, the attributed corpus, and correlative government

- **Name correction:** the historical scholar is **Dong Zhongshu 董仲舒**, not “Dong Zhongzhou.” His career belongs to the second century BCE; precise life dates vary in scholarship. [HS 56; BU]
- **Separate three evidence levels:** the historical Dong; the heterogeneous received *Chunqiu fanlu* attributed to him; and the wider, older repertoire of yin–yang and Five-Phase reasoning. They are not interchangeable authors or systems. [BU]
- **Secure textual claim:** *Hanshu* 56 presents Dong interpreting disasters as Heaven's warnings, linking humane rule and punishment to seasonal/yin–yang order, and connecting calendar and clothing-color reform to legitimate kingship.
- **Contested historical inference:** that this biography transparently records his exact influence on Wu's government. Its retrospective presentation differs from the briefer *Shiji* account; read the political reputation as something constructed over time. [BU, sections 2–4]
- **Strong attribution caution:** the Five-Phase chapters of *Chunqiu fanlu* cannot safely be assigned to Dong himself. Buljan reports that Queen/Major and other scholars exclude Five-Phase sections from his secure corpus. Do not say “Dong invented the Five Phases.” [BU, section 6; QM]
- **Named framework:** correlative reasoning links classified processes across cosmos, seasons, rulership, ritual, and human affairs; resonance supplies an explanatory connection, not merely a decorative resemblance. [HN 3, 6; HS 56]
- **Translation calibration:** *wuxing* 五行 is conventionally “Five Phases”; “Five Elements” can misleadingly suggest a fixed Greek-style inventory of substances. These categories also organize sequences, activities, and relations.

| Phase | Spatial/seasonal association | Color family | Directional creature in *Huainanzi* 3 |
|---|---|---|---|
| Wood 木 | East; spring | *qing* 青, blue/green range | Azure/Blue-Green Dragon, *Canglong* 蒼龍 |
| Fire 火 | South; summer | Red | Vermilion Bird, *Zhuniao* 朱鳥 |
| Earth 土 | Center; regulates the quarters | Yellow | Yellow Dragon, *Huanglong* 黃龍 |
| Metal 金 | West; autumn | White | White Tiger, *Baihu* 白虎 |
| Water 水 | North; winter | Black/dark | Dark Warrior, *Xuanwu* 玄武 |

- **Table limits:** this is a text-specific correspondence set, not every Han person's beliefs. The chapter also presents fivefold temporal schedules; do not force Earth into one universally agreed “fifth season.” Color words are not exact modern paint specifications. [HN 3]
- **Generating sequence:** Wood → Fire → Earth → Metal → Water → Wood. **Conquest sequence:** Wood → Earth → Water → Fire → Metal → Wood; succession arguments can depend on which relation is chosen. Do not silently switch cycles. [HN 3; SJ 28]
- **Political effect:** an anomalous season can become a criticism of administration; a ruler's correction, ritual response, or changed timetable can be presented as restoring order. This is the sources' causal claim, not an empirically established theory of weather. [HS 56]
- **Evidence against a single fixed imperial color:** *Shiji* 28 records rival Water/Earth claims for Han and differing ritual colors; *Hanshu* 6 links the Taichu reform to honoring yellow. “Han always wore red” is untenable.
- **Apply by inference:** choose who owns the correspondence table, how rivals challenge its interpretation, and which remedies impose real costs on officials, dyers, farmers, and mourners.

## Mythic/pantheon core: overlapping jurisdictions, not one catechism

| Repertoire | Attested anchor | Boundary for borrowing |
|---|---|---|
| Heaven, ancestors, and moral rulership | Heaven's responses and ancestral legitimacy in *Hanshu* 56; ancestral/court sacrifices in *Shiji* 28. | Do not equate Heaven automatically with a single anthropomorphic creator. |
| Taiyi 太一 and Five Emperors | Wu's sacrificial programs rank Taiyi and associate the Five Emperors with directions/colors. [SJ 28] | A court's ritual hierarchy is not a census of local gods. |
| Houtu 后土, earth and territorial ritual | Wu's earth sacrifices at Fenyin are narrated with yellow-clad participants. [SJ 28; HS 6] | Do not import all features or gender presentations of later Houtu cults. |
| Nüwa 女媧 repairing the sky | Five-colored stones, the giant turtle's legs, and flood control in *Huainanzi* 6. | Cosmic repair is not automatically a creation-from-nothing narrative. |
| Xiwangmu 西王母 and immortality | Yi seeks the drug of deathlessness; Heng'e takes it and goes to the moon. [HN 6] | This passage does not establish every later Queen Mother/Chang'e story. |
| Household, regional, and specialist rites | *Shiji* 28 enumerates regional ritual specialists and cults, including practices beyond central sacrificial administration. | Avoid treating court classicists as the only religious actors. |

- **Symbol syntax:** creature + direction + placement + occasion conveys more than creature alone; a dragon in a directional scheme is not automatically an exclusive emblem of the emperor. [HN 3]
- **Object check:** Lady Dai's painted silk banner combines an elite human figure, attendants, celestial imagery, and lower registers. It is a localized mortuary assemblage, not a diagram explaining all Han religion. [MD]
- **Contested interpretation:** the banner's function and the identities of some depicted beings remain debated. Do not confidently label every human-headed/serpentine figure Nüwa, Fuxi, or an ancestor without object-specific scholarship. [MD]
- **Coupling lesson — inference:** material luxury can carry a mortuary cosmography; clothing in the image also makes the deceased's social position legible. Distinguish the living garment, its painted representation, and the burial object's ritual use.

## Era-name invention: authority over historical time

- **Secure anchor:** named eras develop under Emperor Wu. The received annals call his first named period *Jianyuan* 建元, conventionally 140–135 BCE; this does not prove the title was proclaimed in 140. [HS 6; SJ 28]
- **Retrospection matters:** *Shiji* 28 describes replacing numbered periods with auspicious names and later relates naming *Yuanding* 元鼎 to the tripod and *Yuanfeng* 元封 to the sealing sacrifice. Earlier names can be retrospective labels.
- **Open precision limit:** scholarship does not settle when named eras formally began: modern reference accounts give *Jianyuan* as the first era name from 140 BCE while also treating the 110 BCE Fengshan proclamation of *Yuanfeng* as the formal establishment of the institution [era-name survey, citing Wilkinson 1998, p. 177, and Sato 1991]. The exact first administrative use/proclamation year of each early title therefore remains **(to-verify)** at document level. Do not turn a modern chronological table into an eyewitness record of what a clerk wrote that year.

| Dating layer | Example or anchor | What it communicates |
|---|---|---|
| Regnal counting without a named era | Earlier reigns also restart year counts. [SJ 28] | A new “first year” is not, by itself, an invented era name. |
| Retrospectively organized era | *Jianyuan* 建元, “Establishing the Origin” | A court/historian can organize an earlier reign into meaningful named blocks. |
| Event-linked title | *Yuanfeng* 元封, beginning 110 BCE in the received chronology. [HS 6] | Ritual accomplishment authorizes a renewed beginning; proclamation need not precede every month retrospectively assigned to it. |
| Calendar reform | *Taichu* 太初, beginning 104 BCE; first month becomes the year's beginning, with yellow and five privileged. [HS 6; SJ 28] | Naming, calendrical reckoning, official terminology, and ritual symbolism can move together. |

- **Keep layers separate:** ruler's lifetime, accession, era-title boundaries, calendar-year boundaries, and later dynastic periodization need not coincide. One Han sovereign can use several era names.
- **Dating recipe — inference:** specify authority + era title + numbered year + month, including intercalation when relevant + day designation; retain the source calendar before converting to an external timeline.
- **Useful phrase-level coupling:** *Hanshu* 56 puts “change the calendrical beginning” and “change clothing colors” into a kingship argument. An era reset can entail textile orders and administrative retraining, not just a new heading on a map.
- Do not import the Ming/Qing expectation of one principal era title per emperor into this Han model.

## Hemp, silk, and dress-code registers

- **Material base:** bast-fibre processing and silk reeling are different production chains; hemp/ramie and silkworm silk require different plants, labor, equipment, and expertise. Do not treat “cloth” as a single resource tier. [KT]
- **Lexical calibration:** *ma* 麻 and *bu* 布 cannot always be converted mechanically into a modern botanical fibre label; distinguish wording, fibre identification, weave, and finish. “Linen” must not silently imply flax. [KT; YI]
- **Signature:** Han elite evidence includes silk robes and elaborately furnished burials; cross-collars, layered garments, and wrap construction are useful visual leads, but exact cuts require a dated garment or image, not a modern “hanfu” shop taxonomy. [MD; LE]
- **Class is not a fibre binary:** silk can mark wealth, official gifts, or ritual expenditure; coarse bast clothing can mark hardship, deliberate restraint, or a required mourning relation. Rank, occasion, and moral performance can override price. [HS 6, 24; YI]

| Register | Material/visual signal | What can safely be inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Agricultural and ordinary work | Cloth production sits within the farming/weaving ideal. [HS 24A] | Gendered household labor is a normative economic model, not proof that every household was self-sufficient. |
| Elite display and burial | Silk clothing and represented attendants at Mawangdui. [MD] | Resources and mortuary aspirations of a particular elite household, not typical peasant consumption. |
| State reward | Wu's annals record graded distributions of silk. [HS 6] | Textile value connects favor, hierarchy, and public distribution; not simply personal fashion. |
| Sumptuary demotion | Gaozu's prohibition on merchants wearing silk and riding in carriages; subsequent relaxation. [HS 24B] | Legal humiliation targets status; it does not prove merchants lacked wealth or complied universally. |
| Mourning | Graded cloth, hemp cords, construction details, and durations in “Sangfu.” [YI] | Textile register makes kinship and obligation visible; coarse material is not automatically low rank. |
| Cosmic/court ritual | Yellow for specified earth/imperial ritual contexts; other rites use other colors. [SJ 28] | Occasion-specific alignment, not a single dynasty-wide wardrobe color law. |

- **Mourning framework:** *zhancui* 斬衰, *zicui* 齊衰, *dagong* 大功, *xiaogong* 小功, and *sima* 緦麻 distinguish grades; hemming, cloth fineness, cords, duration, and relation matter together. The five grades are not five social classes. [YI]
- **Norm versus practice:** *Yili* is a transmitted prescriptive ritual tradition, older in origin than Zheng Xuan's Han commentary. Its detailed kinship hierarchy must not be presented as a directly observed uniform Han dress law.
- **Useful contradiction:** *Hanshu* 24A complains that merchants enjoy conspicuous clothing despite legal abasement. A law and a luxury market can coexist; record both instead of using prohibition as proof of absence.
- **Do not back-project:** late-imperial dragon robes, embroidered official rank badges, and “yellow exclusively for the emperor” require their own period-specific evidence; they are not default Han identifiers.
- **Construction limit:** exact *quju* 曲裾/*zhiju* 直裾 pattern reconstruction, status-exclusive cuts, and province-wide dye rules are **(to-verify)** against named finds and specialist clothing studies; this entry does not supply a sewing pattern.
- **Apply by inference:** write a garment record as fibre → processing → weave → dye/finish → cut → fastening/headwear → wearer/occasion → permitted/prohibited/reported use; never infer the last fields from fibre alone.

## Naming practices: persons, offices, and reigns are different namespaces

| Namespace | Historical example | Design guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Family + personal name | 董｜仲舒, Dong Zhongshu; 公孫｜弘, Gongsun Hong; separators show surname/given-name boundaries. [HS 56] | Compound surnames and multi-character personal names both occur; do not impose one fixed syllable count or split every name after its first character. |
| Adult/social address | Courtesy names, *zi* 字, belong to elite address conventions. [LE] | A courtesy name is not a hereditary surname or evidence that every commoner had one recorded. |
| Office or territorial position | Dong as chancellor of Jiangdu in the biography. [HS 56] | A title can identify a person relationally without becoming their birth name. |
| Posthumous designation | 孝武, Xiaowu, at the opening of Wu's annals. [HS 6] | “Emperor Wu” is retrospective designation, not his childhood personal name. |
| Era title | 建元, 元封, 太初. [HS 6] | A date-label belongs to a period under an authority, not automatically to the sovereign as a personal name. |

- **Written forms versus sounds:** use modern readings only as labels unless a historical reconstruction is provided; apparent modern rhymes or homophones do not demonstrate Han naming motives. [SC]
- **Archive asymmetry:** elite male biographies preserve richer naming detail than many women's, laborers', or local ritual actors' records. Missing recorded names do not imply nameless people or an absence of naming customs. [LE; HS]
- **Graphic politics:** *Hanshu* 24B attributes part of Wang Mang's monetary reform to reading 金 “metal/gold” and 刀 “knife” in 劉, the Han ruling surname. This is narrated political graph-analysis, not a scientific etymology or neutral account of his motives.
- **Apply by inference:** maintain separate fields for birth name, lineage, courtesy/address form, office, prohibited/avoided forms, posthumous label, and chronological usage; let access and occasion determine which one is spoken or written.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Standardization without cultural flattening:** choose a common administrative script, then preserve differences in speech, handwriting, ritual expertise, and local memory; identify who pays the cost of learning the standard.
- **A disputable correlation engine:** publish the court's correspondence table and its competitors' tables; make a disputed portent change a budget, calendar, punishment policy, or textile commission rather than merely decorate a prophecy.
- **A material grammar of relationships:** let coarse mourning cloth temporarily outrank expensive silk as the correct sign of duty; specify the relationship and occasion that make the inversion intelligible.
- **Named time as political action:** distinguish an event, its proclamation, and retrospective redating; require archives and households to reconcile the resulting document and supply-chain changes.
- **Coupling worksheet — inference:** choose a dated authority → define its legitimating precedent → choose graphic/register consequences → specify ritual date/color → check dye/fibre access → list dissenting or exempt actors → record confidence for every link.
- **Stress test:** can a clerk, wealthy merchant, mourner, regional ritual specialist, and court scholar encounter the same emblem differently without the setting contradicting its own rules? If not, the model is probably too homogeneous.
- **Handoff to lore-shokunin:** offer the rule, historical analogue, allowed alternatives, dependencies, uncertainty, and likely contradiction tests. Only lore may select and record fictional canon; this reference selects none.

## Provenance cautions and thin areas

- **Chronicle bias:** [SJ]/[HS] privilege elite state concerns and moral-political narratives. [HS]'s portrayal of Wang Mang is written from the victorious Han perspective; reported omens, motives, and universalizing claims require corroboration.
- **Survival bias:** rich sealed tombs preserve spectacular silk and images; ordinary garments, oral practice, and local religious interpretation are less evenly visible. Do not upscale Mawangdui into an empire-wide wardrobe or creed.
- **Thin coverage here:** frontier communities, non-Sinitic languages, regional dye workshops, ordinary women's naming, and garment construction are not reconstructed in depth. Specific claims about them remain **(to-verify)** rather than being filled with generic “ancient Chinese” detail.
- **Scholarship calibration:** [BU] documents genuine attribution disagreements, including disagreements within modern Western scholarship; “Dong's system” is a convenience label, not settled authorship. Chinese and Japanese textual criticism are part of that debate, not merely footnotes to Western discovery.
- **Category/translation bias:** reject timeless “Confucian state religion,” primitive-superstition caricatures, and modern personality/element charts as substitutes for dated sources. Colonial and missionary translations may impose their own religious categories; check terms, edition, and translator rather than assuming either universal error or neutrality.
- **Object-source caution:** [MD] supplies useful images and acknowledges interpretive disputes; do not inherit its broad social generalizations or detailed identifications without checking excavation and specialist literature. No direct excavation-report collation was performed for this entry.
- **Stop rule:** a plausible coupling is a design hypothesis, not recovered Han fact; leave an unresolved attribution, proclamation date, or costume entitlement marked **(to-verify)** until the relevant text or artifact supports it.
