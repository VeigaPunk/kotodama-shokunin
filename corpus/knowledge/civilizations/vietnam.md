## Boundaries: Vietnam is not one timeless culture
- This base concentrates on Vietnamese-speaking lowland Việt/Kinh histories, especially northern written traditions; modern Vietnam’s borders do not delimit an ancient linguistic or religious unit. [L97; T13]
- Mường and other Vietic languages are relatives, not “primitive Vietnamese”; Cham is Austronesian and Khmer is Austroasiatic but neither is a variant of Vietnamese. Family membership does not imply shared costume or pantheon. [L97; T13]
- Southern expansion involved warfare, migration, displacement, trade, and contact with Cham, Khmer, Chinese, and other populations; do not make the south an empty stage filled by an unchanged northern culture. [T13; PT04]
- Treat “Austroasiatic core” as linguistic descent plus reconstructible inheritance, not a claim that all village rites are primordial survivals; “Sinitic strata” includes everyday contact as well as elite textual culture. [L97; P25]
- Knowledge is thinner here on minority-language ritual literatures, detailed Cham/Khmer interactions, regional garment cutting, and premodern village speech. Commission separate references rather than inventing connective history.

## Language: genealogy, contact, and register are different axes
| Axis | Vietnamese reference pattern | Fictional-design operation | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Descent | Austroasiatic → Vietic; Vietnamese and Mường are closely related | Build inherited vocabulary and sound correspondences before adding loans | Established broad placement [L97] |
| Structure | Predominantly analytic; SVO, classifiers, compounding, reduplication | Specify grammatical constructions, not just a list of one-syllable names | Established; constructions vary [L97] |
| Sound history | Tonal modern language; earlier consonantal contrasts matter to reconstruction | A later tone system can preserve distinctions after consonants change | Broad historical framework; exact stages need specialist checking [L97; P25] |
| Contact strata | Learned Hán-Việt vocabulary coexists with older, more deeply integrated Sinitic loans | Permit several borrowing episodes with different adaptation rules | Established layering; individual etymologies need evidence [L97; P25] |
| Social history | Phan reconstructs a local Sinitic variety, “Annamese Middle Chinese,” and consequential language shift | Model bilingual households, shifting prestige, and language replacement, not just court borrowing | Named recent reconstruction, not a universal settled chronology [P25] |
| Geographic variation | Northern, central, and southern speech differ in sound and vocabulary | Keep orthography, local pronunciation, and literary reading separate | Established; do not give every locality the same tone inventory [L97] |
| Person reference | Kin terms, names, titles, and pronoun-like forms locate a relationship | Address depends on age, stance, intimacy, role, and audience | Social-pragmatic framework [Lu90] |
- Do not classify Vietnamese as Sinitic because of loans, tones, or Chinese-derived writing; neither “pure native language” nor “Chinese with local pronunciation” describes the history. [L97; P25]
- A practical vocabulary contrast is ordinary *nước* “water” versus learned *thủy* in compounds; semantic distribution is not free synonymy. Verify each intended compound, register, and etymology rather than substituting mechanically. [L97]
- Contact layers can cut across intimacy and formality: an old loan may be ordinary speech; an inherited word may occur in solemn poetry. No percentage of “Chinese words” is supplied: results depend on corpus, dictionary, and counting unit.
- For conlang work, annotate each item with `{inherited/borrowed, donor stage, sound adaptation, earliest evidence, social domain}`; a fictional mapping is a design choice, not a reconstruction of Vietnamese.

## Writing: chữ Hán, chữ Nôm, and quốc ngữ are not one ladder
| System | What it records / does | Coupling opportunity | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Literary Chinese (language); chữ Hán (characters) | Literary Chinese served scholarship and government; its character repertoire also supports conventional Vietnamese readings | Diplomatic documents and learned names can cross spoken-language boundaries | Confusing script with language, or every character text with vernacular Vietnamese [L97; T13] |
| Hán-Việt readings | Conventional Sino-Vietnamese readings of characters; also a major lexical stratum | One graph may connect local learned speech to a wider textual network | Treating this as another alphabet or modern Mandarin pronunciation [L97] |
| Chữ Nôm | Character-based writing of Vietnamese using borrowed graphs and locally created formations | Extend an established graphic technology to words its inherited inventory does not directly represent | Calling it an alphabet, a wholly independent script, or merely corrupted Chinese [L97; N18] |
| Quốc ngữ | Romanized Vietnamese, developed through early-modern missionary collaboration and later expanded into mass public use | Orthographic change reorganizes schooling, publishing, access, and prestige | Giving one European sole authorship or dating general literacy to the first dictionary [L97; T13] |
- **Nôm construction toolkit:** graph borrowing; phonetic borrowing; new phono-semantic compounds; additional formation types, including phono-equivalent compounds, occur in N18’s analysis. Do not reduce every Nôm character to one identical recipe.
- **Phono-semantic craft model:** combine a component indicating semantic class with one supplying a historically plausible sound cue; the sound cue need not match present-day speech exactly. [N18]
- N18 analyzes graphs representing Sinitic loans in *Quốc Âm Thi Tập*: even a borrowed word may receive a newly generated Nôm graph. “New character” does not automatically mean “inherited native word.”
- Existing-graph use can precede elaborate new graph formation; an origin story about one inventor is not a substitute for dated witnesses. Earliest claimed inscriptions, recut stones, and copied manuscripts require individual checks: the earliest securely datable surviving Nôm inscription is generally placed at the Bảo Ân (Tháp Miếu) stele of 1209/1210, so any earlier attribution needs its own witness. [K17]
- Surviving-copy date, composition date, attribution, and linguistic layer are separate fields. A late copy containing archaic spellings is neither automatically ancient nor automatically a modern fabrication.
- Alphabetic spelling separates written syllables with spaces; a space is not a reliable test for a whole lexical word. Preserve Vietnamese diacritics in citations and names. [L97]
- Apply with `../linguistics/writing-systems.md`: design who learns each system, who pays a scribe, which genres permit mixed scripts, and who can hear a text without reading it.

## Làng: communal registers as relationships and institutions
- *Làng* “village” and *xã* “commune” are not interchangeable labels in every historical administration; specify the actual settlement and governing unit. Do not universalize one northern ethnography. [Lu10; T13]
- The framework is **speech event × participant relation × institutional authority × material witness**; the table is a craft research grid, not a claim that every village possessed every document.

| Event or record | Linguistic / documentary layer | Myth–symbol–material connection | What must be specified |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household conversation | Kinship-based reference, personal names, situational stance | Age and relationship can matter more than official title | Who addresses whom, in whose hearing [Lu90] |
| Communal-house gathering (*đình*) | Public participation and locally recognized offices | A space of communal affairs and tutelary worship | Date, village, eligibility, exclusions; not an egalitarian town-hall default [Lu10] |
| Village convention (*hương ước*) | Written regulation distinct from what people actually do | Obligations, ritual expenses, sanctions, and precedence become documentary questions | Actual wording, script, redaction, and enforcement `(to-verify)` for a chosen village |
| Deity narrative (*thần tích*) | An account legitimating a locally honored spirit | Local landscape and miracles can be connected to wider dynastic history | Surviving manuscript, editor, and patron; not an eyewitness biography `(to-verify)` |
| Royal investiture (*sắc phong*) | A sovereign document conferring titles or recognition | Court authority can enter local ritual through an authenticated object | Issuing ruler, era date, recipient, seals, and local reception `(to-verify)` |
| Lineage genealogy (*gia phả*) | Selective written ancestry rather than a neutral population census | Kinship, commemoration, status, and property claims can reinforce one another | Whose branch sponsored the record and who is omitted `(to-verify)` |
| Ritual petition / performance | Written composition, oral delivery, music, and spectatorship need not share one register | A text’s audience may include spirits as well as living participants | Actual genre, officiant, language, dress, and performance sequence `(to-verify)` |

- Terms identify document genres worth researching; no specimen-level contents, scripts, or dates are certified by this entry. Use Lu10 as contextual ethnography, not blanket proof for every row.
- Separate *đình* from *chùa* (Buddhist temple/pagoda) and *đền* (shrine/temple to a deity or honored figure); local functions can overlap, so building labels alone do not determine ritual. [T13; Dr07]
- Encode competing precedence: age, office, lineage, wealth, religious role, and revolutionary service need not rank the same person first. Lu10’s colonial, revolutionary, and market-era changes defeat a frozen “traditional village” model.
- Do not turn *anh*, *chị*, *em*, *ông*, or *bà* into fixed translations of “you”; speaker self-reference can shift with the relationship being claimed. Copying a term without its interactional setting loses the mechanism. [Lu90]
- Apply: generate one everyday exchange, one public announcement, and one ritual/documentary text for the same event; record differences in speaker entitlement, vocabulary, audience, and clothing, not merely “more archaic words.”

## Mythic core: narrative charters, local powers, and plural practice
| Cluster | Reference anchor | Coupling available to a worldbuilder | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lạc Long Quân / Âu Cơ / Hùng kings | Dragon ancestry, an extraordinary birth, division of descendants, royal origins | Kinship can map upland/lowland relationships into an origin charter | Medieval textual witness and later reinterpretation, not a prehistoric census [K12] |
| Hùng kings commemoration | Pilgrimage, offerings, processions, communal participation in Phú Thọ | Ritual food, sound, place, and ancestral language can support public identity | Contemporary heritage practice; inscription in 2012 is not the cult’s birth date [U12] |
| Village tutelary powers (*thành hoàng*) | Local divine protection alongside recognition and narration | A local founder or protector can acquire titles and a public biography | Local cult must be identified; no standard biography for all villages [Lu10; T13] |
| Liễu Hạnh | Goddess with literary accounts and changing religious/political interpretations | Text, gender, authority, locality, and patronage alter a deity’s public identity | Dror traces an ostensible sixteenth-century emergence, not an undated primordial goddess [Dr07] |
| Mother Goddesses of Three Realms | Heaven, water, mountains/forests; worship, possession, music, dance, costume | Performance can make cosmological affiliation visible and audible | UNESCO’s named practice; do not conflate every Three/Four Realms system [U16] |
| Bà Chúa Xứ | Southern pilgrimage and practical expectations of divine aid | Trade, mobility, health, prosperity, and shrine economies can shape devotion | Southern case; not interchangeable with Liễu Hạnh [PT04] |
| Buddhist, Daoist, Confucian, ancestral practices | Interacting institutions, ethics, texts, rites, and household obligations | One person may participate in several contexts without a single exclusive creed | “Three teachings” is not three neatly separated national churches [T13; Dr07] |
- **Textual anchor:** *Lĩnh Nam chích quái* has a history of compilation and redaction; cite the particular account and recension, not an imagined unchanged oral original. [K12]
- Kelley’s “invented tradition” analysis challenges unbroken transmission of Hùng-king historical information from the first millennium BCE; it does not establish that every motif was newly invented or that current devotion is insincere.
- Debate is explicit: see K. W. Taylor’s [2012 comments](https://doi.org/10.1525/vs.2012.7.2.131) and Tạ Chí Đại Trường’s [2012 comments](https://doi.org/10.1525/vs.2012.7.2.139) in the same journal issue; positions must be read, not inferred from the existence of responses.
- For Sơn Tinh–Thủy Tinh flood narratives or Thánh Gióng’s warrior/horse imagery, identify the version and performance before extracting ecological or military meanings `(to-verify)`; do not identify a mythic enemy with a securely dated invasion.
- “Pantheon” is a convenience label: build networks of shrines, titles, specialists, sponsors, and obligations before assigning a closed, canonical list of gods.

## Symbolic system: relationships and uses, not a motif dictionary
| Symbolic surface | Productive coupling | Required restraint |
|---|---|---|
| Dragon ancestry in origin narrative | Descent, water-associated origins, political belonging | This does not fix the meaning of every dragon on a robe or roof [K12] |
| Court-recognized divine title | Written authority, local protector, ceremonial display | Trace the actual grant; a title may be later than the narrative it authorizes `(to-verify)` |
| Mother-Goddess performance costume | A spirit’s appearance can involve clothing, movement, music, and attendants | Exact colour-to-realm and headgear mappings are lineage/site-specific `(to-verify)`; no universal palette supplied [U16] |
| Hùng commemoration’s rice offerings and drums | Agricultural hopes, ancestors, public performance | Present use does not establish unchanged Bronze Age meaning [U12] |
| Áo dài as national image | Tailoring, gender, modernity, diaspora, and nationhood | A national symbol can be historically recent and internally contested [Le03] |
| Landscape-linked shrine | Place-names and journeys make a deity socially locatable | Keep local explanations, pilgrimage economy, and later heritage rhetoric separate [Dr07; PT04] |
- Use `../myth-symbol/symbolism-synthesis-method.md`: record `{object, site, date, medium, user, audience, stated meaning, alternative reading}` before generalizing.
- Đông Sơn bronze drums are archaeological objects, not proof of one recoverable spoken language, a continuous national uniform, or a decoded myth. Specific motif readings and ethnic assignments remain `(to-verify)` unless supported by a named archaeological study.
- Reject “all Asian dragons mean X,” Jungian archetypes as historical proof, and a universal hero’s journey as the explanation of local narrative transmission; resemblance is a comparison prompt, not demonstrated descent.

## Era model: periodization and niên hiệu must stay separate
- Use T13 for broad political staging; combine it with P25 for language-history hypotheses and Le03 for modern dress. Political borders, vocabulary, script use, and garment cuts change on different schedules.

| Historical staging | Political distinction | Language–myth–material consequence to investigate |
|---|---|---|
| Before the tenth century | Multiple populations and changing imperial jurisdictions; not one unbroken provincial condition | Sparse surviving evidence; later chronicles cannot supply direct village dialogue or full costume patterns |
| Tenth-century courts | Ngô, Đinh, Early Lê; locally based sovereignty consolidates | Inherited textual practices can continue after political autonomy; script does not change overnight |
| Lý (1009–1225), Trần (1225–1400) | Distinct courts, Buddhist institutions, warfare, and evolving learned culture | Date inscriptions and images individually; do not project a modern áo dài silhouette backward |
| Hồ (1400–1407), Ming occupation (1407–1427/28) | Dynastic rupture, conquest, and resistance | Separate proposed vernacular policies, actual surviving texts, destruction claims, and later reconstruction |
| Lê sơ (early phase of Later Lê, 1428–1527) | Court consolidation, institutions, literary production | Learned writing and vernacular composition coexist; neither implies universal literacy |
| Mạc from 1527 / restored Lê (1533–1789); Trịnh and Nguyễn lords | Sixteenth–eighteenth-century overlapping claims and regional governments | A court’s era label can survive while lords exercise power; regional dress and contact histories diverge |
| Tây Sơn | Rebellion begins 1771; dynastic claims from 1778; competing rulers until Nguyễn victory in 1802 | Date a policy to a particular ruler and jurisdiction, not a uniformly governed “Tây Sơn moment” |
| Nguyễn (1802–1945) | Unified monarchy; nineteenth-century conquest produces uneven colonial subordination | Court institutions, vernacular literature, garment regulations, and colonial documentation overlap |
| Colonial, revolutionary, divided, reform-era contexts | Conquest from 1858; 1945 rupture; 1954 division; 1975 end of war; Đổi Mới from 1986 | Quốc ngữ publics, urban fashion, socialist critiques, and national/diasporic revivals are distinct processes |

- **Dynasty:** a ruling house/historical grouping, such as Nguyễn; **niên hiệu** 年號: a promulgated era title used to number years; **miếu hiệu** 廟號: a temple name. None is simply a person’s everyday name. [L97; T13]
- **Disambiguation anchor:** Nguyễn Phúc Ánh is the personal name associated with the Gia Long emperor (era name Gia Long, 1802–1820; temple name Thế Tổ); Gia Long is an era title, not a new family surname. [T13] Specific title forms on an artifact still require its inscription.
- **Examples (conventional year ranges):** Thuận Thiên under Lý Thái Tổ (1010–1028) is not Thuận Thiên under Lê Thái Tổ (1428–1433); Hồng Đức belongs to Lê Thánh Tông (1470–1497). Resolve ruler, jurisdiction, and context rather than using an era label alone. [T13]
- A ruler can adopt several era names; a dynasty contains many eras; an era title can outlive an accession boundary. Exact first/last lunar dates and exceptional continuations require a chronological concordance `(to-verify)`.
- For production, store `{dynasty/regime, ruler, era title, era year, lunar date, sexagenary date if present, calendar conversion, source}`. Never convert an uncertain lunar date by simply adding an era year to a Gregorian accession year.

## Costume and material signature: áo dài is an evolution, not an ancient constant
| Stage / setting | Reference silhouette or change | Coupled social meaning | Evidence limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regional antecedents | Skirts (*váy*), halter/bodice forms (*yếm*), and four-panel tunics (*áo tứ thân*) belong to the comparison set | Region, work, class, and modesty norms differ | Do not distribute one northern ensemble to all women or every medieval century [Le03] |
| Nguyễn-lord southern distinction | Long tunic and trousers participate in differentiating subjects from northerners | Political jurisdiction becomes embodied in dress | The 1744 dress-and-cap reform of Nguyễn Phúc Khoát is recorded in the dynastic compilation *Đại Nam thực lục* (as quoted in later scholarship), and researchers link its buttoned tunic-and-trousers ensemble to the later *áo ngũ thân* [Đ13]; that linkage is scholarly interpretation, not the birth of the modern fitted garment |
| Nineteenth-/early twentieth-century antecedents | Loose long tunics; *áo ngũ thân* denotes five-panel construction | Court, urban, festival, gender, and labor contexts must be separated | Exact pattern pieces and securely dated examples `(to-verify)`; do not treat the name as a complete pattern [Le03] |
| 1930s urban redesign | Nguyễn Cát Tường / Lemur blends local garments with European tailoring and bodily aesthetics | The “new woman,” print culture, class aspiration, conservative criticism | Name a designer and context, not a single final invention date [Le03] |
| 1950s–1960s southern experimentation | Closer fit, neckline changes, raglan-sleeve innovations | Fashionable femininity and competing respectability claims | Not every woman adopted the same style; named-shop priority requires checking [Le03] |
| Socialist critiques and scarcity | Dress may be criticized as bourgeois, impractical, or decadent; utilitarian alternatives gain weight | Labor ideals, politics, resources, and region interact | Critique is not evidence of a uniform nationwide legal ban [Le03] |
| Late twentieth-century revival and diaspora | Uniforms, special-occasion wear, pageants, design businesses, heritage display | A garment can become “traditional” through modern institutions and transnational exchange | Diaspora is an active producer of meanings, not a frozen museum [Le03] |
- In its familiar modern form, áo dài couples a long, side-slit tunic with trousers; standing collar, sleeve treatment, fit, and fabric are variables, not a permanent definition across all eras. [Le03]
- Earlier male and female long-tunic traditions must not be erased by the modern garment’s strongly feminized national image. A school uniform, groom’s formalwear, work outfit, and medium’s costume are different research problems. [Le03; U16]
- Do not make every outfit silk: investigate fiber, weave, price, laundering, climate, work, and repair. Specific historical fiber distributions, loom-width explanations, and dye recipes require textile evidence `(to-verify)`.
- Court caps, rank insignia, ceremonial robes, and everyday clothes need separate inventories; a modern photograph of an áo dài cannot establish dynastic sumptuary rules `(to-verify)`.
- Apply `../culture-material/costume-semiotics.md`: describe cut and making first, then occasion and wearer, then disputed meanings. “Traditional dress” is an actor’s claim to investigate, not a date.

## Naming practices: persons, places, dynasties, and document voices
| Naming layer | Reference rule | Craft implication / safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Personal name | Family name precedes middle/given-name elements; element counts vary | Store family and chosen address name separately; do not force exactly three syllables [L97; Lu90] |
| Address | Given names, titles, kin terms, and relationship-sensitive reference coexist | A formal full name on a register need not be the form used at home [Lu90] |

| Place-name registers | A place may have local oral and official learned forms | Research each attested pair; do not automatically “translate” every village name into Hán-Việt `(to-verify)` |
| Taboo / avoidance | Royal or lineage name avoidance can affect written and spoken forms | Documented case: the 1841 Thiệu Trị taboo on the character *Hoa* (for the empress Hồ Thị Hoa) renamed places such as Thăng Hoa → Thăng Bình and Mộ Hoa → Mộ Đức, per *Châu bản triều Nguyễn* [Th97]; from any single case derive no general sound law |
- Preserve diacritics and complete personal-name order in reference records; an ASCII search key can be additional metadata, not the authoritative spelling.
- Separate historical orthography, present spelling, and fictional adaptation. Random diacritics are neither Vietnamese phonotactics nor a respectful naming method.
- A surname shared by a dynasty and an ordinary person does not demonstrate royal descent. Do not infer genealogy, ethnicity, or political allegiance from one name alone.

## What a worldbuilder can take
1. **Layered inheritance:** choose a language family, then model several contact episodes; couple each episode to institutions, genres, and borrowing rules rather than cosmetic syllables. This is an optional design method, not an imposed fictional history.
2. **Graphic appropriation with local invention:** give scribes a reason to reuse a prestigious script, then create graph-formation rules that solve specific vernacular recording problems; preserve irregularities and dated variants.
3. **A village with several authorities:** define who speaks at home, at a communal gathering, in a regulation, and before a deity; let those entitlements disagree. Specify whose labor and money support public ritual.
4. **Myth as a situated charter:** separate story time from attestation time and performance time; let shrine communities, courts, schools, and migrants interpret the same ancestry differently.
5. **Two clocks:** political succession and linguistic/material change need independent timelines; a new era title need not produce a new accent, script, or garment overnight.
6. **Dress as an argument:** stage alterations in cutting, fabric, circulation, and bodily ideals; “national costume” can be the outcome of debate, enterprise, and displacement, not an unchanged inheritance.
7. **A coupling test:** if a character’s public role changes, check the consequences for address terms, written titles, ritual participation, and occasion-specific dress; permit explicit exceptions rather than demanding mechanical agreement.
8. **Handoff only:** send lore-shokunin alternatives with `{reference, region/date, proposed mechanism, language effect, myth/symbol effect, era constraint, costume effect, uncertainty, approval_needed}`; lore owns all fictional canon choices.

## Provenance cautions and next checks
- **Language:** genealogy is well established; particular loan etymologies, percentages, sound-change dates, and Phan’s detailed demographic scenario require specialist review. New scholarship is not automatically consensus.
- **Chronicles and legends:** survival is uneven, particularly for early vernacular writing and everyday material life. Date the physical witness and its redactions; a chronicler’s attributed ancient speech is not a recording of that speech.
- **Debate:** K12 is an argument with published responses. Neither nationalist antiquity claims nor a blanket “all invented” verdict can replace checking a particular text and its uses.
- **Religion:** Dr07 and PT04 concern different cult histories and regions; U12/U16 summarize nominated heritage practices. UNESCO inscription does not certify primordial origins, national unanimity, or an unchanged pantheon.
- **Village:** Lu10 is a localized longitudinal study, not permission to extrapolate identical offices, covenants, gender access, or ritual precedence across Vietnam. Historical specimen-level village documents remain explicitly unverified here.
- **Colonial archive:** administrative categories, translated terms, posed photographs, and elite informants can distort rural life; record author, purpose, locality, date, and mediation, and compare local-language evidence before generalizing.
- **Costume:** Le03’s scholarship resists a timeless national-dress story; its online companion is a summary, not a substitute for primary decrees, surviving garments, patterns, or period fashion journalism. Exact priority claims remain flagged above.
- **Thin coverage:** detailed minority traditions, early non-elite clothing, premodern spoken village registers, and some regional cult variants are not established by this source set; leave them open, not filled with plausible-sounding lore.
- **Recheck trigger:** before a specific historical reconstruction or close cultural adaptation, obtain the named manuscript/object/site evidence and a Vietnamese-language specialist review; keep the distinction between reference, inference, and invented option visible.
