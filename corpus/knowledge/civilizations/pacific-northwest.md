## Regional scope: an interaction zone, not a single civilization

“Northwest Coast” names a comparative region spanning many sovereign peoples; “Pacific Northwest” also has broader modern geographical meanings. This entry uses the former cultural-historical scope, with explicit northern neighbors where poles and Chilkat demand them. (Suttles 1990; Jonaitis and Glass 2010.)

| Reference unit | Language position | Coupling to investigate | Conflation to reject |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kwakwaka'wakw communities | Kwak'wala, Northern Wakashan | Names, ranked positions, ancestral privileges, bighouse performance, gifts to witnesses | Historical “Kwakiutl” labels do not identify every Wakashan people |
| Haisla; Heiltsuk/Oowekyala communities | Other Northern Wakashan languages/varieties | Local clan/house organization and crest narratives; Haisla examples below | A shared language branch does not guarantee identical descent or ceremony |
| Nuu-chah-nulth, Ditidaht, Makah | Southern Wakashan | Investigate local hereditary authority, marine knowledge, and ceremonial vocabulary | Do not assign Kwakwaka'wakw speech sequences or northern clan templates automatically |
| Coast Salish communities | Several Salishan languages, including Halkomelem, Lushootseed, Squamish | Kin networks, place histories, naming, wool/fiber technologies, ceremonial witnessing | “Coast Salish” is neither one language nor all Salishan peoples |
| Interior Salish communities | Other Salishan branches | Distinct inland ecologies and histories | Coast and interior are not interchangeable costume sets |
| Tlingit, Haida, Tsimshianic-speaking neighbors | Tlingit is Na-Dene; Haida is generally treated as an isolate; Tsimshianic is separate | Northern crest monuments, clan histories, and textile exchange; Chilkat particularly associated with Tlingit weavers | Chilkat is not evidence that its makers speak Wakashan or Salishan |

- Calibration: linguistic descent, political membership, artistic circulation, and marriage networks are different maps. Borrowed regalia need not imply borrowed grammar or identical myth.
- **Coverage boundary:** this entry is strongest on public Kwakwaka'wakw ceremonial teaching and northern weaving. Southern Wakashan and Coast Salish material here rests on regional and collaborative references rather than on community-specific ceremonial documentation: Arima and Dewhirst (1990, pp. 391–411), Renker and Gunther (1990), and McMillan (1999) for Nuu-chah-nulth/Ditidaht/Makah; Gustafson (1980) and Tepper, George, and Joseph (2017) for Salish weaving. Individual southern Wakashan and Salish speech registers remain outside this entry's claims.

## Language families, writing, and a usable design profile

| Dimension | Reference knowledge | Fictional-culture operation |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy | Wakashan and Salishan are distinct established families; geographical resemblance does not establish a combined family (Mithun 1999) | Draw a family tree independently of a trade/ritual diffusion map |
| Sound | Glottalized consonants and substantial dorsal consonant contrasts occur in the region; inventories differ by language (Mithun 1999) | Choose an inventory and phonotactics, not apostrophes sprinkled onto English |
| Word formation | Rich morphological organization, reduplication, and suffixing offer comparative leads; Salish lexical suffixes are especially useful to study (Mithun 1999) | Decide what a stem and suffix actually contribute before creating names or material terms |
| Category analysis | Salish noun/verb analyses are language-specific; slogans such as “a language without nouns” are not a reliable design brief | Specify predication, reference, and derivation rather than deleting a word class by fiat |
| Writing | Current community writing uses language-specific orthographies; U'mista publishes a Kwak'wala alphabet (U'mista n.d.) | Keep phonemic notation, practical spelling, and an outsider's transcription distinct |
| Contact | Chinook Jargon/Chinuk Wawa is a contact language; the widespread word *potlatch* comes through it (U'mista n.d.) | Give multilingual ports their own exchange vocabulary; do not make a trade language the ancestor of every local language |
| Records | Crest objects, spoken histories, songs, and witnessed performances can preserve knowledge without being an alphabet (U'mista n.d.; Jonaitis and Glass 2010) | Specify what an object prompts a trained narrator to recall, rather than pretending every figure translates to a sentence |

- Language-level feature bundles, productive affix meanings, stress patterns, and reconstructed historical pronunciations are not supplied here. A proposed real-language implementation must be checked against a language-specific reference — for Kwak'wala, Grubb (1977) together with the community orthography U'mista publishes (n.d.) — rather than inferred from the regional survey above.
- Modern written forms are neither proof of precontact alphabetic literacy nor a measure of intellectual complexity. Avoid ranking oral institutions beneath literate bureaucracies.
- Coupling test: can an invented material term be derived in the ordinary language, borrowed through trade, then given a restricted ceremonial use without changing its basic phonology?

## Mythic core: ancestral encounters and rights, not a portable pantheon

| Local reference | Publicly supportable pattern | What the pattern couples |
|---|---|---|
| Kwakwaka'wakw ceremonial teaching | Ancestors, inherited stories, songs, dances, animal-spirit relations, and publicly acknowledged privileges are linked (U'mista n.d.) | Narrative authority → named holders → permitted performance → witnesses |
| Haisla example | Wilson's public *Raven and the Fisherman* teaching features Wigit/Raven, supernatural fisherman Kwaganoo, and knowledge of a halibut hook (Bill Reid Centre n.d., Lesson 1) | Appetite/trickery → encounter → acquisition of technique; this is a named version, not “the Raven myth” |
| Coast Salish comparison | Transformer traditions and ancestral accounts connect places, encounters, and present relationships (Suttles 1990) | Landscape name → remembered transformation → local history; exact figures and variants require local sourcing |
| Northern neighbors | Crests can refer to ancestral/supernatural histories rather than an abstract deity's universal jurisdiction (Jonaitis and Glass 2010; Bill Reid Centre n.d.) | Kin history → image → monument or regalia → recognition |

- **Pantheon calibration:** neither “Raven = god of deception” nor a chart of sea/sky/war gods adequately describes this region. A figure can have different narrative, ancestral, ceremonial, and crest functions in different communities.
- **Genre calibration:** do not classify every ancestral account as entertaining fiction, or every animal narrative as a sacred charter. Identify teller, audience, ownership, permission, and occasion before assigning a genre.
- Treat “myth authorizes property” as an analytical relationship, not an assertion that Indigenous histories were invented to manipulate ownership.
- Fictional method: specify an encounter's consequences—who can narrate it, inherit a name, use an image, or approach a place—without copying a family's story or ceremonial sequence.

## Potlatch ceremonial economy: wealth becomes witnessed standing

The strongest documented coupling here is **resources + named rights + authorized display + public acknowledgment**, not “everyone gives away everything.” U'mista describes guests as witnesses receiving gifts for acknowledging inheritance or claims. Ceremonies also mourn, comfort, celebrate marriages, confer names, open houses, and raise poles. (U'mista n.d.)

| Component | Documented function | Design consequence, not a historical formula |
|---|---|---|
| Host and supporting family | Preparation can take years; feast and gifts require coordinated work | Trace procurement, storage, skilled labor, obligations, and transport before prestige |
| Guests/witnesses | Gifts recognize their role in acknowledging transfers and claims | A proclamation needs a socially competent audience, not merely loud delivery |
| Names and privileges | Leadership names, songs, dances, designs, and use-rights may be passed on | Separate ownership of a physical object from authority to perform or display it |
| Food and distributions | Feasting and giving operate within social relationships | Goods are not anonymous currency detached from giver, recipient, and occasion |
| Coppers | Named, historically valued objects express wealth and transactions | Keep an object's biography and publicly known valuation, not just metal weight |
| Mourning and succession | Loss, remembrance, consolation, and renewed standing can coexist | A transfer can have emotional, legal, economic, and narrative consequences together |

- Competitive copper-breaking and rivalry are documented, but they do not define every potlatch. U'mista explicitly says breaking coppers in the ceremonial bighouse is not permitted today because it is hostile; do not universalize destructive competition across periods.
- Likewise, “redistribution,” “gift exchange,” and “prestige economy” are partial analytical lenses, not interchangeable total explanations. Test which rights are transferred, what remains inalienable, and what the guests actually acknowledge.
- Neighboring ceremonial economies differ in descent, rank, hosting, and witnessing. Do not make one Kwakwaka'wakw account the operating constitution of all Coast Salish or northern communities. (Suttles 1990.)
- Explanatory brake: some Northwest Coast historical societies included hereditary inequality and enslavement; generosity does not prove egalitarianism. The local form and period must be sourced rather than romanticized. (Suttles 1990.)

## Potlatch speech registers: authority is an event, not ornamental diction

**Evidence distinction:** U'mista documents speeches within potlatches. Boas/Hunt (1921:788–793) records a particular Kwag'ul **feast** speech sequence, not a universal potlatch script. Use the latter as specific evidence for ceremonial oratory; do not silently equate feast, potlatch, and winter initiation.

| Register/genre operation | Attested evidence or explicit limit | Worldbuilding variable |
|---|---|---|
| Delegated voice | The chief's speaker addresses the gathering; host and guest speakers have distinct turns (Boas/Hunt 1921:788–793) | Who speaks, and whose authority makes the utterance count? |
| Ranked naming | The account explicitly explains the order of chiefly names and omits certain hosts from that naming sequence (pp. 792–793) | Address order can assert precedence independently of who paid for the meal |
| Spatial deixis | Guests are invited to “the seats of your late fathers” (English translation, p. 788) | Seating, inherited position, and ancestral continuity become mutually legible |
| Parallel address | Repeated welcomes, titles, and imperatives organize the recorded speech (pp. 788–791) | Repetition marks participation and social structure rather than merely verbosity |
| Embodied audience design | Speakers turn between their own group and guests; songs and food frame turns (pp. 788–791) | Direction, pause, seating, and response belong in a speech-event specification |
| Public transfer announcement | Potlatches acknowledge names, inheritance, and claims before witnesses (U'mista n.d.) | Separate announcing a claim, accepting it, and distributing recognition gifts |
| Restricted performance | Ownership of stories, songs, and dances is documented; exact access rules differ (U'mista n.d.) | Access restriction is not evidence of a separate secret grammar |

- **Do not invent linguistic evidence:** no source consulted for this entry documents a distinctive ceremonial phonology, an honorific affix paradigm, archaic verb paradigms, or a uniquely “potlatch tense” in Kwak'wala. The recorded feast speeches support register *use* — delegated voice, ranked naming, parallel address — without grammatical annotation of such forms; the documented special-speech evidence in the Boas–Hunt corpus concerns the narrative style used for myth recitation (Berman 1996, quoted in secondary discussion), not a ceremonial tense. A design asserting any such form carries the burden of proof against a grammar.
- The English translation is evidence for the published account's structure, not a license to reverse-engineer an authentic Kwak'wala sentence. Historical transcription and OCR must be checked against the scan before quoting native-language forms.
- For fiction, build a register matrix: **speaker role / principal represented / named addressees / claim type / admissible evidence / response / material action / sanction**.
- A usable fictional scene schema is: establish competence → identify the claim and its history → present permitted evidence → obtain acknowledgment → enact transfer/distribution. This is a design abstraction, not a description of a mandatory Indigenous order.
- Give ordinary work speech, diplomatic exchange, mourning, public oratory, and owned performance different participation rules; do not render all dialogue as an unvarying mystical chant.

## Totem poles, heraldry, and calibration of “totemism”

**Heraldry is a limited analogy:** a crest can identify ancestry, privileges, and standing, but Northwest Coast systems are not European coats of arms with substitute animals. The monumental pole is one medium among housefronts, posts, masks, boxes, garments, and performances. (Jonaitis and Glass 2010; U'mista n.d.)

| Question | Calibrated answer | Error to block |
|---|---|---|
| What does a crest mean? | A particular image has a local history and authorized users | “Bear always means bravery,” regardless of owner or narrative |
| What does a pole do? | House-related, memorial, mortuary, and other functions depend on the pole and community | A single “tribal idol” category for every carved column |
| Can it be read? | Recognition often depends on oral knowledge; figures are not a universal sentence code | A top-to-bottom alphabetic translation or universal rank hierarchy |
| Is it worshipped? | A pole is not simply an idol; histories and sacred relationships cannot be inferred from shape alone | Equating representational art with worship of every depicted animal |
| How old is the form? | Specific monumental traditions have histories; surviving wood and early outsider records are incomplete | Assigning a precise invention date from the oldest photograph |
| Does “totemism” unify it? | Lévi-Strauss (1963) challenged the supposed unity of animal classification, kinship, taboo, and religion | Treating a discredited universal evolutionary package as a civilization generator |

- **Calibration status:** reject “totemism” as a self-explanatory primitive stage; retain carefully defined local relationships between people, species, names, and images. Lévi-Strauss's critique does not erase actual Indigenous kinship or spiritual relations.
- The word *totem* reaches this discourse from an Anishinaabe/Ojibwe linguistic context outside the Northwest Coast; “totem pole” is a conventional external label, not a local-language technical analysis. (Jonaitis and Glass 2010.)
- Northern formline vocabulary includes ovoids and U-forms, thickening/thinning lines, and related internal divisions. Bill Reid Centre's Lesson 2 is specifically framed as **northern** design; do not export it unchanged to all Coast Salish visual traditions.
- Differentiate the right to commission, carve, own, display, narrate, and inherit. An artist's technical competence is not automatically the owner's ceremonial authority.
- Fictional application: design an original visual grammar and an authorization system together. Ask what happens when an image is copied without its history or displayed by someone without standing.

## Chilkat weaving: technology makes a wearable charter possible

Chilkat is a northern weaving tradition particularly associated with Tlingit practice and circulating beyond one community; it is not a generic Wakashan/Salishan costume. Sealaska's public accounts connect the robe to clan *at.óow* and living teaching lineages. Keep this northern comparison explicit. (Sealaska Heritage Institute 2023; Suttles 1990.)

| Technical layer | Evidence and boundary | Coupled design constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Fiber supply | Mountain goat wool and yellow cedar bark are documented traditional materials (SHI 2023) | Ecology, access, collection, and processing precede elite display |
| Preparation | Spinning warp, fiber processing, dyeing, and warping are described in current teaching projects (SHI 2023, 2026) | Textile vocabulary needs process verbs and graded materials, not just garment nouns |
| Structural yarn | Museum records and technical accounts agree on a warp of cedar-bark cord wrapped or spun with wool and a weft of wool yarn (Penn Museum NA1285; National Museum of Finland 2020; Samuel 1982); how much bark enters a given warp varies by weaver and robe, and catalogue descriptions fix no recipe | A composite yarn can divide strength, flexibility, and surface appearance between materials |
| Fabric formation | Finger-twining, not a printed image applied afterward, produces the patterned cloth (SHI 2023) | Skill resides in fabric structure as well as graphic design |
| Curved imagery | Woven ovoids and other curvilinear forms are characteristic; they are difficult and time-consuming (SHI 2023) | Curves require technical choices; do not assume any weave can reproduce any visual grammar |
| Equipment and sequencing | Documented practice: the warp fastens only to the top of an upright frame and hangs free (some accounts add bottom weights for tension), twining proceeds in small vertical sections rather than in side-to-side rows, and the design is transferred from a painted pattern board into a center-plus-two-side-panel layout (Handwoven 2020; Burke Museum 2010; Samuel 1982). This entry still issues no weaving recipe | Support frame, tension, sequence, and design transfer must be specified together |
| Labor | SHI (2023) says an adult robe can take a skilled weaver a year or longer; not a fixed production standard | Calendar, apprenticeship, patronage, and inherited resources become visible in costume |
| Movement and completion | Fringe/finishing and a First Dance are part of Hope's documented mentorship context (SHI 2023) | A robe's social completion need not coincide with the last structural stitch |
| Material change | SHI (2026) links reduced goat-wool use to colonial suppression, disease, and commercial wool availability | Substitution can express survival and continuity, not automatic loss of authenticity |

- Distinguish Chilkat from Ravenstail: both are finger-twined styles in Hope's teaching, not interchangeable labels. Ravenstail is the older northern geometric style — geometric designs, mountain goat wool — that died out in the early nineteenth century; Chilkat is the later technique whose twining could turn curves, making formline designs weavable, with warps combining wool and cedar bark and longer fringe (Alaska State Museum exhibit coverage, Alaska DEED n.d.; Samuel 1987). A shared region and technique family do not make one pattern library: derive no Chilkat design from a Ravenstail robe, or the reverse.
- Do not impose an immutable male-designer/female-weaver division from older ethnography: SHI documents Lily Hope as both designer and weaver, and contemporary teaching continues to develop.
- “All mountain goat wool” in the 2026 project describes a stated fiber objective; do not infer that structural cedar disappears or that the planned robe has already been completed.
- Safe abstraction: **rare materials → long training → technically demanding image → authorized wearer → public recognition**. Change the materials, imagery, and institutions when designing a fictional system.

## Costume/material signatures: differentiate media and eras

| Reference medium | Historically situated signature | Required distinction |
|---|---|---|
| Cedar regalia | U'mista describes cedar shawls, hats, headgear, and dance aprons | Processing, body placement, and ceremonial use differ; bark is not interchangeable with loom cloth |
| Button blanket | Button blankets occur in U'mista's contemporary potlatch teaching; trade wool cloth and buttons place this form in colonial-era exchange histories (Suttles 1990) | Do not project the same construction unchanged into a precontact scene |
| Chilkat robe | Finger-twined northern prestige/ceremonial textile with woven curvilinear design (SHI 2023) | Not an appliquéd button blanket and not every Northwest Coast blanket |
| Coast Salish comparison | Coast Salish weaving has its own fiber base — mountain goat wool plus the fleece of the woolly dog, a now-extinct breed kept for its hair and displaced with the trade in manufactured blankets — its own loom and blanket traditions, and a collaborative technical literature (Lin et al. 2023; Gustafson 1980; Tepper, George, and Joseph 2017) | Do not dress every Coast Salish figure in a northern crest robe; take Salish textile detail from Salish sources |
| Masks, coppers, rattles, staffs | Regalia and held objects can display family histories or privileges (U'mista n.d.) | A visible object may participate in a right rather than be a freely exchangeable accessory |
| Trade and current gifts | U'mista contrasts earlier furs, canoes, and cedar blankets with later manufactured blankets and current household goods | A changed object inventory need not mean a ceremony has ceased to be Indigenous |

- Costume checklist: **maker / material source / production sequence / owner / authorized wearer / occasion / associated story or song / storage / repair / succession**. Missing fields identify research tasks, not invitations to invent history.

## Era model: asynchronous contact, coercion, continuity, and renewal

These are staging lenses, not a universal periodization or a ladder from “primitive” to “civilized.” Local archaeology, Indigenous histories, and colonial calendars do not begin at the same point. (Suttles 1990; Jonaitis and Glass 2010.)

| Staging lens | Historical anchors and limits | Language–symbol–costume interaction |
|---|---|---|
| Long local histories before sustained European contact | No single origin date is established here for rank, crests, ceremonies, or textiles | Do not extrapolate a photographed nineteenth-century ensemble unchanged into deep time |
| Expanding maritime/colonial exchange, late eighteenth–nineteenth centuries | Contact, epidemic disruption, trade, and imposed institutions occur unevenly | Borrowed terms and imported materials can coexist with inherited names and privileges |
| Canadian criminalization | Potlatch prohibition legislated in 1884, effective 1885; enforcement changed over time (U'mista n.d.) | Suppression targets gatherings, gifts, speeches, and regalia together; not “religion” in isolation |
| Cranmer potlatch and confiscation, 1921–1922 | Dan Cranmer's potlatch was followed by prosecutions and removal of ceremonial belongings (U'mista n.d.) | Museum labels may separate objects from owners, performance rights, and remembered words |
| Removal of prohibition, 1951, and later repatriation | U'mista documents continued practice, later public confidence, and long return negotiations | Legal change is not an instant reset of language transmission or material possession |
| Living practice and teaching, late twentieth century–present | U'mista's public teaching and SHI's 2023/2026 projects document continuity and active reconstruction | Orthographies, digital teaching, commercial yarn, and new commissions are not evidence of a frozen past |

- Canadian legislation is not the law of the United States; Tlingit/Alaska histories require their own legal chronology. SHI's 2026 account describes American assimilation pressures, not extension of Canada's potlatch statute.
- Sparse surviving wood, uneven archaeological preservation, translated oral accounts, and selective colonial records constrain precise dating. Earliest surviving specimen, earliest outsider report, and community origin account answer different questions.

## Naming practices: names can be inherited positions

- U'mista explicitly links naming children and passing chiefly titles/names to potlatch acknowledgment. A name can participate in rights and public standing, not just identify a private individual. (U'mista n.d.)
- Boas/Hunt's feast account connects ordered chiefly names, ancestral seats, and the distinction between title-holders and a particular host (1921:788–793); this is local evidence, not a pan-coastal naming law.
- Model separate fields for **person / current name / prior names / title or position / transmitting group / occasion of transfer / witnesses / permitted forms of address**.
- Do not convert every translation of a long name into a surname or a universal “animal + adjective” formula. Productive name morphology and individual translations are language-specific and are not supplied here; check any proposed name against a language-specific dictionary and grammar (for Kwak'wala, Grubb 1977) instead of against English naming habits.
- Kin/clan/house labels, place names, chiefly names, ceremonial names, and an outsider's catalogue name need not coincide. Record who uses each label and in which setting.
- Fictional diagnostic: can a successor inherit a title while an object, song, or use-right passes under a different rule? If yes, specify the divergence rather than assuming every asset moves as a single bundle.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Coupled authority:** design names, images, garments, stories, and speech roles as interlocking but separately transferable entitlements; do not copy Indigenous crests or restricted performances.
- **Public memory:** an event's efficacy can depend on trained speakers and recognized witnesses; objects support remembering without being a phonetic script.
- **Technical causation:** derive display from fiber ecology, yarn/fabric structure, labor, training, and movement; a prestige textile is not just an illustration pasted onto clothing.
- **Historical layering:** stage imported materials, altered audiences, suppressed gatherings, renewed teaching, and inherited claims on separate timelines; continuity does not require unchanged objects.
- **Working handoff to lore-shokunin:** provide a source-tagged constraint set, competing fictional options, transfer rules, a speech-event matrix, and era-specific material limits; leave names, histories, and canon selection to the world-bible owner.

## Provenance cautions

- Colonial ethnography is indispensable evidence but not a neutral camera. Attribute Hunt's contribution rather than treating Boas as the sole voice; collector categories, translation, unequal access, and suppression shape the archive.
- U'mista and Sealaska offer living community authority in their own contexts, not a warrant to generalize all coastal peoples or treat public teaching as unrestricted reproduction permission.
- “Totemism” as a universal primitive religion is rejected here; “heraldry,” “property,” “pantheon,” and “register” remain bounded comparison terms whose local fit must be demonstrated.
- This note's weakest areas are precise precontact chronology, southern Wakashan local variation, individual Salish speech genres, and fine Chilkat structural procedures. These limits are stated in place as evidence boundaries; downstream summaries must not drop them.
- No complete precontact chronicle or transcript of a pan-Northwest-Coast ceremony is claimed. Where evidence is scarce or rights are unclear, keep the proposed reconstruction conditional rather than inventing a tradition.
