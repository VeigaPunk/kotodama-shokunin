## Evidence ladder: do not turn a ritual archive into a civilization census
| Evidence class | Strong use | Limit to carry into design |
|---|---|---|
| Late-Shang oracle inscriptions | Royal concerns, diviners, dated charges, named recipients of ritual | Formulaic, selective, and elite-centered; not a complete spoken-language sample or myth handbook |
| Excavated tomb assemblages | Objects deposited together; associations between bodies, offerings, and materials | Burial display is not identical to daily dress, ownership in life, or the whole population |
| Western Zhou bronze inscriptions | Commissioning, ancestors, awards, appointments, and remembered service | Commemorative ritual documents with interested patrons, not neutral administrative transcripts |
| Transmitted poetry and ritual literature | Later remembrance, legitimating narratives, normative categories | Composition, collection, editing, and commentary have different dates |
| Unprovenanced collection objects | Comparative form, manufacture, and iconography | Stylistic attribution cannot supply a missing tomb, owner, or excavated costume context |
- Working method: **genre–medium triangulation**—ask what a bone record, bronze dedication, burial deposit, and later poem each make visible and each conceal. This is a craft heuristic, not a named Shang doctrine. [K78; S91; R95; LF08]
- Chronicle evidence is especially thin for nonelite speech, local narrative religion, and daily clothing; gaps below are boundaries, not invitations to invent historical facts.

## Era staging: related traditions, not one timeless “ancient China”
| Stage | Approximate placement | Coupled change worth modeling |
|---|---|---|
| Early Shang / Erligang archaeological horizon | Broadly second millennium BCE, before the main Anyang archive | Monumental centers and bronze production; archaeological distribution must not automatically become a map of one uniform ethnic or linguistic population |
| Late Shang / Anyang | 13th–11th centuries BCE, ending in the mid-11th-century conquest | Extensive oracle archive; royal ancestor ritual; bronze and jade assemblages such as Fu Hao’s |
| Western Zhou | Conquest conventionally c. 1046/1045–771 BCE | New ruling house, royal commissions, regional establishments, ancestral bronzes, and a developing language of Heaven’s mandate |
| Eastern Zhou | Conventionally 770–256 BCE for the Zhou royal house | Changed political scale and regional traditions; not a license to project every later ritual prescription backward |
- Dates are scholarly coordinates, not uniformly secure ancient labels: LF08 explicitly adopts one reconstruction, including alternative first-year reckonings for several kings. Do not silently mix it with another chronology. [LF08, p. xv]
- DB99’s oracle handout gives particular Wu Ding reign dates; this entry uses its translated inscriptions, not those dates as a settled universal chronology.
- Separate an **archaeological horizon**, **royal dynasty**, **linguistic stage**, and **costume phase** in the working database; their boundaries need not coincide.
- Fictional staging rule: make conquest change titles and legitimacy claims before assuming that every sound, garment, or ancestor rite changes at once.

## Language family and writing: an attested horizon, not an invention scene
- The inscriptions record early Chinese, in the Sinitic branch of the Sino-Tibetan family, also termed Trans-Himalayan in some scholarship; deeper branching and reconstructed details remain debated. [BS14]
- “Old Chinese” covers a reconstructed historical stage and multiple kinds of evidence, not one fully recoverable Shang court pronunciation.
- Modern pinyin in this entry is a finding aid: *wáng*, *Dì*, *dǐng*, and *fēngjiàn* are not claims about Bronze Age sounds. Do not import modern Mandarin tones or syllable constraints wholesale. [BS14]
- The late-Shang oracle corpus is the earliest extensive, securely readable Chinese writing corpus; its mature conventions do **not** date the invention of writing to the first surviving bone. [K78; Q00]
- Describe the system as **morphosyllabic/logographic**, with phonetic loans and sound-bearing components as well as pictorially motivated graphs; “pictures expressing universal ideas” is a misleading model. [Q00]
- Neolithic marks are not, merely by resemblance, a deciphered continuous ancestry for a particular Shang word; any proposed sign-to-character chain needs dated contextual evidence (to-verify).
- Inscribed bone and shell, and cast bronze texts, are media with different production constraints; neither is simply an evolutionary rung replacing the other. [Q00; S91]
- For a fictional analogue, distinguish graph, lexical reading, pronunciation, and material technique; one can remain conservative while another changes.
- Use incising and casting to constrain line, scale, layout, revision, and access to specialists—not to infer a population-wide literacy percentage.
- Required record fields: graph image/reference; normalized transcription; proposed reading; translation; uncertainty; medium; provenance; date scheme. Never make a modern printed character look like a facsimile of an oracle graph.

## Divination as a language–power workflow
- **Pyro-osteomancy:** prepared cattle scapulae or turtle plastrons were heated to produce cracks interpreted in divination; selected records were inscribed. Terminology describes a technique, not a claim that every rite had one identical procedure. [K78; DB99]
| Record component | Typical informational work | Fictional design lever |
|---|---|---|
| Preface | Cyclical day and often the diviner | Who controls scheduling and whose expertise enters the archive? |
| Charge | Proposition concerning an outcome or action | Which uncertainties may be publicly formulated? |
| Prognostication | Interpretation, sometimes attributed to the king | Who has authority to turn signs into a decision? |
| Verification | Subsequent outcome, when recorded | Can an archive preserve failure as well as success? |
- Components are analytical categories, not mandatory slots present in every inscription; positive/negative paired charges occur but are not an invariant template. [K78; DB99]
- DB99’s inscriptions concern illness, settlement building, harvest, and enemy action: “religion” and practical government are not separate departments in this evidence.
- Treat the charge as a proposition rather than automatically translating every entry into an ordinary spoken question.
- Operational coupling: scheduled ritual → restricted utterance → authorized interpretation → durable record → later ancestral memory; label this sequence a design abstraction.
- Do not present divination as either meaningless superstition or a proven supernatural technology; those are external judgments, not what the evidence itself establishes.

## Mythic and pantheon core: ancestors, Di, and Heaven
| Term or relationship | Evidence-grounded anchor | Calibration |
|---|---|---|
| Royal ancestors | Named deceased kin could be addressed and implicated in benefit or harm | Ancestorhood was differentiated and politically consequential, not one anonymous “ancestor spirit” |
| **Dì 帝** | Oracle charges connect Di with harvest, settlement approval, and hostile action | “High god” is a translation aid; agency is better evidenced here than a complete divine biography |
| **Shàngdì 上帝** | Important designation in transmitted early Chinese religious language | Keep actual wording and textual date visible; do not silently replace every oracle *Di* with a later theological formula |
| **Tiān 天**, Heaven | Zhou legitimation invokes Heaven and its mandate | Not merely modern impersonal “nature,” and not automatically a second name for a European-style creator |
| King Wen and dynastic mandate | Ode 235 places Wen near the high deity and warns that the mandate can be lost | A transmitted legitimating poem, not a stenographic record of the conquest |
- DB99 oracle excerpts attribute harm to particular ancestors and to Di; this supports a relational field of obligation and danger, not a neatly departmentalized Olympian pantheon.
- Ode 235 uses both Heaven and high-deity language: a simple “Shang god abolished; Zhou god substituted” story is inadequate. The historical relationship of Di, Shangdi, and Tian remains contested. [DB99]
- **Tiānmìng 天命**, conventionally “Mandate of Heaven,” can make lineage succession morally conditional; do not reduce it to a mechanical rule that every disaster proves deposition.
- The warning that mandate is difficult to keep is explicit in Ode 235; distinguish that political theology from a later fully systematized philosophy. [DB99, p. 38]
- A continuous Shang creation narrative, full divine kinship chart, or universal popular creed cannot be reconstructed from the small selection used here (to-verify).
- This entry does not establish Pangu, Nüwa, or the Jade Emperor as members of a late-Shang court pantheon; importing familiar later figures requires separate, dated evidence.
- Craft move: give a ruling group rights to address particular dead, while leaving subjects’ local powers and narratives as a separately researched layer—not a blank space called “folk superstition.”

## Bronze ding: provisioning ancestors, displaying power
| Form or feature | Historical anchor | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **Dǐng 鼎** | Food/cooking vessel type; round examples commonly have three legs | Sacrificial provisioning links food, lineage, metal resources, and public authority |
| **Fāngdǐng 方鼎** | Rectangular ding, commonly four-legged; examples occur in Fu Hao’s tomb | “Ding = tripod” is too narrow; form labels require an object reference |
| Ancestral dedication | Vessels may name a maker, ancestor, or remembered royal favor | Material object becomes a portable claim to service, succession, and remembrance |
| Award or appointment record | Western Zhou commissions can commemorate royal authorization | Text, regalia, office, and ancestor rite can legitimate the same person |
| Frontal animal-mask ornament | Often called **tāotiè 饕餮** in later naming practice | A visible pattern is not an automatically deciphered god, clan, or moral allegory |
- Fu Hao’s inscribed ritual vessels provide a concrete bridge between a living named person, posthumous ancestral designations, and a deposited material assemblage. [C21]
- Ding are part of broader vessel ensembles; avoid making one silhouette stand for all bronze ritual functions. [S91; C21]
- Metal procurement, casting labor, patronage, and the ability to provision sacrifices matter alongside iconography; “power symbol” should explain an institution, not just an intimidating prop.
- **Contested:** animal-mask meanings and “shamanic” interpretations are not settled by modern recognition of faces or animals; a particular divine identification remains (to-verify).
- “Nine ding = universal Shang royal inventory” and a fixed 9/7/5 rank ladder must not be treated as excavated laws for all periods; any numerical prescription needs a dated text and regional assemblage (to-verify).
- Later antiquarian vessel names are useful classification terms, but their use does not establish what every Shang maker called the form. [S91]
- Keep **dǐng 鼎**, vessel, distinct from **dīng 丁**, calendrical stem; modern romanization without tones obscures a crucial difference.

## Jade biography: value survives a dynasty
- Use **object biography** as a craft worksheet: manufacture → circulation → repair/reworking → bodily or ritual use → inheritance/transfer → deposition → modern collecting. This is an analytical method, not an ancient doctrinal sequence.
- Fu Hao’s tomb included jades attributed to Neolithic manufacture as well as contemporary objects; an old object in a later burial does not imply manufacture during the burial’s dynasty. [C21; R95]
- Heirloom, exchange, acquisition, and seizure are possible transfer routes, not interchangeable proven histories for each piece; a specific ownership chain remains (to-verify) without independent evidence.
| Observation | Safe inference | Unsafe leap |
|---|---|---|
| Excavated jade pendant with a dressed human figure | Elite assemblage included portable human imagery | Portrait of the tomb owner, complete uniform, or named deity |
| Recutting or added perforation, if documented | A changed attachment or use history | A named conquest or marriage that “must” explain the alteration |
| Jade blade in a burial | A blade-shaped valued object was deposited | A practical battle weapon or one universal ritual meaning |
| Form with older parallels | A chronological comparison worth testing | An unbroken, documented family inheritance |
- SM’s kneeling pendant has an excavated Fu Hao tomb context; preserve that association rather than substituting a similar market object with an invented provenance.
- Jade is not synonymous with emerald green; MM’s blade is identified as nephrite. Identify material and condition before deriving a symbolic palette.
- MM’s label associates jade properties with integrity and honesty; that broad cultural explanation is **not** by itself evidence that those exact moral correspondences were articulated for this object in the Western Zhou.
- Likewise, later *bi* disc = Heaven / *cong* tube = Earth formulas need a dated textual argument before being made the original meaning of every Neolithic or Shang specimen (to-verify).
- Fictional application: let an inherited ornament retain an archaic name while a new court recasts its origin story; record material history and claimed history separately.

## Fengjian vocabulary: kinship and delegation, not transplanted Europe
- **Fēngjiàn 封建**, often rendered “enfeoffment” or “feudalism,” is an interpretive trap when it imports European vassalage, land tenure, or a universal stage of social evolution. [LF03; LF08]
- LF08 models Western Zhou as kin-ordered and settlement-based, with governmental offices and delegation; this is a scholarly model, not the final verdict on every region.
| Label | Working gloss | Do not assume |
|---|---|---|
| **Wáng 王** | King; central royal designation | A Qin-style *huángdì* emperor or modern nation-state sovereign |
| **Fēng 封 / jiàn 建** | Establishing/enfeoffing; setting up | One timeless constitutional package wherever either verb occurs |
| **Hóu 侯** | Conventionally marquis; LF08 prefers “Ruler” | Exact equivalence to a European marquis |
| **Bó 伯** | Elder; conventionally earl/count in some translations | A title unrelated to seniority or kinship language |
| **Gōng 公 / zǐ 子 / nán 男** | Context-sensitive titles, with conventional later rank translations | A fully standardized five-grade ladder across Shang and Zhou |
| **Yì 邑 / bāng 邦** | Settlement/domain; polity, context dependent | Borders, citizenship, and sovereignty identical to a modern state |
- Follow LF08’s convention of retaining or carefully glossing titles rather than automatically translating the whole set as duke–marquis–count–viscount–baron. [pp. xiii–xiv]
- Distinguish a settlement or regional establishment, an appointed office, a kin group, and a ritual position; a person can occupy several without those categories being synonyms.
- Craft move: define what a ruler actually grants—people, settlement, office, ceremonial equipment, service obligation, or precedence—before naming a fictional “fief.”
- For each rank word, record speaker, addressee, inscription genre, date, region, and whether the interpretation comes from a later commentator.

## Cyclical dating: calendar labels become ritual identities
- The **gānzhī 干支** sexagenary sequence combines ten stems with twelve branches, advancing both together; the cycle contains 60 paired labels, not all 120 possible combinations. [K78; DB99]
- Stems, in modern transcription: 甲 jiǎ; 乙 yǐ; 丙 bǐng; 丁 dīng; 戊 wù; 己 jǐ; 庚 gēng; 辛 xīn; 壬 rén; 癸 guǐ.
- Branches: 子 zǐ; 丑 chǒu; 寅 yín; 卯 mǎo; 辰 chén; 巳 sì; 午 wǔ; 未 wèi; 申 shēn; 酉 yǒu; 戌 xū; 亥 hài.
- Reproducible design rule: for zero-based day `n`, select stem `n mod 10` and branch `n mod 12`; day 1 = 甲子 jiǎzǐ, day 2 = 乙丑 yǐchǒu, day 60 = 癸亥 guǐhài, then repeat.
- DB99’s settlement charges provide attested adjacent labels: 壬子 rénzǐ, day 49, and 癸丑 guǐchǒu, day 50; use these as a check on ordering, not invented “ancient quotations.”
- A recurrent day-name is not an absolute date. Keep month, regnal context, provenance, and chronological scheme separate; Western Zhou dating problems cannot be solved by a day pair alone. [S91; LF08, p. xv]
- Stem designations also occur in Shang royal ancestral names; **Xin 辛** in Fu Hao’s posthumous designation connects personal remembrance with ritual scheduling. [K78; C21]
- Do not turn an ancestral stem-name into a modern birthday, civil surname, or a claim that all people bore calendrical personal names.
- Early day-counting evidence does not automatically establish the familiar animal zodiac, later five-phase correspondences, or a fully developed year-based horoscope for that era.
- Fictional handoff must specify cycle anchor, rollover, whether labels mark days or years, and who maintains the ritual calendar; these are design choices, not historical facts inferred from one inscription.

## Costume and material signature: reconstruct the body cautiously
| Evidence or design layer | Usable anchor | Required restraint |
|---|---|---|
| Late-Shang human imagery | Fu Hao tomb jade figures, including SM’s pendant | A represented clothed body is not a complete sewing pattern or necessarily a portrait |
| Adornment and attachment | Perforations, ornaments, fasteners, and their recorded positions | A pendant alone cannot reconstruct the entire garment beneath it |
| Western Zhou appointment display | LF08, ch. 3; Song ding and appointment-ritual figure locators | A gift to one official is not automatically a universal costume code |
| Textile identification | Fiber analysis, impressions, and excavated fragments when available | Silk/plant-fiber attribution, weave, dye, and proportions require a specimen-specific report (to-verify) |
| Reconstructed palette | Distinguish surface aging from original material appearance | Green archaeological bronze patina is not evidence that new ceremonial vessels were painted green |
- Use garment silhouette, head/hair arrangement, attachment points, and access to costly ornaments as separate variables; do not derive all of them from a deity’s supposed attributes.
- Exact collar construction, sleeve dimensions, and color rules for a named Shang/Zhou ensemble remain (to-verify) here; small jade carving cannot settle them unaided.
- Treat later ritual-text garment vocabulary as dated normative testimony, not an automatic label for a Shang figurine. A generic modern “hanfu” reconstruction is not a site-specific excavation report.
- Couple an appointment formula to permitted regalia only when the text supports the association; distinguish entitlement, actual ownership, burial display, and later antiquarian idealization.
- C21’s Fu Hao case joins military, ritual, and familial roles in one elite woman; it cannot establish either universal female exclusion or universal gender equality.

## Naming practices: preserve social address and source genre
- **Fù Hǎo 婦好**, conventionally Lady Hao/Fu Hao, and **Mǔ Xīn 母辛**, Mother Xin, illustrate different living/status and posthumous designations; do not model them as given name plus a modern hereditary surname. [C21]
- Keep a Shang stem-bearing ancestral designation distinct from an individual’s unattested everyday self-introduction. [K78]
- Zhou naming records may combine lineage or place affiliation, title/office, kinship seniority, and personal designation; LF08, p. xiii, explicitly preserves several compound inscriptional designations.
- **Bó 伯, zhòng 仲, shū 叔, jì 季** can participate in seniority distinctions; a particular occurrence still requires context, rather than automatic birth-order arithmetic.
- Do not impose a later, fully regularized **xìng 姓 / shì 氏** distinction on every inscription; exact reconstruction of a named individual’s affiliation remains (to-verify).
- Naming worksheet: attested designation; source/date; speaker; living/dead; kin relation; title; affiliation; graph-reading uncertainty; modern index form. Do not merge two people merely because a title or stem matches.
- Fictional application: specify which name a spouse, diviner, subordinate, ancestral officiant, and later chronicler can use; the contrasts should follow institutions, not decorative syllable lists.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- Take **couplings**, not a costume-and-gods shopping list: ancestral address can govern calendar, archives, food offerings, inheritance, and public rank simultaneously.
- Choose a historically bounded reference package: late-Shang divinatory court, Western Zhou delegated institutions, or a deliberately labeled synthesis. Do not call the synthesis a reconstruction.
- Build a medium/register contrast: short procedural oracle record versus commemorative bronze dedication versus later legitimating poem; use a fictional language’s own grammar rather than copying Chinese surface forms.
- Give powerful objects two records: demonstrable manufacture/transfer history and the history claimed by their owners; let an older jade and a newly commissioned bronze authorize status differently.
- Operational check: distinguish vessel 鼎 from stem 丁; test the 60-day rollover; separate alive/dead names; make each proposed garment feature point to evidence or an explicit design choice.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: proposal status; selected era/region; source keys; linguistic labels; ritual-authority chain; object biography; dress evidence; uncertainties; alternatives. Lore decides whether anything becomes canon.

## Provenance cautions and stopping rules
- **Contested:** Di–Tian relationships, animal-mask meanings, chronology before securely anchored years, and “feudalism” remain interpretation-sensitive. Carry named models and alternatives, not an averaged certainty.
- **Archive bias:** royal ancestors and elite tombs dominate these sources; marginal communities, local languages, and ordinary dress are thinly represented in this entry.
- **Historiographic bias:** dynastic labels and later ritual classifications can make diverse regions look like one continuous national culture; European rank analogies and evolutionary “feudal stage” schemes add other distortions. None is a neutral template.
- **Collection bias:** separate excavation provenience from later ownership provenance; a museum acquisition date cannot establish an ancient context. R95’s collection catalogue and SM’s excavated-object record are different evidence classes.
- **Translation bias:** “God,” “Heaven,” “prince,” and “feudal” can carry Christian, modern, or European assumptions; retain the Chinese term and the source’s convention when the distinction matters.
- **Thin areas:** no full Shang narrative mythology, regional costume atlas, original-garment pattern set, or independently collated inscription corpus is supplied here. Any such extension remains (to-verify) until sourced.
- Stop before inventing a deity biography, exact ancient pronunciation, undocumented jade owner, dye prescription, or universal vessel-count law. Present an unresolved reference question or a clearly labeled fictional option instead; never silently promote either to historical fact or canon.
