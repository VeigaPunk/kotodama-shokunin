## 1. Scope and calibration

- **Unit of description:** a named community, dialect, institution, period, and speaker position; “Igbo civilization” is not one historical state or a uniform religious jurisdiction.
- **Documented** below means a cited source supports a bounded practice; it does not mean every Igbo person follows it, or that it is unchanged today.
- **Contested** marks competing historical/interpretive accounts; **(to-verify)** marks an unresolved detail; **design transfer** marks a proposed method, not an ethnographic fact.
- Standard Igbo, indigenous religious practice, contemporary Christianity, urban life, and diaspora identity can coexist; do not place living people in a single “traditional” time capsule.
- Distinguish village-group institutions from Nri ritual influence and Onitsha kingship. Neither “all kings” nor “all egalitarian republics” is an adequate master template (Uchendu 1965; Isichei 1976, reading leads).
- Thin coverage here: specific northeastern/western ritual registers, women’s insider accounts of particular masking institutions, and precolonial month-reckoning procedures. Their details remain (to-verify).

## 2. Language: contrasts before atmosphere

- **Family:** Niger-Congo; Nkamigbo (2014, §1) uses West Benue-Congo. Igbo is conventionally placed in Igboid; reconciliation of intermediate nodes across current family classifications is (to-verify), not a reason to mix outdated Kwa/Benue-Congo labels into one unexamined tree.
- Treat the dialect continuum as overlapping isoglosses, not a list of defective versions of Standard Igbo. A named dialect cluster is an analyst’s grouping, not automatically a bounded ethnicity.
- Nkamigbo (2014, §3) contrasts Ikekeonwu’s and Nwaozuzu’s classifications. Do not report one fixed dialect count, or infer that every endpoint is equally intelligible to every other.

| Feature | Verified anchor | Coupling consequence / constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Lexical tone | High/low contrasts; descriptions also distinguish downstep (Onyenwe et al. 2014, §2.2) | A name’s pitch pattern belongs in its lexical record, not merely in acting direction |
| Grammatical tone | The cited study distinguishes declarative/interrogative readings through pronoun tone | A ritual question and assertion cannot safely be distinguished by English punctuation alone |
| Tone-bearing units | Vowels and syllabic nasals bear tone | Preserve nasal syllables in sung names and metrical templates |
| Vowel system | Standard orthographic vowels: a, e, i, ị, o, ọ, u, ụ; harmony commonly groups e/i/o/u against a/ị/ọ/ụ | Dot-below distinctions are vowel identities, not tone marks; do not strip them as decoration |
| Dialect-specific consonants | Nkamigbo documents nasalization, aspiration, and labialization contrasts in selected dialects | A standardized spelling can hide a local sound distinction important in names or chants |
| Standardization | The Onwu Committee orthography dates to 1961 (Onyenwe et al. 2014, §2.3) | A modern shared spelling is not proof that all earlier communities pronounced it alike |

- **Tone-model caution:** “high, low, downstep” is a useful descriptive shorthand, not proof of three immutable absolute pitches. Select one analysis before implementing tone spreading, register lowering, or phrase intonation; local rules remain (to-verify).
- The well-attested `akwa` example distinguishes cry, cloth, bed, and egg by tone (Onyenwe et al. 2014, §2.2). Do not reproduce a tone table without its convention: some systems leave high tones unmarked.
- **Writing:** contemporary Igbo uses a Latin-based orthography with digraphs and diacritics. Linguistic transcription, Standard Igbo spelling, and ordinary writing are separate layers; ordinary texts often omit most tone marking (Onyenwe et al. 2014, §3).
- Orthographic omission is not loss of spoken tone. Conversely, a fully marked pedagogical wordlist is not evidence that every historical letter or name was written that way.
- **Design transfer:** store `local form / pronunciation + tone / morphemes / gloss / standard spelling / period / genre`. Evolve related dialects by explicit changes before adding regional ritual vocabulary.
- Avoid sound-determinist claims: tonality does not cause communalism, mysticism, “musical thought,” or a particular cosmology.

## 3. Chi and a relational cosmology, not a universal god chart

- **Chi** is variously glossed personal deity, spirit counterpart, or personal providence. These are imperfect comparative glosses, not interchangeable anatomical parts of a Western “soul” (Achebe 1975, interpretive lead; Arinze/Kalu 1987/2005; religion summary).
- Its central craft problem is the relation between personal initiative and circumstances/destiny. Preserve the tension rather than deciding that Igbo thought is either pure fatalism or unlimited individual will.
- A person’s chi is not simply a patron selected from a communal pantheon, and is not automatically identical to an ancestor, an ikenga carving, or Chukwu.

| Node / relation | Bounded reference function | Flattening to reject |
|---|---|---|
| Chi and person | Individual fortune, agency, destiny, spiritual personhood; interpretations vary | A standardized “soul class” with a fixed game statistic |
| Chukwu / Chineke | Creator/supreme-being language in many accounts and contemporary names | An uncontested ancient doctrine identical across every locality |
| Ala / Ani | Earth deity; land, fertility, moral order, and prohibitions are linked | A decorative “earth-element goddess” detached from jurisdiction |
| Alusi / arusi | Locally situated powers with particular shrines, histories, personnel, and obligations | One complete pan-Igbo list with a single rank order |
| Ancestors | Kinship, burial, memory, and obligations connect living and dead | Every dead person automatically receiving the same ancestor status |
| Agwu and practitioners | Divination/healing vocation and its powers in religious accounts | “Magic” as an unrestricted skill anyone buys from a shop |

- **Contested:** the religion summary questions the historical universality of a creator-supreme-being doctrine; Arinze/Kalu presents a more unified theology. Record the disagreement rather than claiming either missionary invention everywhere or unchanged primordial monotheism everywhere.
- The pantheon table is an indexing aid, not evidence that people experience these relations as a Greek-style divine royal family.
- **Design transfer:** generate an obligation graph: `person ↔ personal power`, `household ↔ ancestors`, `land/community ↔ tutelary power`, `specialist ↔ vocation`. Give each edge a practice, interpreter, failure explanation, and disputing voice.
- Keep emic claims separate from authorial truth: “the diviner attributes this loss to…” is not “this spirit objectively caused it.” Lore-shokunin decides any fictional supernatural truth.

## 4. Odinani: registers are situated acts

- Odinani/Odinala are umbrella labels for indigenous religious-cultural traditions; omenala concerns customary/normative practice. Exact local usage and word boundaries remain (to-verify); do not impose a universal sacred-language taxonomy.
- Use **Hymes’s SPEAKING grid (1974)**, via `../linguistics/linguistic-anthropology.md`: setting, participants, ends, act sequence, key, instrumentalities, norms, genre. This is an external analysis, not an indigenous doctrine.
- The following separates attested speech-event families from questions a designer must answer. It supplies no supposedly authentic secret text.

| Event family | Reference anchor | Register/costume questions for design transfer |
|---|---|---|
| Prayer and offering | Petition, praise, thanksgiving; household and shrine contexts (Arinze/Kalu) | Who speaks for whom; what vocatives, repetition, gesture, and response authorize it? |
| Kola reception/blessing | Hospitality and ritual framing appear in religious-cultural accounts; exact sequence (to-verify) | Who presents, blesses, breaks, and distributes; is seniority or office decisive here? |
| Oath / authoritative declaration | Ọfọ is associated with legitimate authority, truth, and justice (religion summary) | What makes the utterance binding: holder, place, object, witnesses, or all together? |
| Divination / therapeutic consultation | Dibia and specialized divining/healing roles (Arinze/Kalu) | Who interprets; what is opaque to clients; who can contest a diagnosis? |
| Masquerade address and song | Performance joins speech, music, dress, audience, and restricted knowledge (Cole–Aniakor; Curnow) | Who may call a name or answer; which voice is public versus restricted? Local transcripts (to-verify) |
| Praise and commemorative naming | Names can praise divine action; title display has its own occasions | Can the everyday personal name be spoken here, or is an acquired name preferred? |

- **Do not equate register with another language.** Formulaic phrasing, archaic words, melody, indirection, and role-controlled turns may differentiate an event without a separate grammar.
- Do not coin an “Odinani language” from random Igbo words, or present ordinary Igbo as incapable of sacred speech.
- Preserve lexical tone when designing chant; how melody negotiates tone must be checked for the specific performance tradition (to-verify), not assumed to be “tone disappears in song.”
- Avoid universal rules such as “kola cannot understand English” unless attributed to a named speaker, locality, and period. A modern language-identity assertion does not establish an ancient prohibition.
- **Design transfer:** for each ritual register define a licensed speaker, expected hearer, opening/closing cues, permissible substitutions, dress/object requirements, and a socially recognized failure. Do not make all ritual speech literally supernatural.

## 5. Mmanwụ: costume, performance, and controlled knowledge

- Mmanwụ, with regional spellings such as mmonwu/mmọnwụ, names a masquerade complex, not simply a detachable wooden face. Distinguish the manifested being, institution, performer, ensemble, and museum object.
- “Secret society” is an imprecise outside label: ask which association controls membership, making, storage, revelation, appearance, and sanction. Access restrictions differ by place and mask type (Ottenberg 1975 is an Afikpo-specific lead).
- Many documented masking institutions restrict performance/initiation to men; maiden-spirit imagery does not imply female performers. This does not erase women as artists, singers, spectators, critics, or holders of other ritual authority (Curnow; Vida 2020).

| Bounded example | Material/performance signature | Institutional / symbolic coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Agbogho mmuo, documented in Agukwu-Nri/Awka material | Light-painted carved face, elaborate coiffure, patterned fitted cloth, dance | Youthful feminine ideals performed by men; textile/body-mark patterning and movement matter together (Vida 2020) |
| Mgbedike-type forms in northern Igbo examples | Darkened, projecting, sometimes animal-like features and forceful movement | A repertoire of potency/danger, not a universal “evil mask” category (Curnow §4.5) |
| Ijele in Anambra communities | Tall bamboo framework, colourful fabric, figurines, central python, accompanying music/attendants | Collective labour, controlled access, civic display, festive/solemn occasions, and entertainment coexist (UNESCO 2009) |

- These examples are not an exhaustive taxonomy and cannot be stacked into a single village wardrobe without a historical/local reason.
- **Costume assembly record:** carved or built headpiece → attachment/vision provisions → body covering → moving/sounding appendages → attendants → dance route → storage/repair. Record unknown construction rather than filling it from another mask tradition.
- A conservation account can establish pigments, holes, fibres, mounts, and damage without establishing the original dance or secret role.
- Vida (2020) explicitly cannot confirm whether MAA Z 13689 was danced before collection, or recover its complete associated costume. Do not attach a convenient museum costume to it as if proven.
- A publicly recognized masquerade can depend on unevenly distributed knowledge. “Outsiders do not know the performer” and “nobody knows a human is involved” are different claims.
- Do not describe women or children as simply duped; distinguish belief, publicly maintained convention, exclusion, negotiation, and coercion. A particular community’s accounts are needed before choosing among these.
- **Design transfer:** control revelation through licensing and consequences, not merely a “mysterious voice.” Decide who pays makers, maintains clothing, clears the route, disputes authority, and remembers retired forms.
- Christian calendars, festival stages, tourism, and heritage recognition can reframe performances without making them either unchanged relics or spiritually empty theatre.

## 6. Izu: market time links economy, names, and ritual

- The recurring market-day names **Eke → Orie → Afọ → Nkwọ → Eke** provide a four-day cycle, commonly called izu in traditional reckoning. Orie/Oye and Afo/Afor spellings occur; do not silently treat every spelling as an identical pronunciation (Obiejesi 2019; Arinze/Kalu).
- The display begins with Eke for convenience; it is not a claim that every community recognizes one cosmic first day or one universal historical epoch.
- A marketplace is also a social/ceremonial location; Obiejesi’s account links markets with gatherings and masquerade performance. This is infrastructure shared by economy and ritual, not two unrelated calendars.

| Layer | What to record | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|
| Recurring cycle | Local sequence and the observed day for a dated event | A Gregorian conversion without an anchor |
| Trading schedule | Which town holds which market, intervals, travel time | Every market closing on the other named days, or all markets operating identically |
| Ritual restrictions | Which shrine/festival/work rule attaches to a particular day locally | A pan-Igbo day-by-day zodiac |
| Naming | Whether a birth-day name is assigned, retained, inherited, or now a surname | A living bearer’s actual birth weekday from a surname alone |
| Month/season | Lunar observation, farming cues, festival authority, correction practice | A single ancient arithmetic calendar inferred from the week |

- **Contested reconstruction:** popular “13 months × 28 days = 364” descriptions are not enough to establish one historical Igbo calendar. Intercalation, year beginning, lunar-month fit, regional usage, and chronology remain (to-verify).
- Mythic accounts connecting named days to powers or directions are evidence of a narrated order; they do not by themselves prove the calendar’s date of invention. Obiejesi reports differing expert explanations.
- **Design transfer:** choose an event-day anchor and run the cycle continuously. If a fictional market cycle of four days coexists with a seven-day civil week, their pairings repeat after 28 days; that arithmetic is a design test, not proof of a historical Igbo institution.
- Test travel and preparation: a masquerade requiring substantial collective labour cannot be generated by a festival date alone; connect market income, makers, seasonal work, and performance permissions.

## 7. Symbolic system and material signature

| Carrier | Contextual reading | Calibration / useful comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Ọfọ | Authority, truth, justice in particular office/lineage settings | Not a generic wizard wand; possession and legitimate use are separate questions |
| Ikenga | Achievement and the right hand/arm in many documented shrines; ram-horn forms recur | Distinguish an object’s ritual use from chi; form and distribution vary (Cole–Aniakor; Curnow) |
| Light/dark surfaces | Maiden-spirit and forceful masculine examples use contrasting visual effects | Not an Igbo-wide white=good/black=evil dictionary, and not racial coding |
| Uli patterning | Body-marking designs and their echoes in costume and sculpture | Do not infer a phonetic script or decode every curve as a fixed word (Vida; Curnow) |
| Market-day names | Recurrence joins place, trade, personal naming, and sacred explanations | Fourfold order here is institutionally grounded, not proof of universal number mysticism |
| Akwete woven cloth | Harn 2016.38.11: rayon/cotton, floating-weft patterning, dated 1965–1967 | A specific textile records material exchange/change; it is not an unchanged prehistoric national costume |

- **Everyday ≠ ceremonial ≠ spirit body:** a cloth wrapper on a figure, an acquired title’s regalia, and a fitted masquerade suit occupy different sign systems. Inventory garment structure and wearer/occasion separately.
- Modern combinations of wrappers, tailored shirts/blouses, head coverings, beads, imported fibres, and print fabrics must be dated and localized; none establishes one uniform “authentic Igbo outfit.”
- A status garment must connect to an office and a means of acquisition; an object labelled “chief’s” in a collection is not independent proof of hereditary kingship.
- **Design transfer:** model a symbol as `carrier + maker + authorized user + occasion + audience + competing reading`. Reuse one motif across cloth, shrine, and praise-name only after specifying its route of transmission.

## 8. Era staging without an evolutionary ladder

| Evidence horizon | Usable anchor | What must not be projected backward |
|---|---|---|
| Igbo-Ukwu, commonly placed around the ninth century CE | Highly accomplished copper-alloy ritual vessels/regalia and beads; dating cautions in Apley (2001) | Exact modern deity identities, language forms, masquerade costumes, or a proven continuous political dynasty |
| Differentiated precolonial networks | Community institutions, title systems, Nri/Onitsha/Aro histories require separate treatment (Isichei, reading lead) | A single centralized “Igbo empire”; detailed institution dates (to-verify) |
| Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Missions, colonial intervention, collecting, photographs, and performances enter different records | Treating a colonial photograph as an unmediated view of remote antiquity |
| Colonial to independence period | Orthographic standardization culminates in the 1961 Onwu reference point; Harn cloth shows later fibre combinations | A literacy or clothing change occurring everywhere at once |
| War and later transformation | Nigerian Civil War/Biafra, 1967–1970, and disrupted collecting/shrine histories (Curnow; Isichei lead) | Aestheticizing displacement or treating missing objects as voluntary religious abandonment |
| Contemporary/diasporic/heritage settings | UNESCO’s 2009 Ijele inscription; changing venues, media, patronage, and religious stances | Declaring a newly public performance either wholly invented or unchanged in meaning |

- Archaeological evidence is strong for surviving materials and much weaker for reconstructing spoken formulae or vanished textiles. Scarcity of written chronicles does not imply absence of history; oral accounts also require genre, narrator, occasion, and collection-date metadata.
- **Design transfer:** stage changes by channels—trade, religious translation, conquest, schooling, tailoring, recording—rather than “tribal → feudal → modern.” Permit different channels to move at different rates.

## 9. Naming practices: propositions and social biographies

- Names can record time, kinship, hopes, misfortune, gratitude, or religious orientation; treat a name as an utterance with a naming occasion and later history, not an ornamental syllable chain.
- **Market-linked forms:** Nweke, Nworie/Nwoye, Nwafọ, Nwankwọ; Okeke, Okorie/Okoye, Okafọ, Okonkwọ are among Obiejesi’s recorded examples. These demonstrate a productive time–person coupling, not a licence to infer every bearer’s birth circumstances.
- Obiejesi also records Mgbeke, Mgborie, Mgbafọ, Mgbankwọ/Mgbọkwọ for women; contemporary gender distribution and local forms need speaker checking (to-verify), not a universal prefix rule.
- **Propositional/theophoric forms:** Chukwudi is glossed “God lives,” Kenechukwu “thank God,” in Arinze/Kalu. Preserve the source’s gloss and context; segmentation, local pronunciation, and tone require dictionary/speaker verification (to-verify).
- A contemporary Christian interpretation of a divine element and an indigenous cosmological interpretation may differ. Shared spelling does not settle the historical meaning, and chi/Chukwu elements are not freely interchangeable name-generator tokens.
- Record birth name, everyday short form, praise/acquired title name, surname, religious name, and outsider spelling as distinct fields when supported. Do not assume all individuals possess all fields or that all periods used modern surname conventions.
- **Design transfer:** generate full propositions first; derive conventional shortened forms through stated sound/usage changes; then add address permissions and era-specific reinterpretation. Keep naming practice linked to tone, calendar, religious affiliation, and rank display.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

- **A coupled design method, not a copied people:** borrow relations among language, social authority, religious interpretation, objects, and time; do not rename Igbo communities and call the result an invented culture.
- Start with a localized speech community and one historical horizon. Give neighboring communities systematic linguistic differences and partly overlapping institutions rather than palette-swapped costumes.
- Link personal destiny discourse to naming and disagreement about misfortune; distinguish personal powers from territorial shrines and ancestor obligations.
- Make costume an authorization technology: who can appear as what, who makes the appearance possible, who speaks for it, and who may refuse its claim?
- Connect the market cycle to travel, birth names, work, ritual scheduling, and supply chains; model the calendar rather than decorating a month chart with unfamiliar labels.
- Use a **coupling ledger**: `form/tone ↔ meaning ↔ ritual speaker ↔ object/garment ↔ occasion/day ↔ locality/era ↔ source/status`. This is a design scaffold, not a claim about a native master system.
- Stress-test with an outsider mispronouncing a name, two communities using different forms for the same market day, a disputed ritual office, a repaired costume made with new fabric, and a festival moved to another civil date. Record consequences as options, not canon events.
- Hand off only candidate mechanisms, alternatives, source limits, and consistency tests through `../synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md`. Lore-shokunin owns adoption, population assignments, chronology, and supernatural truth.

## 11. Provenance cautions and recheck triggers

- Checked on 2026-10-17: open Onyenwe/Nkamigbo texts; Arinze/Kalu and contrasting religion summaries; Curnow’s Igbo subsections; Vida’s dated conservation account; UNESCO; BBC Igbo; Apley; Harn’s object record. Bibliographic verification is explicitly distinguished above from reading a full book.
- **Thin local detail stays thin:** exact initiation procedures, restricted words, deity-name tones, ritual chant transcriptions, ancient month mechanics, and dated origins of particular institutions remain (to-verify). Do not fill them with another African tradition or generic fantasy occultism.
- Colonial/missionary records can preserve valuable observation while imposing “tribe,” “fetish,” “pagan,” male-elite, and conversion frames. Retain observer, translator, collection setting, and power relationship; neither endorse their categories nor discard all evidence indiscriminately.
- The Arinze/Kalu entry’s unified supreme-being account and the other summary’s historical skepticism are a real interpretive fork. Neither licenses stating a settled origin theory without a local historical study.
- Curnow is a teaching synthesis, not a final verdict on all communities; gender binaries and broad claims about historical continuity require checking against bounded studies and contemporary voices.
- UNESCO descriptions are heritage representations with their own selection frame. Its large workforce, preparation times, and dimensions should not become fixed specifications for every Ijele, much less every masquerade.
- BBC interviews document narrated belief and naming; uncorroborated claims there about remote origins or sacrifice are not generalized here. A reported origin story is not an archaeological date.
- Museum objects may have incomplete collection histories, uncertain pigment histories, missing costume parts, or broad ethnic attribution. Object date, collection date, attribution, and date of a proposed meaning are different fields.
- Recheck whenever a design needs an exact translation, community-specific permission, chronology, or manufacturing specification. Cite the actual edition/object/transcript and mark unresolved details inline; do not launder a bibliographic lead into page-level proof.
