# Celtic reference base — language, oral authority, metalwork, and layered identities

- topic: Continental versus Insular Celtic; restricted druidic oral transmission; La Tène ornament and torcs; later ogham; triple-spiral interpretation; regional warrior dress, mythology, and naming as coupled worldbuilding references, not fictional canon.

## Scope and evidence discipline

- **Celtic is a branch of Indo-European and first a linguistic classification here**, not a racial type, unified state, costume, or single religion. Language, archaeological assemblage, self-identification, and modern national identity require separate evidence.
- “Insular” in language history is not equivalent to “everything excavated on an island”; “Insular art” is a different, early medieval art-historical category.
- Use three calibration labels: **attested** = a named text/object records it; **inferred** = an interpretation of that evidence; **contested/unknown** = competing explanations or insufficient evidence. An attested ancient allegation is not automatically an established custom.

| Evidence layer | Useful access | Main distortion | Safe craft extraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Classical ethnography | Caesar, *BG* 6.13–18; Diodorus 5.27–31 | Conquest justification, exoticizing comparison, elite and male emphasis | Reported institutions, dress contrasts, and outsider naming practices; retain attribution |
| Continental epigraphy | Gaulish inscriptions discussed by Lambert; Greek/Latin-script texts | Short, unevenly distributed, sometimes difficult to interpret | Language contact, dedication formulas, named persons; not a complete theology |
| Metalwork and deposition | Torcs, scabbards, mirrors, hoards in Megaw & Megaw | Durable elite goods survive better than everyday cloth and wood | Manufacturing vocabulary, visual grammar, unequal access; meanings remain hypotheses |
| Early medieval inscription | Ogham corpus, McManus | Formulaic names rather than narrative histories | Kinship display, case endings, memorial media; not recovered druidic liturgy |
| Medieval vernacular literature | Gray's Irish text; Davies's Welsh tales | Christian transmission, literary composition, copying and redaction | Narrative institutions and performances; do not silently date them to the Iron Age |

- Scarcity matters: this entry has no indigenous pre-conquest chronicle that explains a complete Continental pantheon or decodes its ornaments. Our strongest evidence changes category between regions and periods.
- Models of Celtic origins remain disputed. Sims-Williams (2020) critiques both an automatic Hallstatt-to-west migration story and a Neolithic Atlantic linguistic origin; his alternative is an argument, not a settled replacement map.

## Language family and writing: separate genealogy from geography

| Reference grouping | Languages and relationship | Writing evidence | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Continental Celtic | Gaulish, Lepontic, Celtiberian; Galatian is closely associated with Gaulish | Lepontic uses a north Italic alphabet; Celtiberian uses an adapted Paleohispanic script and Latin letters; Gaulish uses Greek and Latin alphabets | Shared ancestry does not require a shared script, political system, or dress code |
| Goidelic, within Insular Celtic | Irish; later differentiated Scottish Gaelic and Manx | Primitive Irish in ogham; Old Irish and subsequent literary traditions in Latin script | Stage sound changes, spelling, and naming formulas rather than giving all centuries modern Irish forms |
| Brittonic, within Insular Celtic | Welsh, Cornish, Breton; also extinct varieties | Latin-script manuscript and inscriptional traditions | Choose a specific language stage; do not attach Irish morphology to Welsh-looking names |
| Breton's diagnostic exception | Brittonic ancestry, despite its Continental location | Medieval and later Latin-script tradition | Breton is not surviving Gaulish; migration and contact complicate geographic labels |

- Ball & Müller (2009) provide the comparative and historical frame; Lambert (2003) supplies Gaulish linguistic description and inscription commentary.
- **P/Q-Celtic is a sound-correspondence shorthand**, not a sufficient culture tree: inherited labiovelar *kʷ becomes p in Gaulish/Brittonic, but has a different history in Goidelic and Celtiberian. Internal subgrouping needs multiple shared innovations.
- Do not equate P with Continental and Q with Insular: Brittonic is Insular and P-type; Celtiberian is Continental and Q-type.
- Verb-first clauses, initial mutations, and inflected prepositions are characteristic resources of the Insular languages; their systems differ. Do not project a complete modern Welsh or Irish grammar into fragmentarily attested Gaulish.
- **Writing does not imply population-wide literacy, and orality does not imply an absence of writing.** A community can write accounts, dedications, and names while restricting other discourse to trained speakers.
- Fiction method: specify language ancestry, current contact languages, script donor, public literacy domains, and protected speech domains independently; then explain their interaction.

## Druidic oral transmission: what the writing restriction actually says

- Caesar, *BG* 6.14, reports memorization of many verses and says some students remain in training for twenty years. Treat that duration as his report, not a verified universal curriculum.
- He says the druids consider it improper to commit those teachings to writing, **although Greek letters are used in almost all other matters, including public and private accounts**.
- His two explanations — preventing dissemination and exercising memory — are explicitly his interpretation. “Druids banned all writing” is contradicted by the same passage.
- “Taboo on writing rites” is usable shorthand only with this qualification: Caesar describes a restriction on the transmitted sacred teaching, not an extant Gaulish regulation specifying every forbidden genre.
- *BG* 6.13 places druids in ritual instruction and adjudication; it also reports assemblies and religious exclusion. This is a powerful institution model, but still an account by the conquering commander.
- Diodorus 5.31 distinguishes praise/satire singers called bards, learned religious specialists called druids, and diviners. Do not collapse these roles into one hereditary “wizard class.”
- Similar classical accounts may share earlier ethnographic material; agreement is not automatically independent corroboration. Medieval Irish learned poets are not proof that Caesar's exact institution survived unchanged.

| Design lever | Historically grounded prompt | Fictional decision to make, not historical fact |
|---|---|---|
| Access | Who may learn a protected repertoire? | Admission by sponsorship, kinship, examination, or another chosen institution |
| Authorized performance | What changes when words are spoken by a recognized specialist? | Which judgments, invocations, or praise acts require witnesses and recognized competence? |
| Memory technology | What can a long apprenticeship transmit reliably? | Repetition, parallelism, correction, and performance tests; do not assume verbatim preservation merely because it is oral |
| Domain restriction | Why can accounts be written but sacred teaching not? | Define prohibited content, permitted mnemonics, sanctions, and exceptions |
| Institutional change | What happens when a new script or religion arrives? | Separate loss of authority, selective transcription, and retrospective claims of antiquity |

- Couple the institution to costume through **authorized contexts of display**, not an invented historical uniform: no standardized druid robe color follows from *BG* 6.14.

## Mythic and pantheon cores: keep the archives apart

| Archive | Secure reference anchors | Useful coupling | Calibration boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Late Republican Gaul through Caesar | *BG* 6.17 describes gods as Mercury, Apollo, Mars, Jupiter, Minerva; 6.18 names Dis | Foreign observers translate unfamiliar cults into their own divine categories | **Interpretatio romana** is mediation, not a preserved native pantheon list; “Mercury = Lug” is not established by this passage |
| Gallo-Roman Epona | Equine iconography and dedicatory evidence surveyed by Linduff (1979) | Horse economies, movement, military and civilian cult networks | Do not turn one widespread cult into evidence that all Celtic-speaking communities had identical divine offices |
| Romano-British Bath | RIB 150 names Sulis Minerva and a dedicant, Sulinus son of Maturus | Sacred locality + paired divine name + Latin vow + personal affiliation | Strong evidence for this dedication; not a warrant for projecting the pairing into every pre-Roman region |
| Medieval Irish *Cath Maige Tuired* | Túatha Dé Danann; Núadu, Lug, Dagda, Morrígan, Goibniu, Dían Cécht, Bres | Kingship, craft, healing, hospitality, tribute, and strategic speech are interdependent | Gray (1982) edits a transmitted narrative, not a pre-Christian worship manual |
| Medieval Welsh *Mabinogion* | Pwyll, Rhiannon, Branwen, Manawydan, Math, Gwydion across distinct tales | Obligation, status, transformation, marriage, and skilled action structure social worlds | Davies (2008): literary characters and otherworld narratives cannot simply be reassigned as offices in an Irish pantheon |

- **Irish narrative mechanism:** Núadu's lost hand affects eligibility for kingship; the story then makes healing skill politically consequential. Model the relation between body, legitimacy, and technical expertise rather than copying a “silver-hand god” cosmetic.
- **Hospitality mechanism:** Bres's failures of provisioning and patronage bring complaint and satire; the tale links legitimate rule to redistribution and the support of performers (Gray 1982, translation pp. 33–35).
- **Craft mechanism:** Lug's admission to Tara depends on possessing a combination of arts, not simply an otherwise unavailable single trade (Gray 1982, pp. 39–41). A roster of professions becomes a theory of leadership.
- **Speech mechanism:** praise, satire, vows, and named obligations can alter reputation or obligation; distinguish literary supernatural efficacy from documentary evidence of legal practice.
- Do not reduce the Fomoire to an ethnic enemy race: *Cath Maige Tuired* also gives them kinship and alliance ties with their opponents.
- Similar motifs across Irish, Welsh, and Continental evidence invite comparison, not automatic identity: Epona and Rhiannon are not interchangeable merely because horses occur in both traditions.
- The edition's manuscript witness, a tale's composition layers, its represented past, and a modern translation's publication date are four different dates.

## Symbolic system: La Tène is a visual grammar, not a decoded alphabet

- Megaw & Megaw (2001) treat early Celtic art through objects, regional styles, borrowing, and manufacture. Read ornament as choices constrained by substrate and workshop, not a dictionary of universal meanings.

| Form or surface | Observable grammar | Supportable inference | Unsupported shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Torc/torque | Neck ring; varied rods, wires, terminals, metals, and construction | Costly labor and display; possible status, gift, heirloom, or dedicatory histories depending on context | “Every torc means kingship,” or a compulsory ornament for every warrior |
| La Tène scrolling | S-scrolls, spirals, tendrils, trumpet/lyre-like forms, stylized plant and animal elements | A shared repertoire can be transformed across workshops and media | Each curl has a recoverable named religious meaning |
| Concealed/transformed faces | Heads or animals emerge from apparently abstract designs | Deliberate ambiguity and changing views can structure recognition | Every apparent face is a specific named deity |
| Decorated scabbard, mirror, brooch | Pattern conforms to long, circular, or curved working surfaces | Object function helps organize motif scale and visibility | A museum's flat drawing reproduces how a moving wearer experienced the object |
| Hoarded or deposited metal | Objects removed from ordinary circulation; sometimes broken or grouped | Deposition has an object biography requiring contextual explanation | Every hoard is a sacrifice, or every broken weapon a single known rite |
| Medieval interlace | Repeated over/under crossings in manuscript, metalwork, or sculpture | Another dated design grammar, with multiple cultural inputs | Book-of-Kells-style knotwork is the universal uniform of pre-conquest Gaul |

- **Era-specific art codes:** distinguish early La Tène adaptations of Mediterranean motifs, flowing vegetal/Waldalgesheim conventions, relief-rich plastic styles, and regional scabbard traditions. These are overlapping scholarly style categories, not successive ethnic castes.
- **Torc coupling:** track who makes it, who acquires it, who recognizes its terminals, and when it is worn or deposited. Rank or sacred meaning must be specified as a hypothesis unless an associated context supports it.
- Do not infer an ancient native garment word from a modern museum label such as “torc,” “brooch,” or “fibula.” Keep artifact terminology and reconstructed vocabulary in separate fields.

## Triple spirals: separate the motif's date from its claimed meaning

- Heritage Ireland identifies triple spirals at Newgrange and dates the passage tomb complex to approximately **3200 BCE**. This is Neolithic evidence, millennia before the historical Celtic-language record used here.
- Calling the Newgrange builders “druids” or assigning them a demonstrably Celtic language is unsupported. Present-day Irish location does not establish prehistoric linguistic affiliation.
- A triskele/triskelion describes a threefold rotational arrangement; triple spirals and three bent legs are related formal categories, not necessarily identical signs with one origin or meaning.
- Spirals and threefold forms also occur in later art. **Repeated geometry is attested; uninterrupted transmission of a particular doctrine is not thereby demonstrated.**
- “Land/sea/sky,” “birth/life/death,” and “maiden/mother/crone” are not secure translations of the Newgrange carving. A universal Celtic triple-goddess decoder is not an evidence-based historical framework.
- Even where a text groups beings or events in threes, that does not identify the meaning of a separately excavated threefold ornament.
- Fiction method: choose a geometric construction rule, an institution that uses it, and an in-world interpretation; label that interpretation as an original design proposal. Reinterpretation by later communities is a separate, plausible process, not proof of real-world continuity.

## Era model: archaeological phases are not language replacement dates

| Stage | Approximate reference horizon | Language/myth/material coupling | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Neolithic antecedent landscape | Newgrange c. 3200 BCE | Monument, orientation, and megalithic carving; later communities may encounter an inherited landscape | Do not retroactively fill it with Iron Age languages or druidic institutions |
| Central European early Iron Age | Hallstatt C–D, broadly c. 800–450 BCE | Regional elite networks, burial display, imported prestige goods | An archaeological assemblage does not identify every person's language |
| Later Iron Age | La Tène, broadly c. 450 BCE to regional Roman conquests | Curvilinear ornament, specialized metalwork, local political networks, varied writing contacts | Its spread is not a universal invasion clock; Insular styles have their own trajectories |
| Roman imperial contexts | Gaul and Britain enter different conquest/contact sequences | Latin epigraphy, native divine names paired with Roman ones, changing material repertoires | Ireland was not a Roman province; do not impose one imperial experience across the islands |
| Early medieval inscription and Christian learning | Monumental ogham principally c. fourth–sixth centuries CE; Latin-script learning overlaps and expands | Names on stone, cross-channel contact, changing language and religious authority | Ogham is later than Caesar's Gaul; it is not the script of every Iron Age druid |
| Early medieval Insular art | Especially c. seventh–ninth centuries CE | Christian books and church/secular metalwork combine several visual inheritances | “Insular art” is neither a pagan doctrinal survival certificate nor an exclusively Celtic production category |
| Medieval literary and later reception layers | Different Irish/Welsh composition, copying, translation, and revival dates | Older motifs acquire new narrative and political uses | Date each witness and edition; later authenticity claims need their own evidence |

- A fictional era transition should change several coupled variables: writing domain, patronage institution, ornament placement, naming morphology, and access to prestigious materials. Do not merely rename the same wardrobe.

## Costume and material signature: build a regional ensemble

- Diodorus 5.27 describes gold neck and arm ornaments; 5.30 describes colored shirts, breeches called *bracae*, and shoulder-fastened outer garments with stripes/checks and seasonal weight differences.
- This is attributed testimony about Gauls, not a measured garment pattern or a wardrobe census. His grand claims about physique, temperament, and “barbarian” behavior disclose the framing bias.

| Component | Reference foothold | Coupling use | Reconstruction limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Shirt/tunic and breeches | Diodorus 5.30 | Local weaving, cutting, work, and movement determine vocabulary | Exact cut, seam placement, fiber, and dye for a specified site/century require separate evidence (Grömer 2016 supplies Central European fibre, dye, weave, and tailoring work) |
| Shoulder-fastened outer garment | Diodorus 5.30; brooches in art/archaeology | Fastener visibility can make textile, metalwork, and rank interact | Translation choices such as “coat” or “cloak” are not ancient tailoring diagrams |
| Stripes/checks and color | Diodorus's account | Distinguish woven pattern, dye access, and ownership rules | Checks do not prove hereditary Scottish clan tartans in the Iron Age |
| Torc and arm ornament | Diodorus 5.27; surviving metalwork | Portable wealth, workshop prestige, exchange, and bodily display | Restriction to one sex, rank, or ritual is not universal |
| Shield, sword suspension, helmet | Diodorus 5.29–30; regional surviving objects | Ornament, movement, sound, and distance legibility form a display system | Do not generalize a spectacular helmet or decorated shield to standard equipment |
| Mail and exposed bodies | Diodorus 5.30 contrasts chain-wrought armor and unarmored fighters | Equipment access can differentiate fighters rather than define an entire people | Naked combat is reported, not the default dress of every Celtic warrior |
| Later prestige brooches/interlace | Early medieval objects in Megaw & Megaw | New patrons and workshop traditions can change the same cloak-fastening surface | A later brooch type cannot date an earlier mythic scene by itself |

- Treat swords, shields, and mail as expensive manufactured objects with repair histories; keep everyday textiles and laboring bodies in view alongside elite metalwork.
- Do not default to a “kilt + blue paint + horns” package. Any particular body-paint substance, application method, helmet type, or garment reconstruction needs dated regional support: Caesar's *vitrum* is not securely identified with woad, and paint-versus-tattoo is debated (Carr 2005).
- Costume coupling worksheet: `place → century → activity → available fiber/metal → garment construction → fastener → visible motif → proposed social reading → source/confidence`.
- Literary descriptions of brilliant metal, gold-thread clothing, and extraordinary bodies can encode the otherworld or exceptional status; they are not automatically evidence of ordinary historical availability.

## Naming practices: preserve morphology, chronology, and social function

| Naming layer | Reference pattern | Application rule |
|---|---|---|
| Gaulish personal names | Compounding and recurring elements, including royal *-rīx*, discussed by Lambert | Build a language-specific element inventory; do not treat every compound as a literal biography or royal office |
| Monumental Primitive Irish | Personal names often in genitive formulas; **MAQI** marks a son relationship | Preserve case and spelling stage; an inscriptional form need not equal the citation form used in speech |
| Ogham affiliation | **MUCOI** occurs in formulas marking group affiliation | Keep group membership distinct from immediate paternity; exact affiliations and readings require inscription-level checking |
| Medieval patronymics | Irish *mac*; Welsh *map/mab* and later *ap/ab* | Date the form and apply the chosen language's grammar; never glue prefixes randomly to attractive syllables |
| Hereditary surnames | Later social developments using descent language | Do not give every prehistoric individual a fixed modern Mac-/Ó-style surname |
| Epithet and narrative name | Names/titles interact with performance, deeds, genealogy, and place in the literary sources | Store birth/personal name, patronymic, epithet, and translated gloss as separate fields |
| Roman-contact naming | RIB 150 names Sulinus and his father Maturus in a Latin dedication | Script/language of record, cult affiliation, and personal-name origin need not coincide |

- McManus (1991) is the control for ogham forms: this is an alphabet encoding language, not a collection of pictorial charms or a generally proven prehistoric “tree calendar.”
- Medieval tree-name glosses are real evidence for medieval learned interpretation; they do not establish that every original letter name denoted a tree or that the earliest stones encoded a monthly calendar.
- Name generation procedure: select region and century; choose attested naming functions; define inflection and sound correspondences; generate a consistent inventory; mark borrowed forms; retain source-language forms beside editorial translations.
- The male-heavy monumental record does not establish that women lacked names, kin identities, or public roles. Corpus selection constrains what it can tell us.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Selective literacy:** combine written economic/public records with protected spoken repertoires; make the boundary enforceable and socially consequential rather than treating writing as absent.
- **Authority through trained speech:** connect apprenticeship, praise/satire, judgments, and patronage to institutions; distinguish the powers attributed by stories from demonstrated historical practice.
- **A portable visual grammar:** let curve, terminal, relief, and negative space vary by workshop, object shape, and viewing distance. Assign original fictional meanings explicitly, not as recovered Celtic facts.
- **Prestige worn at interfaces:** torc at neck, brooch at garment closure, decoration at sword suspension — body, textile, metal, rank, and movement become one design problem.
- **Layered sacred landscapes:** distinguish the makers of old monuments from later users and interpreters; a later group's explanation can be new even when the stone is ancient.
- **Diachronic names:** couple case endings, sound change, scripts, patronymics, and institutional change. Related languages can share roots without sharing current spellings or identical naming customs.
- Handoff only options: `reference region/date → evidenced mechanism → proposed adaptation → language/myth/symbol/era/costume effects → uncertainty → decision needed`. lore-shokunin accepts, rejects, and records canon; this entry decides none.

## Provenance cautions and thin coverage

- **Verified scope:** the linked Caesar, Diodorus, Gray, RIB 150, and Heritage Ireland passages were inspected; publisher/catalog records and scholarly cross-references checked the other works. This is not a claim that every page of every cited monograph was inspected.
- **Bias:** Caesar is both ethnographer and agent of conquest; Diodorus presents a Greek comparative account. Keep reported practices, modern scholarly interpretation, and this entry's design suggestions visibly separate.
- **Thin coverage:** Celtiberian ritual, Galatian local dress, Pictish inscriptional problems, regional textile cuts, and detailed female naming practice are not adequately reconstructed here. Exact local forms and associations are not established by the sources checked here, not gaps to fill with Irish analogies.
- **Scarce narrative evidence:** Continental material/epigraphic evidence and medieval Insular stories do not form one continuous chronicle. Shared names or motifs can justify a comparison, not a missing historical bridge.
- **Calibration stop:** precise triple-spiral meanings, universal triple-goddess systems, obligatory warrior nudity, a single druid uniform, and ogham as an Iron Age pan-Celtic secret script are not established by these sources. Do not promote them into historical facts or fictional canon by implication.
