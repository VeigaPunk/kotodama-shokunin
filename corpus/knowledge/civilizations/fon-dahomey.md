## Scope and calibration
- **Distinguish units:** Fon is a people/language designation; Dahomey was an expanding kingdom centered on Abomey, incorporating populations beyond Fon speakers. Modern Benin is neither synonymous with that kingdom nor the historical Kingdom of Benin in present-day Nigeria.
- **Localize claims:** Abomey court, Allada, Ouidah/Xwéda, Porto-Novo, and particular lineage or shrine communities are not interchangeable cultural samples.
- **Established** below means supported at the stated scale; **situated** means documented but not universal; **contested** means competing historical interpretations; `(to-verify)` identifies an unresolved detail rather than an invitation to invent it.
- Written evidence is uneven: Atlantic commerce and royal encounters are better documented than early household ritual or pre-seventeenth-century migration. Oral traditions are evidence with speakers and political contexts, not failed written chronicles. [Law 1991; Bay 1998]
- This entry is especially thin on performer-checked Fon drum-speech transcriptions, exact palace honorific grammar, and ordinary household naming corpora. Those gaps are retained explicitly.

## Language family, tonal structure, and writing
| Dimension | Reference knowledge | Coupled design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Family | Fon/Fongbe belongs to the Gbe group within Niger–Congo; intermediate higher classifications vary by analysis. | Distinguish shared inheritance from borrowing across the Gbe continuum. |
| Typology | Relatively isolating morphology, basic subject–verb–object order, and serial-verb constructions. | Do not generate ceremonial language merely by adding elaborate inflection absent from ordinary speech. |
| Segmental texture | Oral/nasal vowel contrasts and labial–velar stops /kp, gb/ are important; exact inventories belong to a specified variety. | Track nasalization and digraphs consistently in names rather than scattering exotic-looking spellings. |
| Tone | A high/low underlying analysis supports richer surface patterns, including contours and downstep. | Lexical tone is not emotional intonation; specify both citation forms and connected-speech realization. |
| Literacy | Modern Fon uses a Latin-based orthography, including ɛ, ɔ, ɖ and digraphs; tone marking varies by text and purpose. | A modern dictionary spelling is not evidence of a seventeenth-century palace alphabet. |
| Contact | Gbe varieties, Yoruba/Nago traditions, coastal intermediaries, and European languages interacted in different settings. | A shared deity or borrowed title does not establish mutual intelligibility or identical social institutions. |

- Linguistic baseline: Lefebvre & Brousseau (2002); spelling lookup: Höftmann & Ahohounkpanzon (2003). Do not treat every surface pitch as an independent phonemic tone.
- Keep three separate representations when designing: phonological form → spoken realization → conventional spelling. Historical European transcriptions often obscure their differences.
- Royal bas-reliefs and appliqué emblems support narration and recognition; they are not a demonstrated phonographic script encoding arbitrary Fon sentences. [Piqué & Rainer 1999]
- Application: establish a small, coherent sound system before generating names, praise formulas, or hypothetical speech-surrogate music; do not reverse-engineer phonology from untoned royal-name spellings alone.

## Drum speech and the requested “gun-gun” label
- **Identification unresolved:** “gun-gun” as a specifically Fon talking-drum designation, with a verified spelling, instrument construction, and repertoire, remains `(to-verify)`.
- Do not silently equate that label with **Gun/Gungbe**, a Gbe language designation, or with Yoruba **gángan/dùndún**. Similar-looking words are not an etymology or an instrument identification.
- **Established comparison:** Durojaye et al. (2021) acoustically studied Yoruba dùndún speech surrogacy: pitch, timing, and intensity can correspond closely to vocal utterances, with different degrees of correspondence in different performance modes. This is Yoruba evidence, not a Fon experiment.
- Finnegan's comparative “Drum Language and Literature” discusses the importance of conventional phrasing and literary repertoires; it does not license transplanting one community's drum vocabulary into another.

| Communication type | What carries meaning | Verification needed before calling it Fon practice |
|---|---|---|
| Speech surrogacy | Language-specific prosodic patterns interpreted through a learned repertoire and context | Named instrument, performer, spoken original, tonal transcription, and translation |
| Conventional signal | A learned call or rhythmic pattern associated with an event | Who recognizes it, its occasion, and evidence that it is a signal rather than a translated sentence |
| Ritual or dance music | Repertoire, movement, invocation, participation, and occasion | A community's account of its function; rhythmic complexity alone does not demonstrate lexical speech |
| Royal praise performance | Names, epithets, narration, and musical framing | Evidence separating sung/spoken words from anything independently communicated by instruments |

- Application: a fictional drum code should specify what is preserved, what is lost, who learns it, and how listeners resolve ambiguity. Tone without consonants and vowels is not automatically a lossless message channel.
- Prototype with a synthetic language: compare phrases sharing a tonal pattern; add conventional expansions or contextual cues; test whether trained listeners can recover the intended message.
- Do not invent a Fon drum score, universal beat-to-letter cipher, fixed transmission distance, or precise “gun-gun” ceremonial rank. Those particulars remain `(to-verify)`.

## Vodun: a network of powers and institutions, not a fixed roster
- **Situated synthesis:** Fon vodun traditions include cosmological narratives, local divinities, initiatory communities, household obligations, healing, divination, and royal patronage. A printed pantheon table is a reference aid, not a universal creed. [Blier 1995; Landry 2019]

| Power or complex | Attested reference associations | Calibration / useful coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Mawu–Lisa | Complementary female/male and moon/sun associations in prominent accounts | Accounts differ in genealogy and whether the pair is treated as twins, a unity, or differentiated powers; avoid a compulsory gender diagram. |
| Nana Buluku | Primordial creator or progenitor in some narratives | Do not make every local shrine accept one standardized creation sequence. |
| Legba | Mediation, openings, communication, and potentially disruptive agency | Couple invocation to access and interpretation; “the devil” is a distorting missionary equation, not a neutral translation. |
| Sakpata | Earth and disease, historically especially smallpox | Protective and dangerous relations coexist; not simply an evil “plague god.” |
| Hevioso / Xɛvioso | Thunder, lightning, and justice | Identify a source's spelling and locality; do not infer universal colors or costume from the domain alone. |
| Dan / Dàn complexes | Serpent powers; prosperity and cosmological associations in differing traditions | Specify the particular serpent tradition; do not merge every python cult, cosmic serpent, and royal name. |
| Gu | Iron, metalworking, tools, and warfare | Craft, maintenance, and changing technology matter as well as battle. |
| Fá | Divination with a learned interpretive repertoire, related through regional history to Yoruba Ifá | Treat it as an institution and practice, not merely another deity's job description. |

- These associations are documented in the ethnographic literature summarized by Blier (1995) and Landry (2019); individual genealogies and local terminology need a named account before reuse.
- Follow **relations**, not “god of X” substitutions: who sponsors a shrine, inherits responsibility, commissions objects, interprets a sign, or contests a priest's authority?
- Royal incorporation of a cult is not proof of its invention by a king; a court's universalizing account may coexist with older local claims. [Bay 1998]
- Haitian Vodou and Brazilian Jeje traditions are historically connected but separately developed; neither is a transparent survival manual for precolonial Fon religion.

## Family cult, ancestors, and materialized obligation
| Scale | Institutional question | Linguistic/material coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Household or lineage | Who owes remembrance, offerings, and maintenance to particular dead or inherited powers? | Kinship terms, remembered names, recurring speech, and a maintained ritual location |
| Initiatory community | Who may learn, handle, or interpret particular practices? | Specialized vocabulary, training, permissions, and occasion-specific dress |
| Local public cult | Which community recognizes a shrine's authority and efficacy? | Place-names, festivals, patronage, and identifiable ritual personnel |
| Royal ancestor cult | How does dynastic memory authorize the reigning institution? | Praise, succession narratives, palace spaces, commemorative objects, and public display |

- “Family cult” does not imply one standardized temple in every nuclear household. Specify descent, residence, ritual responsibility, and the particular shrine community instead. [Bay 2008; Landry 2019]
- **Asen** are metal commemorative works used in relations with the dead; their scenes can evoke remembered lives, proverbs, and social identities rather than functioning as literal portraits. Their manufacture and meanings changed over time. [Bay 2008]
- Bay's account connects asen to imported materials/technologies and changing patronage: “ancestral” does not mean materially untouched by Atlantic commerce or modern markets.
- **Bocio** and other empowered assemblages require contextual interpretation; a bound or accumulated object is not automatically a deity portrait or a device for attacking someone. [Blier 1995]
- Invocation, ingredients, installation, maintenance, and authorized handling can matter to efficacy; a museum-isolated object may conceal those relationships. Do not supply invented ritual recipes. [Landry 2019]
- “Wife of a vodun” is a relational religious idiom, not a safe inference about an initiate's biological sex, marriage arrangements, or everyday clothing.
- Royal human sacrifice is historically documented; European sensationalism does not make all evidence fictitious. Conversely, contested numbers and royal ceremonies must not define all Fon life or contemporary vodun. [Law 1991; Bay 1998]

## Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́: a neighboring parallel, not a generic Fon mask
- **Established attribution:** Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ belongs to Yoruba-Nago communities in Benin, Nigeria, and Togo. A performance in Benin is not thereby ethnically Fon. [Lawal 1996; UNESCO 2008]
- The “Mothers” encompass important female powers and social relations, not simply a single imported mother-goddess archetype; ritual authority, esteem, ambivalence, and social criticism belong together.

| Dimension | Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ comparison | Fon/Dahomey boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Verbal action | Song, praise, irony, satire, and instruction | Compare permissions to speak publicly; do not relabel Yoruba texts as Fon palace speech. |
| Visual carrier | Carved headdress integrated with cloth, movement, and the performed body | A detached carving is not the complete costume; an asen or palace relief is a different medium. |
| Authority | Female ritual authority and gendered performance roles | Neither female authority nor men performing female-coded roles proves an earlier universal matriarchy. |
| Audience | Community performance that entertains while addressing conduct | Compare with court-authorized praise without assuming identical political institutions. |
| Change | New social references and technologies can enter imagery | Contemporary motifs do not invalidate a tradition, but cannot automatically be projected into earlier eras. |

- Nighttime **ẹ̀fẹ́** and daytime masking are documented performance patterns; verify community-specific scheduling rather than making the heritage-page summary an exhaustive script. [Lawal 1996]
- UNESCO's wording about a “former matriarchal order” is a heritage interpretation, **not established proof of a dated prehistoric social stage**. Separate that claim from its useful attribution and safeguarding information.
- Application: borrow the analytical problem—how costume authorizes otherwise risky social speech—not protected imagery, an entire ritual, or an assertion that all neighboring traditions are one system.

## Aja–Tado origin traditions and an era model
- **Contested history:** dynastic traditions connect Tado, Aja ancestry, Allada, and subsequent centers including Abomey. They are not a securely dated itinerary followed by an undifferentiated “Fon nation.” [Law 1991; Bay 1998]
- Leopard ancestry and royal descent narratives link sovereignty to extraordinary origins; preserve their status as narrated claims, not biological explanations. [Piqué & Rainer 1999]
- The story interpreting Dahomey through building on the “belly of Dan” is a political/etiological narrative with variant tellings, not an independently proven account of the name's original formation. [Piqué & Rainer 1999, pp. 9–11]
- Migration accounts can establish precedence, kinship, or a right to rule. Ask when a version was recorded and whether later conquests made particular genealogies politically useful. [Law 1991]

| Historical layer | Relatively secure staging anchor | What not to back-project |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier Aja–Tado / Allada traditions | Recalled origins and regional relationships; precise early chronology is uncertain | Exact migration dates, a complete royal chronicle, or a uniform ancestral costume |
| Seventeenth-century Abomey consolidation | Houegbadja is conventionally central to narratives of state formation; early reign dates are approximate | Every institution recorded in the nineteenth century already existing in finished form |
| Agaja and coastal expansion | Conquest of Allada in 1724 and Hueda/Whydah in 1727 | Coastal cults as timeless creations of the Abomey court |
| Eighteenth-century regional system | Oyo pressure/tribute, Atlantic trade, and exchanges among courts and cult communities | An isolated, culturally sealed kingdom |
| Nineteenth-century monarchy | Gezo, Glele, and Béhanzin; changing slave exports, palm-oil production, diplomacy, and military pressures | Palm oil as a clean replacement that automatically ended coerced labor |
| Conquest and colonial rule | French campaigns in 1890 and 1892–94; military defeat, exile, and later institutional reorganization | One conquest date erasing every household cult, royal memory, or artisan network |
| Postcolonial and present practice | Independent Dahomey in 1960; state renamed Benin in 1975; ongoing religious, artistic, and heritage transformations | Present-day festivals or museum displays as unchanged seventeenth-century scenes |

- Read this as overlapping political, ritual, commercial, and material chronologies—not “tribal → civilized → modern” evolution. [Law 1991; Bay 1998, 2008; Piqué & Rainer 1999]
- Date objects separately from the stories they depict: a recent appliqué can narrate an old reign; a restored wall can reproduce an older image without preserving every original surface.

## Palace registers, royal names, and emblematic memory
- **Register** here means an authorized way of speaking in a social setting, not a claim that the palace maintained a separate language or a phonographic written register.
- Piqué & Rainer (1999, pp. 8–9) describe royal heralds transmitting court history; their account foregrounds official narration. A court's demand for fidelity is not evidence that the surviving narrative never changed.
- Their account of enthronement distinguishes a royal **“strong name,” a name motto, and multiple emblems**: related identity devices, not automatically interchangeable translations. [Piqué & Rainer 1999, p. 26]

| Analytical register | Social authorization | Design question, not a reconstructed Fon grammar |
|---|---|---|
| Court praise/history | A recognized speaker voices dynastic claims | Which names and episodes are obligatory, and whose version may be corrected? |
| Royal titulature | Titles magnify claims to wealth, territory, and sovereignty | Which titles may subjects utter directly, and which require mediation? |
| Ritual/ancestral address | Office, initiation, or inherited obligation authorizes speech | Does a change of addressee require different names, objects, or clothing? |
| Diplomacy and delegated command | Messengers and intermediaries speak with constrained authority | What identifies the sender and limits what the envoy may promise? |

- Published royal titles include **dokunnon**, **simido**, and **ainon**, glossed in the Getty handbook (p. 26) as the master and possessor of all riches, the master of the world, and the eminent master of the earth respectively; these are that publication's spellings, checked here in its open PDF. Exact tone-marked transcriptions and usage restrictions remain `(to-verify)`; check a Fon-language source before presenting them as palace speech.
- Do not invent a complete taboo-word substitution list, special court conjugation, or universal rule against speaking a king's personal name: palace honorific grammar needs a text corpus and specialist verification `(to-verify)`.
- **Naming coverage limit:** royal name/motto/emblem practice is well supported here; birth-circumstance names, twin names, household sentence-names, and initiation renaming need a separate Fon onomastic corpus `(to-verify)`. Do not fill that gap with Akan day-names or Yoruba forms merely because they are West African.

| Royal example | Emblematic association in the cited handbook | Coupling lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Agaja | European ship among his emblems | Objects of Atlantic encounter can become royal signs; their political reading needs the associated account. |
| Gezo | Perforated jar, with collective action required to retain its contents | A proverb-like proposition can organize both an image and a claim to unity. |
| Glele | Lion; also the ritual knife of Gu | A king may have several signs; animal imagery and cult affiliation need not be identical systems. |
| Béhanzin | Shark; also an egg held by hands | Avoid reducing a reign to one mascot or deriving every sign from one literal etymology. |

- Examples: Piqué & Rainer (1999, pp. 26–31). Read **name/motto → image → patron → occasion → material carrier**, not “animal → universal meaning.”
- Their account also notes Adandozan's exclusion from an acknowledged dynastic sequence: royal memory can be edited by succession politics. An emblem list is a political artifact, not an infallible census of rulers.

## Costume and material signatures by function
| Context | Material reference | Use and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Royal ceremonial display | Appliqué banners/textiles, parasols, bas-reliefs, weapons, and regalia | Repeat a ruler's signs across media; do not call courtyard decoration the population's everyday dress. |
| Delegated royal voice | A **récade**, the messenger staff of the Fon palace; recent art-historical work separates the messenger's own term **wensagunkpo** from the thunder-priest's **sokpo** ([Capo Chichi 2023]); museum labels derive *récade* from Portuguese *recados* and describe it as the emblem that authenticated a messenger and his message ([NMAfA]) | Connect an object to authorized communication; because entitlement differs by office (royal messenger, So priest, ritual dancer), cite the specific object record before specifying it. |
| Ancestral remembrance | Asen, metalwork, and commemorative scenes | Link craft supply, patronage, remembered identity, and ritual use; not simply a detachable costume accessory. |
| Shrine/initiatory practice | Garments, beads, substances, and objects appropriate to a particular community and occasion | No single “vodun uniform”; exact deity/color/bead prescriptions remain local and need verification. |
| Neighboring masquerade | Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ headdress plus the complete textile/body ensemble | Keep attribution Yoruba-Nago; do not make it default Fon military equipment. |
| Military reconstruction | Period-specific evidence for arms, units, and dress | “Amazon” is an external classical comparison, not a garment specification or an explanation of every palace woman's role. |

- Palace material baseline: Piqué & Rainer (1999, pp. 26–31, 98–99); court household and gender: Bay (1998); ancestral metalwork: Bay (2008); Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ costume: Lawal (1996).
- A useful costume record separates **fiber/cloth supply, cutting and sewing, motif, entitlement, occasion, repair, and date**. “Traditional” is not a fabric technology.
- Do not assert an unchanged indigenous/foreign divide: Atlantic goods, local artisanship, royal commissions, colonial markets, and modern heritage commerce can inhabit the same object's history.
- Exact robe cuts, ordinary workwear, regimental uniforms, and sumptuary prohibitions are not established by this entry `(to-verify)`; obtain a dated object or image before specifying them.

## How to apply this reference to a fictional culture
1. Choose a locality, institutional scale, and time layer before selecting sounds, myths, or clothing; record alternatives instead of writing a single timeless composite.
2. Build the language's phonology and tone realization first; establish which names survive into praise and which forms are borrowed or archaic.
3. Decide whether an instrument conveys lexical surrogacy, conventional signals, musical accompaniment, or several separately learned modes; mark this as a design choice, not Fon evidence.
4. Model powers through custodianship, obligations, locality, and transmission; allow competing genealogies without assuming participants regard variation as error.
5. Give each speech setting an authorized speaker, addressee, audience, and material credential; distinguish royal praise from neighborhood satire and family remembrance.
6. Create a name–motto–emblem relationship, then test how it changes across cloth, a wall, a staff, a spoken formula, and a remembered ancestor's object.
7. Give materials a supply chain and chronology; a new technology can change an old institution's visible expression without changing all its meanings at once.
8. Test a scene for intelligibility: who recognizes the name or sign, who misses it, who may interpret it, and what social risk attaches to the interpretation?
9. Hand lore-shokunin the alternatives, source/locality/date tags, and uncertainties; do not declare a fictional dynasty, pantheon, origin story, or costume canon here.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- A **multimodal political identity system**: a name becomes a proposition, an emblem, a textile commission, a narrated wall, and an authorized performance—without becoming an alphabet.
- **Religion as maintained relationships**: shrines, family obligations, specialists, and materials generate social constraints that a deity list alone cannot provide.
- **Register as institutional access**: who may praise, criticize, interpret, or command can matter more than ornamental “archaic” vocabulary.
- **Historically layered design**: migration claims, state expansion, trade goods, ritual change, and museum representation can coexist without collapsing into one era.
- These are transferable analytical relationships, not permission to copy sacred prescriptions or author lore-shokunin's world bible.

## Provenance cautions and unresolved work
- Book records verify publications and synopses, not every page-level proposition. Apart from the specified Getty passages, the open acoustic article, and the récade article read in round-1 triage [Capo Chichi 2023], this is a calibrated synthesis rather than a claim to have reread every cited work in full.
- Early European trader, missionary, and colonial descriptions require source criticism; they could exoticize sacrifice, sexuality, gender, and religion while serving commercial or imperial arguments. Later ethnographies also select informants and organize variable traditions into tidy systems.
- Palace accounts have their own interests: dynastic legitimacy, suppression of defeated rivals, and official memory. Do not correct colonial bias by treating court tradition as politically neutral.
- Heritage publicity, art-market labels, tourist performance, and contemporary initiation networks are evidence of particular present-day contexts, not automatic windows onto an untouched ancient past.
- “Gun-gun,” ordinary naming corpora, court-specific grammar, early migration dates, and precise garment regulations remain `(to-verify)`; resolve them with named performers/specialists, a localized text corpus, or dated objects before making stronger claims.
- No source here warrants universal Fon color meanings, a fixed pantheon genealogy, an exact ancient migration timetable, or a reconstructed private ritual script. Keep uncertainty visible in any downstream handoff.
