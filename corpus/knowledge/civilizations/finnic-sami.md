# Finnic and Sámi: language, sounded identity, ritual images, and dress

- topic: Finnic/Sámi reference base; Uralic inheritance versus substrate, joik as a situated register, noaidi drum interpretation, gákti as an identity map, and contested sky/thunder terminology.
- sources: Bakró-Nagy, Laakso & Skribnik (eds., 2022); Aikio (2012); Lönnrot (compiler, 1849); Rydving (2010); Larsson (2013); Kelemen (2022); Gaski (1999); Länsman (1999); Magga (2022); Aalto & Lehtola (2017). Editions, links, and verification limits appear below.

## Scope and calibration

- **Not one civilization:** Finnic and Sámi are distinct branches of Uralic, not interchangeable names for a single northern people or religion (Bakró-Nagy et al. 2022).
- **Finnic** includes Finnish, Karelian, Estonian, Veps, Ingrian, Votic, and Livonian; a Finnish literary epic cannot stand for every Finnic tradition.
- **Sámi** includes distinct languages and communities across Sápmi; North, South, Lule, Inari, Skolt, and Kildin evidence must retain its location.
- Use **attested** for a claim in a named source, **inferred** for a historical reconstruction, and **design transfer** for a fictional technique; these are not equivalent confidence grades.
- An attested missionary statement establishes that the writer reported something, not that the statement describes all Sámi, an unchanged ancient belief, or the speaker's intended meaning.
- Working comparison unit: **language/community × locality × livelihood × date × genre × recorder**. “Uralic mythology” alone is too coarse for a usable evidence record.
- Coverage is strongest here for western Sámi source criticism and Finnish/Karelian-derived literary material; Estonian, Livonian, Veps, and eastern Sámi ritual histories are thin.
- Do not fill those gaps by projecting the best-documented western example eastward. Eastern joik genre names are documented (below), but exact eastern clothing and ritual correspondences remain `(to-verify)`.

## Language family, writing, and substrate: three different questions

| Question | Reference finding | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogical relationship | Finnic and Sámi are Uralic; neither is a dialect of the other (Bakró-Nagy et al. 2022). | Give related fictional branches regular correspondences, not identical words with decorative diacritics. |
| Structural profile | Rich inflection and suffixing are useful starting points, but phonology, case inventories, gradation, and syntax vary. | Choose one language-level reference; do not transfer Finnish vowel harmony or grammar wholesale to every Sámi language. |
| Contact history | Germanic and Finnic contacts contributed different lexical strata to Sámi (Aikio 2012: 68–80). | Date imported cloth, tools, ritual titles, and administrative terms independently. |
| Substrate | Aikio argues for vocabulary and place-names from otherwise unknown, non-Uralic predecessor languages (2012: 80–90). | A Uralic-speaking population can preserve older landscape names and ecological vocabulary without preserving an identifiable older pantheon. |
| Writing | Modern Finnic standards use Latin-based writing; Sámi standards are language-specific, with Latin-based and Cyrillic-based traditions represented (Bakró-Nagy et al. 2022). | Record script, orthographic standard, schooling, and religious translation as historical variables. |
| Ritual graphics | A drum's painted figures are not thereby an alphabet or a transcription of spoken sentences (Rydving 2010, ch. 3). | Keep a graphic lexicon distinct from the writing system unless independent evidence connects them. |

- **Terminology repair:** “Uralic substrate” may describe Uralic influence beneath some other language, but it is misleading as a blanket name for Sámi inheritance. Specify the receiving language and proposed donor layer.
- Aikio's **unknown-substrate test** combines quantitative, structural, irregular-correspondence, semantic, and onomastic criteria; an unexplained word by itself does not establish a lost language (2012: 83).
- His account links some obscure ecological vocabulary and topographic names to language shift; the donor languages are not directly documented. Their grammar, self-names, myths, and costumes cannot be recovered by invention.
- His Middle Iron Age expansion model is a linguistic-historical reconstruction, not a witnessed migration chronicle or a universal consensus date for Sámi identity (Aikio 2012: 63–67, 87–90).
- Language spread is not proof of population replacement; continuity of artifacts is not proof of continuity of language (Aikio 2012: 80–82).
- **Design transfer:** build an inherited grammatical core, a locally retained landscape lexicon, and successive trade/clerical loans; do not label every opaque root “sacred.”
- Orthographic example: North Sámi letters such as **č, đ, ŋ, š, ŧ, ž** belong to a specific standard, not an interchangeable “Arctic alphabet.” Kildin's Cyrillic-based practice requires a separate reference.
- For a fictional script history, distinguish oral composition, missionary literacy, state spelling, and later reclamation. A change of script need not imply a change of language family.

## Mythic cores: compare functions without manufacturing a common pantheon

| Evidence cluster | Safe reference use | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Finnish/Karelian-derived runo epic in the *Kalevala* | Väinämöinen couples knowledge and song; Ilmarinen couples smithcraft and extraordinary making; Louhi and Pohjola organize major narrative conflicts (Lönnrot 1849). | Literary agents are not automatically members of a historically standardized worship pantheon. |
| Sampo cycle | Making, prosperity, possession, and conflict can be bound to an exceptional artifact. | The Sampo's definitive historical identification is not settled by the poem's narrative role. |
| Finnic thunder invocations | Ukko is a major thunder/heaven-associated name in Finnish reference material. | Do not replace all Finnic or Sámi celestial figures with “Ukko under another name.” |
| Western Sámi religious descriptions | Sun, thunder, hunting, childbirth, the dead, and relations with particular places appear in the recorded religious field (Rydving 2010; Kelemen 2022). | A functional index is not proof of one stable pan-Sámi divine genealogy. |
| Áhkká-associated figures | Historical accounts name Máttaráhkká and figures commonly rendered Sáráhkká, Uksáhkká, and Juoksáhkká (Kelemen 2022: 76–77). | Preserve source spelling, locality, and differences in assigned roles; do not infer one universal “triple goddess.” |
| Sacred landscapes | Sacrificial places and their names can complement textual evidence (Rydving 2010, chs. 2, 7–8). | A striking rock is not automatically an attested sieidi, and a place-name alone does not recover its rites. |

- *Kalevala* staging: distinguish collected oral performances, Lönnrot's compilation, the 1835 publication, the expanded 1849 literary work, and later national reception.
- The 1849 text is a primary source for that edited epic, not an untouched prehistoric religious manual; Karelian and other singers are not merely anonymous suppliers of “Finnish antiquity.”
- Do not equate Finnish **tietäjä**, Finnish **noita**, and North Sámi **noaidi** as identical offices simply because translations call them sages or shamans.
- **Design transfer:** couple an occupational register to a narrative competence—remembering origins, making, negotiating with places—then separately decide who authorizes its use.
- Reject a universal sequence “animism → shamanism → pantheon → monotheism.” These are not reliable stages through which every northern society passed.

## Sky-god terminology debate: words, offices, and equations

“Sky god” is an analyst's grouping. A word for sky, a thunder-agent's name, a kinship title, and the Christian translation of “God” need not designate the same historical being.

| Form or cluster | Attested/comparative issue | Calibration and craft rule |
|---|---|---|
| Finnish **jumala**; Sámi forms reported as **Ipmil, Jubmel, Jupmele** | Related-looking religious vocabulary participates in translation and contact histories; Kelemen reviews these forms (2022: 73). | Do not infer an unchanged Proto-Uralic sky-father from modern “God” glosses. Kelemen reports the route documented in the reference etymological dictionaries: Finno-Volgaic *juma, “possibly adopted from Finnish to Saami with Swedish mediation” (2022: 73, citing UEW 638 and SSA 1:247) — a dictionary-based proposal, not a documented borrowing event. |
| **Radien / Raedie**, with compounds | Scholarship connects the title to Scandinavian vocabulary of ruling; historical descriptions assign celestial and life-giving functions (Kelemen 2022: 73–74). | A borrowed title can be locally reinterpreted; it is not proof of wholesale importation of a religion. |
| **Horagalles / Hovragaellies / Hovre** | Larsson locates the relevant forms especially in southern South Sámi and discusses a Scandinavian Þórr connection (2013: 168–172). | Strong case for localized contact history; not the universal Sámi name of thunder. |
| **Dierpmis / Tiermes** | Etymological explanations differ; Aikio actually includes *dierpmis* among items of unclear origin in a potential-substrate discussion (2012: 84). | Neither an “original Uralic god” nor a settled Indo-European loan is established merely by repeating a name equation. |
| Grandfather/old-man titles | Kelemen surveys **Aja / Aijeke** and compares Finnish **äijä / Ukko** terminology (2022: 75). | Shared respectful semantics can develop without proving that all named figures are identical or all words cognate. |
| **Beaivi** and variant sun names | Celestial vocabulary and religious personification are connected, but gender, narrative, and cult descriptions vary regionally (Kelemen 2022: 74–75). | Never deduce the gender or genealogy of a divine figure from “sun worship” as a general category. |

- **Specific citation warning (re-checked 2026-10-17):** Kelemen (2022: 76) attributes an Indo-European-loan view of Dierpmis to Aikio (2012: 84), but Aikio's p. 84 instead lists *dierpmis* in his substrate word list among items of unknown origin. Report that attribution as Kelemen's own move, not as Aikio's finding.
- Separate three inference steps: **word etymology → distribution of a name → history of a cult**. Evidence sufficient for the first may be insufficient for the other two.
- Larsson discusses alternative explanations for phonological irregularity in Hovre-related forms; his borrowing chronology is not a date for the birth of the thunder cult (2013: 169–172).
- Missionary descriptions may translate indigenous powers through “supreme God,” heaven, devil, or soul vocabulary. A neat celestial hierarchy may partly belong to the recorder's explanatory system.
- **Design transfer:** allow neighboring communities to use an inherited term, a loaned honorific, and a clerical translation for overlapping functions; retain disagreement rather than forcing a master concordance.

## Joik register: sounded relationship, not decorative chanting

- **Reference anchor:** North Sámi **luohti** is a situated tradition within the broader field commonly called joik/yoik. The neighbouring language areas carry their own genre names — **leu'dd** (Skolt Sámi), **livđe** (Inari Sámi), **vuolle** (Lule and Pite Sámi), **vuöllie** (Ume Sámi), and **vuelie** (South Sámi) (Renzi 2025) — so treat them as language-specific terms with their own forms and contents, not interchangeable translations of *luohti*.
- Ursula Länsman presents joik as bringing its subject into presence rather than merely describing it; this is a practitioner's account, not a universal acoustic law (1999).
- Gaski's **culturally internal interpretation** foregrounds the knowledge needed to understand a yoik text (1999). Use **indexicality and register** as analytic tools, not as purported Indigenous genre names.

| Register dimension | Question to ask of a reference performance | Fictional coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Referent | Who or what is recognized through the melody, words, voice, or gesture? | Link a musical identity to a person/place without making it a spoken personal name. |
| Shared knowledge | What can an informed listener recognize that a stranger cannot? | Make interpretation depend on kinship, local history, and occasion, not only dictionary translation. |
| Text and vocables | Does reference depend on explicit lexical content? | Nonlexical syllables need not be semantically empty; avoid inventing a faux Sámi “spell text.” |
| Authority | Who may perform, disclose, adapt, or record this material? | Keep subject, maker, performer, and permitted audience as separate fields. |
| Context | Is this intimate remembering, social interaction, ritual evidence, a recording, or stage work? | A change of audience changes meaning and permission, not automatically authenticity. |
| Era | What changed through religious suppression, revival, and new media? | Let a register survive, become private, or be publicly reworked without claiming total disappearance. |

- Do not prescribe “always pentatonic,” “wordless,” “always circular,” or one timbre as a diagnostic for every Sámi genre; such generalizations outrun this entry's evidence.
- Do not equate all joik with noaidi ritual. Historical religious uses and ordinary social uses must be evidenced separately.
- Länsman's own discussion accompanies a band combining older joiks and contemporary dance beats; contemporary innovation is not evidence of cultural falsity (1999).
- **Coupling test:** the same fictional person may be recognizable through a public name, kin relation, garment, and sounded portrait, while each medium reveals a different subset of identity.
- Record a performed example's language, performer, referent if disclosed, occasion, recording date, and permission context. A transcript alone is not a complete performance record.

## Noaidi drum symbolism: an object-specific interpretive discipline

- **Noaidi** is the North Sámi reference term used here for a ritual specialist; it is not a title to attach to every singer, craftsperson, or Sámi character.
- Rydving's central rule is decisive: interpret drum figures through the preserved explanations of their owners where available, not through a universal visual dictionary (2010, ch. 3).
- Surviving descriptions and objects passed through collection, confiscation, mission, and museum systems. Their survival is a selection process, not a census of past practice.
- Kelemen discusses alder-bark pigment and hunting-associated symbolism (2022: 76–77); a material association does not authorize a fixed meaning for every red mark.
- **Bounded motif examples:** her collated descriptions associate a hammer with Horagállis and a shovel with a wind figure (2022: 75–76). These are reported interpretations with source histories, not a verified key for an unprovenanced drum.

| Layer to record | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Object | Material, construction, painted positions, repairs, collection history. | The owner's understanding of every figure. |
| Owner's explanation | A situated interpretation attached to a particular drum. | A key to every other drum or every century. |
| Recorder's transcript | What an intermediary preserved in a particular language and institutional setting. | An unfiltered quotation or independence from other missionary texts. |
| Comparative arrangement | Repeated layouts or proposed regional similarities. | A universal three-level cosmos, alphabet, or standard pantheon. |
| Later reconstruction | A modern hypothesis about missing meanings or relationships. | Direct Indigenous testimony from the object's period. |

- **Symbolic-system method:** diagram the relations among figures, regions of the surface, performer, and action; avoid treating symbols as isolated clip-art meanings.
- If a source labels a figure sun, thunder, animal, church, or dwelling, retain that attribution and its uncertainty. Shape alone cannot decide between cosmological, topographic, and social reference.
- Do not combine a central-solar layout from one example and a vertically partitioned layout from another into a supposedly “complete Sámi drum.” Regional layout claims need an object corpus `(to-verify)`.
- A cross-like mark is not automatically Christian, and a Christian-looking feature is not automatically a collector's contamination; both require object-specific evidence.
- Evidence concerning divination and altered states must remain distinct; a generic “trance drum” caption suppresses differences in reported use.
- **Design transfer:** invent an internally coherent diagram whose meanings depend on its maker's journeys, relationships, and ritual task; do not copy an extant sacred surface and supply fictional translations.

## Gákti: identity map with changing conventions

- **Gákti** is the North Sámi term used here, not a universal name for every Sámi garment. Magga's framework is a **double perspective**: dress as inherited practice and as something on the pulse of contemporary time (2022).
- Design, material, color, and pattern can communicate local provenance, family, and gender; these are socially learned readings, not a universal cryptographic table (Magga 2022: 43–45, as discussed in the NiCHE article cited below).
- **Duodji** names a field of Sámi craft knowledge/practice, not simply a decorative look. Garment authority includes making, relationship, use, and transmission.
- **Dated material anchor:** Georgi's 1776 account, summarized by Kelemen (2022: 72), describes leather tanning and garment embroidery with gold, silver, and woollen thread. This is an outsider's generalized report, not a specification for every locality or an ancient outfit.
- **Lexicon/material coupling:** Aikio discusses a tanned-leather/strap borrowing comparison (2012: 75) and possible substrate vocabulary for sinew thread and hide anatomy (84–86). Craft terminology can preserve a different historical layer from garment fashion.

| Visible/material dimension | Coupled system | Calibration rule |
|---|---|---|
| Cut and arrangement of trims | Regional/local belonging and learned recognition. | Start with a named community and dated garment; do not assemble a pan-Sámi “best of.” |
| Material and technique | Climate, work, skill, access to cloth and other supplies. | Do not infer isolation from a traditional silhouette or antiquity from a modern example. |
| Family-associated variation | Kinship, teaching, inheritance, maker choices. | Do not assign a particular trim to an actual family without documentation. |
| Gendered conventions | Social roles and community-specific dress practice. | Description is not a timeless biological rule or a license to police present identities. |
| Festive/public use | Occasion, political visibility, belonging, self-representation. | Contemporary ceremonial visibility does not make the garment a theatrical costume. |
| Change and experimentation | Generational taste, fashion, new materials, reclamation. | Innovation can express continuity rather than its loss. |

- **Marital-status caution:** claims that every belt, button, hat, or neckline has a single marital-status meaning require locality, date, garment part, and an authoritative source `(to-verify)`.
- Distinguish working/weather clothing, occasion-specific ensembles, museum garments, and contemporary dress. They need not display identical information.
- Do not map herding status directly onto Sámi identity. Fishing, hunting, craft, coastal, inland, and urban histories cannot be reduced to the image of one reindeer-herding outfit.
- Keep Finnic linen/wool, local peasant dress, and later national-costume reconstructions in a separate reference track; no single “Finnic costume” is established here. Specific ensembles remain `(to-verify)`.
- **Design transfer:** build a clothing grammar with a few local variables, a maker/transmission network, and rules of occasion; couple its changes to trade and politics rather than borrowing real identifying combinations.

## Era model: parallel tracks rather than a progress ladder

| Reference horizon | Evidence and change | Staging rule |
|---|---|---|
| Reconstructed prehistory | Inheritance, contact strata, substrate vocabulary, and place-names (Aikio 2012). | Separate linguistic models from eyewitness history; no attested prehistoric full costume/pantheon package. |
| Medieval northern contacts | Norse texts depict trade, difference, magic, and relationships through literary conventions (Aalto & Lehtola 2017). | Do not read a saga's magical outsider as transparent ethnography. |
| Late medieval/early modern livelihood changes | Large-scale pastoralism has a history; it is not the eternal condition of all Sámi communities (Aikio 2012: 86). | Date herd scale and seasonal labor by locality; do not make a single “reindeer revolution” fleet-wide. |
| Seventeenth–eighteenth-century missions and states | Religious suppression, recording, translation, and collection shape western evidence (Rydving 2010). | Written visibility can increase while the practices being described are persecuted. |
| Nineteenth-century collection and nation-making | *Kalevala* editions reorganize oral material into a literary-national artifact (Lönnrot 1835/1849). | A collector's edition date is not the date of origin of every motif. |
| Twentieth century to present | Suppression, cultural assertion, new media, and changing dress coexist (Länsman 1999; Magga 2022). | Contemporary Sámi people are not survivors outside history or automatically nontraditional. |

- Date **language, literacy, livelihood, religion, institutions, and dress separately**. Their transitions need not synchronize.
- Avoid “Iron Age religion plus modern gákti plus timeless joik” as an unmarked composite. It can be a fictional proposal only if each divergence is explicit.
- Christianity and Sámi identity are not mutually exclusive categories; nor must every present practice be explained as a secretly preserved pre-Christian rite.

## Naming practices: identity across speech, records, places, and song

- Treat an individual's official written name, locally used name, kin-based reference, and joik association as potentially different channels, not interchangeable labels.
- Exact Sámi genealogical-name order and patronymic/matronymic practices need a language- and locality-specific source `(to-verify)`; do not invent one universal naming formula.
- Preserve source orthography separately from a normalized display form. State and church spellings, language-specific standards, and present self-identification can disagree.
- Aikio's substrate place-names show how a name may survive language shift without remaining transparent to current speakers (2012: 86–87).
- Some repeated name elements are reconstructed as possible former topographic terms; these are hypotheses, not a recovered dictionary of an extinct people's language.
- Old Norse **finnar** is an outsider designation in the saga material, not a safe one-to-one substitution for modern “Finns” (Aalto & Lehtola 2017: 10–11).
- Do not treat a joik's referent as proof that its melody can be freely used as that person's public identifier; association is not blanket permission.
- **Design transfer:** define separate systems for household address, ancestry reference, official registration, landscape names, and sounded identity; specify who can connect the records.
- Naming packet fields: language/standard; endonym/exonym; given/byname/kin/place category; context of use; inflected forms if checked; date; bearer-preferred form; evidence status.
- Avoid “name generators” that attach Finnish **-nen** or Sámi-looking consonants to arbitrary roots. A productive suffix, inherited surname, place-name, and ethnonym are different naming mechanisms.

## Coupling workflow for a fictional culture

1. Select one bounded reference locality and era; use another tradition only as an explicitly labeled comparison.
2. Build lexical layers: inherited core, ecological substrate candidates, trade loans, religious translations, and official terminology.
3. Build an authority matrix: who names people, recognizes melodies, interprets ritual images, teaches garment-making, and licenses disclosure?
4. Assign each medium its information: a word categorizes; a sounded portrait evokes; a garment indexes belonging; an object organizes ritual relations. Do not make every medium encode everything.
5. Introduce one historical pressure—conversion, a border, trade access, or schooling—and trace different consequences for vocabulary, performance, dress, and naming.
6. Test a misunderstanding: an outsider translates a title, misreads a trim, or takes a melody out of context. Explain the mismatch structurally rather than assigning mystical racial perception.
7. Prepare a proposal-only handoff to lore-shokunin: options, constraints, unresolved choices, source confidence, and continuity risks. Do not write a world-bible fact here.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **Layered inheritance:** one language family can overlay multiple ecological vocabularies, place-name strata, and contact histories without one-to-one descent of religion.
- **Plural identity media:** names, sounded relationships, garment conventions, and ritual graphics can identify the same person differently rather than redundantly.
- **Uneven legibility:** local competence determines which signals are readable; shared knowledge is not a supernatural ethnic faculty.
- **Contact without replacement:** loaned titles and imported materials can be incorporated into locally controlled systems; resemblance does not settle ownership or origin.
- **Asynchronous eras:** let language standardization, religious change, garment fashion, and livelihood transform at different rates.
- **Continuity through innovation:** new performance settings and new clothing choices can continue a tradition, while also provoking real internal disagreement.
- Transfer these relationships into newly invented systems; do not lift sacred names, personal joiks, or identifying garment combinations as an aesthetic kit.

## Provenance cautions

- Colonial and missionary sources are indispensable but interested witnesses; modern scholarship must examine dependence between accounts, translation choices, coercion, and geographical reach.
- Georgi's 1776 account is mediated through earlier writers, not a fresh independent witness to every listed god; Kelemen identifies Leem and Högström in that chain (2022: 79–80).
- A medieval saga, an eighteenth-century report, an 1849 compiled epic, a museum object, and a present practitioner's explanation have different evidential jobs. Do not flatten them into one “ancient northern worldview.”
- Chronicle evidence is especially inadequate for reconstructing a continuous early pan-Sámi theology. Absence from a record does not establish absence of practice.
- This entry does not authenticate a specific drum diagram or present a marital-status codebook; those items remain explicitly `(to-verify)`. Regional joik genre names are sourced individually above rather than collapsed into one equivalence table.
- Living Sámi knowledge and dress are not abandoned cultural resources. Publication or museum display does not itself grant permission to reproduce a personal performance, a family-associated design, or a sacred object.
- Keep unresolved provenance attached to each proposed transfer when handing it to lore-shokunin; that sibling, not this reference file, decides whether anything becomes fictional canon.
