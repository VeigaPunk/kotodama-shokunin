## Scope and evidence grammar

- **Boundary:** extract generative relationships, not a fictional Parthia, authorized canon, or a ready-made national character.
- Distinguish Parthia, the northeastern Iranian region, from the much larger Arsacid realm and from neighboring societies sharing its fashions.
- **Attested:** a named object or passage actually bears the feature; an ancient author's assertion remains that author's testimony.
- **Reconstruction:** scholarship connects surviving traces across languages, regions, or centuries; the connection is not a contemporary transcript.
- **Design:** an explicitly fictional mechanism suggested by the evidence, not a claim about historical Parthians.
- Use `(to-verify)` for unresolved readings, dates, source access, or institutional generalizations; do not convert plausible inference into attestation.
- Strongest working anchors here: a catalogued Ecbatana coin type, Isidore's route descriptions, and Plutarch's described battle operations.
- Thin areas: everyday speech at Ecbatana, ordinary people's wardrobe, Arsacid bardic training, and an internally narrated court myth cycle.

## Era model: overlapping institutions, not a civilization ladder

| Working stage | Historical anchor | Coupling to model | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Regional establishment, third century BCE | Arsacid origins in northeastern Iran; conventional dynastic era begins 247 BCE | A founder-name and regional riding idiom become durable political signs | Era epoch, accession, and territorial conquest are not necessarily the same event |
| Imperial expansion, second century BCE | Incorporation of Media and Mesopotamia; inherited Hellenistic urban institutions | Iranian dynastic claims coexist with Greek coin language and civic audiences | Do not label every city culturally identical to the court |
| Roman-facing monarchy, first century BCE–first century CE | Carrhae, 53 BCE; Vologases I's first-century CE issues | Cavalry prestige, elite patronage, inherited coin formulas, and added royal-name markers | Military narrative and mint practice are different evidence streams |
| Later Arsacid plurality, second–early third century CE | Continuing Greek-derived legends beside Iranian royal names on some issues | Different mints and patrons can preserve different symbolic registers | Reign dates, ruler numbering, and some mint assignments vary between catalogues |
| Political replacement and cultural afterlives | Sasanian victory in 224 CE; later Parthian-language and Armenian evidence | Institutions and stories may outlive the dynasty under different patrons | A later manuscript is not automatically an Arsacid-era composition |

- This is a **design staging model**, not a newly established chronological typology; historical orientation follows Hopkins's linked coin and language records.
- Select a region, medium, and date before combining features: a first-century BCE military vignette cannot silently inherit a medieval storyteller's repertoire.
- Avoid a linear “Greek imitation → pure Iranian awakening → decadence” plot. Script choice, mint skill, patronage, and audience can change independently.
- Catalogue crosswalk requirement: retain author, edition, type number, specimen, and attributed mint rather than copying a ruler's Roman numeral alone.

## Language family, writing, and the split between script and speech

- Parthian is a **Northwestern Iranian** language of the Middle Iranian period; it is not a variety of Greek or simply another name for Middle Persian. [Hopkins, “Parthian Language”]
- Middle Persian belongs to the Southwestern Iranian grouping; the historical label *Pahlavi* can obscure which language or writing tradition is intended.
- Parthian writing derives from Aramaic and can retain Aramaic heterograms: an inherited written form may be read with an Iranian lexical equivalent.
- Therefore an Aramaic-looking string does not, by itself, establish the spoken language of a mint worker, courtier, or market customer.
- Greek on coins is direct evidence for an institutional written register, not a census of Greek-speaking subjects.
- Nisa's administrative ostraca provide a different domain from royal coin legends; later Manichaean texts provide another script and transmission setting. [Hopkins, “Parthian Language”]
- Do not treat a later Manichaean manuscript as evidence that Arsacid courts practiced Manichaeism or used that manuscript's orthography.

| Surface | What can safely be extracted | What cannot be inferred without another source |
|---|---|---|
| Royal coin | Public title, graphic formula, portrait convention, abbreviated name | Universal spoken language or individual literacy |
| Administrative ostracon | A local accounting practice and its written categories | A complete conversational lexicon for the empire |
| Greek or bilingual inscription | A particular public audience and translation occasion | Equal status for every language in all institutions |
| Later religious manuscript | Attested language within a dated transmission history | An unchanged Arsacid vernacular or court theology |

- **Design move:** assign spoken language, script, reader training, and prestige separately; let professional scribes preserve an older graph while audiences pronounce something else.
- Lexicon record fields: `form / language / script / date / site / genre / literal reading / interpreted sense / confidence`.

## Arsacid coinage: bilingual is not necessarily parallel translation

- **Worked anchor:** Sellwood type **71.1**, attributed to Vologases I and the Ecbatana mint, as documented with specimens on Hopkins's Vologases I page.
- The obverse has a diademed royal bust and the short Aramaic-character inscription transliterated **wl**; the reverse has a seated archer with a bow and a partly blundered Greek legend.
- This couples royal identification, inherited imperial titulature, portrait dress, and a durable archer emblem on one portable object.
- “Bilingual coinage” is useful shorthand, but this example is more precisely a **mixed-script issue with unequal textual functions**: a short name marker is not a translation of the long Greek formula.
- The catalogue calls the characters “Aramaic”; distinguish the script ancestry from the Iranian royal name being represented. The letters `wl` are read as a short form of the royal name — compare the fuller Aramaic spelling `wlgšy mlk’` on later Vologases IV issues — so the vocalization cannot be recovered from the short sign alone. [Hopkins, “Inscriptions”; Nabel (2017) on Sellwood 71.1]
- Early Arsaces I types 3ii and 4ii are also listed with Aramaic legends; do not describe Vologases I as inventing all Aramaic use on Arsacid coins. [Hopkins, “Inscriptions”]

| Element | Attested or normalized example | Coupled function | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Dynastic name | `ΑΡΣΑΚΟΥ`, “of Arsaces” | Founder identity persists across rulers | Not every coin bearing Arsaces depicts the founder |
| Sovereignty formula | `ΒΑΣΙΛΕΩΣ ΒΑΣΙΛΕΩΝ`, “of the King of Kings” | A claim about hierarchy in Greek grammatical form | A title is political assertion, not proof of administrative centralization |
| Relational epithet | `ΦΙΛΕΛΛΗΝΟΣ`, “philhellene/friend of the Greeks” | Addresses a Hellenic political-cultural register | Does not measure private cultural preference or ethnic ancestry |
| Name abbreviation | `wl` on the cited Ecbatana type | Additional royal identification in an Aramaic-derived graphic register | Not a complete second-language legend |
| Portrait furnishings | Diadem, earring, neck torque in the type 71.1 description | Dress and bodily presentation make sovereignty visible | A numismatic portrait is not an ordinary person's wardrobe |
| Seated archer | Bow-bearing figure on drachm reverses | Repeated dynastic visual identity | Identifying the figure as a specific ancestor or deity needs type-specific argument |

- Greek examples above are **normalized formula components**, not a diplomatic transcription reconstructed from the cited damaged legend.
- Some later issues retain barely legible Greek-derived legends alongside Iranian royal-name forms. “Blundered” describes the engraving, not an entire population's intelligence. [Hopkins, “Inscriptions”]
- **Design move:** preserve an old prestige script on one face while adding a locally legible name on the other; let a new regime change a portrait or epithet before replacing the entire monetary grammar.
- Verification packet for a fictional coin system: denomination, circulation zone, issuing authority, intended readers, exact legend, image, clothing, and which feature changed from the preceding issue.

## Cavalry culture: technique, supply, hierarchy, and outsider rhetoric

- **Anchor:** Plutarch, *Crassus* 21.6–7 and 23–25, describing Surena's force at Carrhae; this is a later Greek biography about a Roman disaster, not a Parthian drill manual.
- At 24.6 he explicitly describes shooting during flight and compares Parthians with Scythians: “Parthian shot” should not imply exclusive invention.
- Separate **rearward shooting while moving away** from **feigned retreat that draws an enemy into a trap**; they can cooperate but are not synonyms. Compare 24.6 with 25.2–3.
- At 25.1, camels carrying replacement arrows sustain the missile attack; the useful cultural mechanism is a supply organization, not magically inexhaustible archers.
- At 21.6–7, the narrative ties Surena's wealth, armed following, and hereditary coronation privilege together: elite power is material and ceremonial as well as martial.
- Distinguish lighter mounted archers from heavily armored horsemen with long spears in 24–25; do not force both into one universal costume or social role.

| Observable practice | Candidate fictional institutions | Vocabulary and costume coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Shooting while withdrawing | Training that distinguishes controlled withdrawal from rout | Separate honor terms for disciplined retreat and abandonment; riding-compatible sleeves and equipment placement |
| Arrow resupply | Storekeepers, transport contracts, fletchers, and accountable officers | Technical terms for bundles, replacement stocks, and transport duty; status need not follow battlefield visibility |
| Wealthy armed household | Patronage linking land, horses, retainers, and ceremonial rights | Different address forms for king, commander, dependent, and client; dress displays patronage as well as rank |
| Mounted archery plus armored shock | Specialist equipment and differentiated training | Distinct occupational lexicons rather than “all warriors wear the same exotic armor” |
| Drums and armor display in the narrative | A designed relationship between sound, visibility, command, and intimidation | Ceremonial sound can signal deployment; bright metal and concealing coverings are situational tools |

- These institutional rows are **design hypotheses**, not a reconstruction of a complete Arsacid social constitution.
- Avoid unqualified “feudal knights”: analogy with medieval Europe can conceal different landholding, dependency, and succession arrangements.
- Plutarch's descriptions of Surena's beauty, cosmetics, and dress at 24.2 expose Greek gendered judgments; do not adopt “effeminate” as an objective indigenous category.
- Do not import medieval stirrups, heraldry, or chivalric oaths without an independently dated equipment or institutional source.

## Ecbatana: build a road lexicon from interfaces, not invented loanwords

- Ecbatana, modern Hamadan, lies in Media, not in the northeastern province of Parthia; an Arsacid city need not be culturally identical to the dynasty's homeland.
- **Direct route anchor:** Isidore, *Parthian Stations* §6, calls Ecbatana Media's metropolis and treasury and reports a sanctuary of Anaitis.
- The same regional sequence includes a customs point at Bazigraban, a royal residence at Adrapana, villages, and stations; §7 continues toward Rhaga and the Caspian Gates.
- Keep those functions distinct: treasury, customs collection, residence, sanctuary, and stopping place are not interchangeable names for a “Silk Road city.”
- Hopkins's “Parthian Mints” places Ecbatana on the east–west High Road and reports its royal-residence and mint roles; mint assignments remain catalogue interpretations.
- **Silk Road is modern analytical terminology**, not an attested Parthian self-name for one road or one coordinated trading organization.
- Isidore's itinerary is not an Ecbatana merchant phrasebook and does not itself supply a Parthian word for silk.

| Semantic field to design | Documented stimulus | Useful lexical distinctions | Evidence limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Fiscal exchange | Customs point and treasury, §6 | Toll, assessed value, receipt, custody, payment, exemption | These are design slots, not translated local terms |
| Travel stages | Stations, villages, wells, and route distances, §§1–7 | Stage, stopping place, water access, escort, route obligation | Do not assume a modern hotel or a later caravanserai institution |
| Distance | Schoeni in Schoff's translation | Official distance versus travel effort; local conversion conventions | Do not impose one universal kilometer conversion |
| High-value textiles | Wider long-distance exchange setting | Fiber, yarn, woven cloth, finish, garment, origin claim | Silk appears in this edition only in Schoff's commentary and in the appended Chinese annal passages about long-distance trade; Isidore's itinerary attaches no silk sample or term to Ecbatana |
| Multiple audiences | Greek itinerary and mixed-script monetary evidence | Interpreter's rendering, trader's usage, fiscal spelling, court title | Their coexistence does not establish every resident's multilingual competence |
| Sacred and civic space | Anaitis sanctuary beside treasury and road functions | Donor, offering, dedication, sanctuary personnel, civic authority | §6 keeps the Bazigraban custom house and the Ecbatana treasury/sanctuary as separate stations; Polybius 10.27.12 attests the sanctuary's own stored wealth (gilded pillars, silver tiles and bricks), and no source links the two |

- **Borrowing workflow:** choose commodity or institution → identify brokers → choose recipient social group → adapt sound and morphology → date first use → permit specialist and everyday terms to diverge.
- Do not invent a Chinese→Parthian→Greek etymology merely because goods traversed several regions; trade transmission and word transmission need independent histories.
- A useful fictional distinction is prestigious textile provenance versus fiscal material classification; neither supplies a historical Ecbatana vocabulary without a lexical source.

## Mythic and symbolic core: regional cults rather than a recovered state pantheon

- Direct religious anchors in Isidore are **Anaitis at Ecbatana** (§6), an **Artemis sanctuary at Concobar** (§6), and a guarded **perpetual fire at Asaac**, where Arsaces was proclaimed king (§11).
- Anaitis is the Greek-form divine name conventionally associated with Iranian Anāhitā; a Greek writer's Artemis label does not prove an identical Greek cult in every named sanctuary.
- Ahura Mazdā, Mithra, and Anāhitā are relevant Iranian religious comparanda, not a securely reconstructed, uniformly ranked “Parthian trinity”; their roles at any selected Arsacid court must be argued from localized evidence rather than assumed from the triad. [Curtis, “Parthian Coins: Kingship and Divine Glory,” in Wick–Zehnder (2012)]
- A fire linked to royal proclamation can couple sacred place and dynastic memory; the passage alone does not reconstruct the ceremony, priesthood, or doctrine.
- Royal founder-names, diadems, bows, and sovereignty epithets provide firmer design anchors than an invented complete Arsacid creation myth.
- Interpret a symbol through **issuer + medium + audience + neighboring signs**: an archer on money, a fire at a royal place, and a deity in a sanctuary do different work.
- Do not identify every star, crescent, eagle, or mounted figure with the same god or royal-glory doctrine without a dated, localized argument; identifications in this field are actively revised. [Curtis (2012)]
- **Rejected shortcut:** Iranian worship of Mithra is not sufficient evidence for the Roman mysteries of Mithras, or for blanket claims that Christianity copied “Parthian rituals.” Hopkins's religion overview asserts such equivalences without adequate support; do not use it as authority for them.
- Avoid “all Parthians were orthodox Zoroastrians” and the reverse claim that every Greek divine name proves religious replacement. Court, city, sanctuary, and household are separate scales.
- **Design move:** let one ruler's title, a city's divine patron, and a household's ancestry coexist without making them one systematic pantheon.

## Costume and material signature: identify the wearer before naming the fabric

- Coin type 71.1 directly supplies a royal **diadem, earring, torque, beard, and arranged hair**; use those together as a portrait code, not a mandatory ethnic uniform.
- The bronze figure from **Shami in Elymais** is a major reference for elite Iranian-style dress: a belted upper garment, voluminous leg coverings, and footwear are described in the online art excerpt. [Iran Chamber Society, “The Art of Parthians,” excerpt pp. 188–189]
- The excerpt reproduces Edith Porada's chapter “The Art of the Parthians” in *The Art of Ancient Iran: Pre-Islamic Cultures* (1965; Arts of the World series); its Shami dating follows H. Seyrig (*Syria* XX, 1939) and must not be adopted as settled. Use it as a checkable secondary object description, not an unqualified chronology authority.
- Elymais, Ecbatana, and northeastern Parthia are not the same locality; keep a Shami reference's findspot attached to the design record.

| Material or visual cue | Permissible craft use | Overclaim to prevent |
|---|---|---|
| Trousered, belted silhouette | Couple movement, tailoring, and elite representation | Every trouser-wearer is ethnically Parthian or necessarily a horseman |
| Diadem and neck ornament | Design portable signs of authority and affiliation | One ornament has the same rank meaning for every century and region |
| Armor and horse protection in Plutarch | Separate expensive martial display from lighter equipment | Literary metallic brilliance identifies an excavated alloy or exact armor construction |
| Deep folds and surface ornament | Build silhouette and decorative vocabulary before material assignment | Sculpture proves silk fiber, weave structure, dye, or tailoring pattern |
| Elite representation | Establish a court or donor's preferred public image | It supplies ordinary women's, farmers', or children's dress |

- Do not infer silk from luxurious drapery. Record `depicted form / proposed material / material evidence / alternative explanation` separately.
- The name *bashlyk* in modern coin descriptions is a comparative costume label; do not present it as a securely attested Parthian garment word.
- Plutarch's “Median” and “Scythian” contrasts at 24.2 are outsider classifications, not self-evident native fashion departments.

## Naming: distinguish founder-name, personal name, spelling, and title

- Hopkins's attested-name tables explicitly distinguish written forms from reconstructed/read-off Iranian pronunciations and cite Alram (1986).
- Greek **Arsakēs**, Latinized **Arsaces**, and written Iranian-name forms belong in linked records; the Greek genitive **Arsakou** on a coin is a grammatical form, not another person.
- Names such as Mithradates, Phraates, and Vologases occur through different documentary languages; do not generate a “Parthian sound” by adding Greek endings randomly.
- Naming template: `individual / house or dynasty / founder-name claim / title / language-specific rendering / attestation date and medium`.
- A founder's name can become a sovereignty label shared by rivals; identical public dynastic names need not imply personal anonymity in every other setting.
- **Design move:** let the mint preserve a dynastic name, retainers use a personal name plus patronymic, and foreigners use an adapted form; specify the institutional reason for each.
- A historical name's etymology or divine element requires a philological entry — Alram (1986) is the coin-name authority used here — not a guessed meaning from its modern spelling.

## Gōsān: calibrate the evidence before designing a bardic institution

- **Verified scholarly anchor:** Mary Boyce's 1957 article, *The Parthian Gōsān and Iranian Minstrel Tradition*, supplies the classic reconstruction; the article metadata and opening abstract were checked, not the full argument.
- Its opening example is a **gōsān-i navāgar** singing at a feast in *Vīs u Rāmīn*: tree, spring, and bull encode a dangerous allegory about royal marriage and adultery.
- This is a strong craft example of **performance setting → patronage risk → symbolic indirection → audience interpretation**, not an Arsacid lyric preserved verbatim.
- The surviving New Persian poem belongs to the medieval transmission layer. Boyce argues for Parthian origins; a reconstructed source stratum is not an independently dated Arsacid manuscript.

| Proposition | Evidence layer | Working status |
|---|---|---|
| Boyce reconstructs a Parthian minstrel tradition | Identifiable 1957 scholarly study | Secure attribution of the argument; not automatic proof of every reconstruction |
| A feast-song can threaten a ruler through allegory | Medieval narrative discussed in the checked abstract | Attested literary scenario, not a documented Arsacid court incident |
| The term has Parthian and Armenian transmission relevance | Boyce's later “Gōsān” entry, now consulted | Parthian witness: Manichaean fragment `gwsʾn` (text dated 4th–5th century CE); loanwords: Armenian `gōsān`, Mandaean `gwsānā`, probably Georgian `mgōsāni`; earliest literary occurrence: 11th-century *Vis o Rāmin*, from a Middle Persian version of a Parthian oral work |
| Every noble house employed a hereditary genealogical bard | Boyce presents it as probable inference (“Probably, however, each noble family would have had its own hereditary gōsāns”), not attestation | Do not standardize a guild or caste from this entry |
| A particular epic, melody, instrument, or costume is Arsacid | Only five texts are identified as of gōsān origin, all transmitted in Middle Persian or classical Persian; no contemporary performance detail accompanies them | A bardic hypothesis does not supply performance detail |

- Distinguish **language of a term**, **date of its surviving witness**, **date assigned to a story**, and **date of the institution inferred from it**.
- Do not call every ancient Iranian musician a gōsān, or back-project later Armenian singer-poet forms wholesale into Arsacid society.
- Plutarch's *Crassus* 33 reports Greek dramatic performance at the Armenian–Parthian royal encounter; it does not identify those actors as gōsāns or establish an indigenous epic repertoire.
- **Design move:** specify repertoire ownership, payment, permissible mockery, commemorative duty, and audience competence before inventing a bard's formal title.
- **Design test:** who recognizes the allegory, who can punish its singer, and why would a patron preserve a song that embarrasses the dynasty?
- This entry's knowledge of Arsacid performance practice is deliberately thin: meter, training system, instrumental ensemble, and hereditary status remain unestablished here.

## What a worldbuilder can take

- **A layered public voice:** founder-name, Greek prestige formula, Iranian royal identification, and portrait ornaments need not change together.
- **A logistical warrior prestige system:** connect skilled withdrawal and martial honor to horses, arrows, transport labor, patrons, and ceremonial rights—not an innate ethnic talent.
- **A road-city vocabulary:** let sanctuary, treasury, customs, and seasonal court audiences classify the same people and goods differently; lexical forms remain inventions unless documented.
- **A differentiated visual language:** separate riding silhouette, court portrait code, sacred image, and specialist equipment; tie each to wearer, place, period, and medium.
- **A transmission-aware bardic design:** stories can preserve older prestige while changing language, religious framing, patron, and performance context; record those stages instead of declaring an unbroken ancient songbook.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: supply options, constraints, source IDs, dated evidence, and unresolved questions. Only the sibling's canon process selects fictional names, events, institutions, or beliefs.

## Provenance cautions

- Greek and Roman testimony is often adversarial or moralizing; it also preserves real observations. Evaluate each claim rather than accepting or rejecting an entire author wholesale.
- Schoff's 1914 commentary includes modern reconstructions and nineteenth-century travel comparisons; do not misattribute them to Isidore or use them to prove a centralized silk monopoly.
- Older art histories privilege Hellenistic naturalism and call other forms “crude,” “decadent,” or “barbaric”; these judgments are not neutral archaeological measurements.
- Formal resemblance across Hatra, Palmyra, Dura-Europos, and Iran does not establish one ethnic identity, one ruler's patronage, or the same political control at the date of each object.
- Thin internal chronicle and performance evidence cannot be repaired with later epics, nationalist continuity stories, universal archetypes, or invented bibliographic precision.
- Before historical reuse, resolve each relevant `(to-verify)` item against an edition or identified object. Before fictional reuse, label the transformation as design and keep the evidence boundary visible.
