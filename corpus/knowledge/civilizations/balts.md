## Scope: related traditions, not one timeless civilization
- “Baltic” here is principally linguistic-cultural, not a synonym for every people bordering the Baltic Sea.
- Lithuanian and Latvian are living East Baltic languages; Old Prussian is an extinct West Baltic language. Baltic Prussians are not interchangeable with later German-speaking Prussia. [Dini 2014]
- Estonian and Livonian are Finnic, not Baltic languages; their contact with Baltic speakers is integral to regional history, not evidence that they share a language family. [Dini 2014; Vanags 2024]
- Curonian, Semigallian, Selonian, and Yotvingian material requires its own source dossier; modern national borders do not delimit medieval speech or cult communities.
- Working method: **source stratigraphy + feature bundles**. Couple features witnessed together in a locality and period; explain imports rather than manufacturing a pan-Baltic average.

| Evidence layer | What it can establish | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Historical linguistics | Cognacy, relative sound changes, inherited versus borrowed vocabulary | An unchanged prehistoric religion or ethnic “purity” |
| Excavated objects and contexts | Dated materials, manufacture, deposition, exchange | A named god or complete everyday costume from a single grave |
| Medieval/early modern writing | What a particular author reports, translates, or condemns | An unmediated insider catechism |
| Collected folklore | A recorded variant, its language and attributed performance context | The prehistoric date of every motif |
| Modern religious/heritage practice | A living community’s interpretations and ceremonies | Unbroken institutional continuity from antiquity |

## Language family, retention, and writing
- **Archaic Indo-European retention is feature-specific.** Lithuanian preserves valuable inherited inflectional and accentual evidence; it is a modern language with innovations, not Proto-Indo-European spoken unchanged. [Dini 2014; Derksen 2015]
- Baltic–Slavic shared innovations matter to comparison; an attractive Sanskrit resemblance is not a demonstration of recent descent, migration, or identical ritual.

| Design axis | Lithuanian reference | Latvian reference | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Accent | Mobile stress and pitch-accent distinctions | Normally initial stress; syllable intonations and dialect variation | Build separate prosodic histories; do not give both the same verse engine |
| Morphology | Productive case inflection, agreement, participles | Case inflection, agreement, participles; its own innovations | Names, invocations, and titles need paradigms, not decorative endings |
| Sound history | Inherited quantities and later changes | Quantity, palatal consonants, contact effects | Generate related names through regular correspondences, not random respelling |
| Contact | Slavic and Germanic interaction in different periods | Germanic, Finnic, Slavic, later international vocabulary | Loanword strata can date institutions and imported clothing |
| Written tradition | Mažvydas’s printed catechism, 1547, is a landmark | Surviving printed catechisms, 1585/1586; earlier textual witnesses exist | Christian print records vernacular language without preserving a pagan scripture |

- Sources for the table: Dini 2014, especially chapters 2–4 and 6–8; Derksen 2015; Vanags 2024.
- Old Prussian survives in a restricted written corpus including vocabularies and sixteenth-century catechisms; reconstructed competence must not be equated with a complete native literary archive.
- Present Lithuanian and Latvian use Latin-based orthographies with different diacritics; their modern conventions must not be projected unchanged onto medieval documents.
- Latvian modern orthography was adopted in stages after the 1908 reform decision; older German-influenced writing is a different graphic layer. [Vanags 2024]
- **Application:** specify oral vernacular, ritual register, chancery language, and print standard separately; one population may use different languages for each.

## Mythic core: a network with uneven attestation
- Start with attested names and narrative relations, not a definitive Olympian-style family tree. The *Lexicon* is a research map; Vėlius supplies historical witnesses whose agendas still need criticism.

| Name/reference cluster | Usable reference knowledge | Calibration boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Lithuanian *Dievas*, Latvian *Dievs* | Inherited divine vocabulary; later also the Christian God | Cognacy does not fix a single original personality or genealogy |
| *Perkūnas / Pērkons* | Thunder-associated deity; useful conjunction of weather, sound, and authority | Distinguish historical testimony, song variants, and comparative reconstruction |
| *Saulė / Saule* | Female sun figures prominent in Baltic song and mythic imagery | Solar weddings and celestial kinship vary; do not harmonize all songs into one plot |
| *Laima* | Fate, birth, and life-course associations, especially visible in song | Do not confuse similarly named beings or turn every fate motif into one doctrine |
| Lithuanian *Žemyna*; Latvian earth/mother figures | Earth, locality, household and agrarian concerns | These are a comparison, not a claim that every figure is the same goddess |
| *Velnias / Velns* | Later folklore and Christian usage include devil figures | A Christian-era name or tale is not a transparent prehistoric theology |

- Table basis: Běťáková and Blažek 2021; Vėlius 1996–2005; Biezais 1987/2005. Confidence concerns particular attestations, not an entire row equally in every century.
- Grass-snake imagery and household belief offer a further reference cluster; identify the actual narrative, species term, place, and witness before assigning it an ancestral cult.
- “Milda, ancient goddess of love” is unsafe as straightforward ancient testimony: Young’s review highlights nineteenth-century invention associated with Narbutt, also discussed by the *Lexicon*.
- Gimbutas’s large prehistoric goddess reconstructions are contested, not a secure decoder for every Baltic ornament; neither dismiss all her archaeology nor present speculative religious continuity as settled fact. [Young 2023]
- **Application:** build domains through relationships—storm/field, hearth/household, birth/fate—then let regions disagree; do not transplant these real divine names as automatically approved fictional canon.

## Romowe, Romuva, and solstice rites: evidence calibration
- **Medieval Romowe:** Peter of Dusburg’s *Cronica terre Prussie* (1326), III.5, describes a Prussian sanctuary and the priest Criwe; consult the Latin passage with translation reproduced in Mažeika 2023.
- His comparison with Rome and a pope is a Christian author’s framing, not proof of a centralized pan-Baltic church.
- Mažeika argues against both literal acceptance and wholesale dismissal as a fabricated moral exemplum; her interpretation remains a scholarly argument, not recovered insider liturgy.
- **Modern Romuva:** a Lithuanian religious revival/reconstruction using folklore, historical interpretation, song, and contemporary community practice; its self-understood continuity is evidence about identity, not independent proof of every ancient rite. [Dundzila and Strmiska 2005]
- Romuva and Latvian Dievturība are related modern Baltic religious movements, not interchangeable names for an undifferentiated medieval religion.
- **Do not conflate three clocks:** the astronomical solstice, the St John calendar feast, and the date chosen by a modern organizer.

| Reference practice | Attestation to use | Safe reading / unsafe extrapolation |
|---|---|---|
| Latvian Jāņi/Līgo season | Rancāne 2026: historical accounts, song, customary practice | Singing, greenery, wreaths, fires, food, and vigil form a seasonal complex; not all features share one origin |
| Lithuanian Joninės; Rasos/Kupolinės terminology | Vaicekauskas 2014; modern Romuva account | Track name, region, and source date; do not assume every name labels an identical ancient festival |
| Lithuania Minor’s changing Midsummer | Blockytė-Naujokė 2023 | Patriotic programming and later public staging transform the rite; modern visual details need their own dating |
| Present-day sacred-fire ceremony | Dundzila and Strmiska 2005 | Direct evidence for practitioners described there; not a complete medieval reconstruction |

- Rancāne identifies Rüssow’s 1578 chronicle and Einhorn’s 1649 account among early descriptions; both require attention to clerical perspective and the populations actually described.
- The precise pre-Christian sequence of officiants, prayers, and garments for a particular solstice site is **(to-verify)**; the cited evidence does not license filling gaps from modern festival photographs.
- **Application:** couple agricultural deadlines, hospitality obligations, song exchanges, wreath preparation, and night/day transitions; choose which institution later reinterprets them.
- A festival can temporarily suspend ordinary clothing distinctions: Rancāne notes Jāņi wreaths for women regardless of status. Do not read every festival wreath as an everyday unmarried-status marker.

## Symbolic system: use-context before universal meaning
| Carrier | Productive comparison | Evidence guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Sun, wheel, rosette | Daylight cycle, seasonal return, movement in song or rite | Geometric resemblance alone does not identify a solar cult |
| Tree, branch, grove | Local sacred landscape, household greenery, boundary-making | A modern “world tree” diagram is not proof of one ancient cosmogram |
| Wreath | Seasonal vegetation, affiliation, courtship, life-stage display | Meanings change with occasion, gender, marital status, and region |
| Sash, border, repeated textile motif | Bodily boundary, technical pattern, gift, regional belonging | A motif name is not a demonstrated alphabet or universal deity cipher |
| Snake | Household/nature narratives and changing religious evaluations | Species, tale genre, and source period precede any assigned theological meaning |

- Reference base: Vėlius; *Lexicon*; Rancāne; Jurkuvienė; Jansone. Rows are comparison prompts, not a dictionary claiming that each shape always has the indicated meaning.
- Record **form → carrier → making technique → wearer/user → occasion → attributed meaning → witness/date**. Keep a museum label, informant explanation, and later artist’s interpretation distinct.
- Crosses and hooked-cross forms can acquire radically different meanings in later political settings; check the exact object and its reception before reuse, rather than asserting an eternally innocent or eternally identical sign.
- **Application:** let a woven pattern have a workshop name before it has a priestly explanation; competing interpretations can be designed, but must be labeled invention.

## Era model: synchronize politics, language, and costume separately
| Stage | Language / religious evidence | Dress and material decision | Failure to avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Before extensive vernacular writing | Archaeology, names, and comparative reconstruction, uneven by region | Start from a dated site assemblage and technical analysis | Dressing every pre-Christian person in a later national costume |
| Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries | Crusading and conquest in Prussia/Livonia; Lithuanian state-building and conversion | Distinguish elite, town, rural, and burial contexts | One conversion date changing every household overnight |
| Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries | Catechisms, dictionaries, clerical reports, changing confessional vocabulary | Require contemporary images, descriptions, or excavated fragments | Treating a missionary’s polemic as a neutral pantheon list |
| Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Folklore collection, national movements, vernacular print | Surviving regional festive dress; cotton, factory goods, and new dyes enter | Calling all colourful festival clothing prehistoric |
| Twentieth–twenty-first centuries | State institutions, Soviet restrictions/reframing, diaspora, revival | Museum reconstruction, stage ensemble, household inheritance, new ritual dress | Equating revival with either fraud or unchanged survival |

- Historical scaffolding: Dini 2014; material chronology: Jurkuvienė 2006 and Jansone 2025; festival transformation: Blockytė-Naujokė 2023.
- Lithuania’s 1387 conversion is a political/religious landmark, not a date for the disappearance of every local custom; Samogitian conversion belongs to a later phase. [Dini 2014]
- **Application:** assign separate dates to a loanword, a garment cut, a cult prohibition, and a song’s recording; permit lag, survival, borrowing, and revival only through an explicit causal chain.

## Costume and material signature: linen/wool, not uniform whiteness
- Most directly usable costume detail here concerns nineteenth-century rural dress, especially festive ensembles; it is not a verified catalogue of prehistoric Baltic clothing.
- Lithuanian costume evidence includes surviving garments, descriptions, images, and memories; male ensembles often rely more heavily on descriptions and images because fewer garments were collected. [Jurkuvienė 2006]
- Jansone explicitly warns that complete nineteenth-century Latvian outfits and exact change dates can remain uncertain because the surviving evidence is fragmentary.

| Element | Lithuanian / Latvian reference | Coupled social or technical system |
|---|---|---|
| Linen shirt | *marškiniai / krekls*; household-spun/woven cloth, with later purchased alternatives | Flax work, laundering, bleaching, sleeve/border terminology |
| Wool outer layers | Lithuanian *sermėga* of fulled cloth; Latvian wool wraps including *villaine* | Climate, cloth finishing, tailoring, seasonal wear |
| Skirt and apron | Lithuanian *sijonas*, *prijuostė*; Latvian *brunči*, with local ensemble differences | Occasion, modesty conventions, household resources, regional fashion |
| Woven belt/sash | Lithuanian *juosta*, Latvian *josta* | Loom technique, gift exchange, waist fastening, patterned identity |
| Headwear | Lithuanian *nuometas*, caps, kerchiefs, festive crowns/wreaths | Marriage/life stage and local custom; distinguish ordinary and festival use |
| Fastening and adornment | Latvian *sakta* brooch; local jewellery and purchased trims | Metalworking, exchange, wealth, inherited versus newly bought pieces |

- Table basis: Jurkuvienė 2006; Jansone 2025. Similar translated garment labels do not guarantee identical cuts or a shared date.
- Bleached fine linen can signal work and expenditure; undyed wool, bright dyed wool, mixed cloth, and purchased silk/cotton also belong to the evidence. “All Balts wore white” is false for these documented ensembles.
- Lithuanian festive linen skirts could rank below wool skirts; material choice is not reducible to “linen = sacred, wool = ordinary.” [Jurkuvienė 2006]
- Married women’s covered hair and unmarried women’s festive wreath/crown distinctions are documented in Lithuanian nineteenth-century dress, but exact headpieces and rules are local, not universal across Baltic history.
- Colour changes can date market access: Jurkuvienė describes synthetic dyes replacing earlier dyes in the later nineteenth century; do not give identical palettes to every era.
- Lithuanian regional references include Aukštaitija, Žemaitija, Dzūkija, Suvalkija, and the Klaipėda area; Latvian ensembles likewise require locality within historical regions.
- **Application:** specify fibre, yarn, weave, finish, cut, fastening, wear occasion, repair, and acquisition route before assigning symbolic rank. Regional variation should affect both vocabulary and silhouette.

## Daina tradition as archive: witnesses, not a frozen sacred book
- Latvian *daina/dainas* and Lithuanian *daina/dainos* are related terms; do not silently transfer every Latvian genre feature to Lithuanian song.
- Latvian dainas are often short, including four-line forms, with trochaic and dactylic metres; work, seasonal observance, weddings, birth, death, satire, and erotic material coexist. [Biezais 1987/2005]
- Biezais’s strong “ancient survival” interpretations and some etymological proposals are not automatically established by his sound descriptions of repertoire; check them against modern linguistics and source criticism.
- The Cabinet of Folksongs preserves handwritten slips, variants, and Barons’s annotations behind *Latvju dainas*, published 1894–1915; collecting and classification were also part of national awakening. [UNESCO, Cabinet of Folksongs]
- “Archive” means inspectable witnesses and editorial decisions, not a single unaltered communal voice. Recording date, claimed transmission history, and hypothesized motif age are different fields.
- Lithuanian *sutartinės* offer a specifically northeastern Lithuanian multipart tradition: interacting voices, refrains including nonce syllables, and several performance arrangements. They are not a synonym for all Lithuanian songs. [UNESCO 2010]
- UNESCO describes women’s vocal practice and men’s instrumental versions; this is not evidence that every Baltic musical or ritual office was reserved to one gender.

| Song-witness record | Why the worldbuilding method needs it |
|---|---|
| Archive/edition ID; variant; collector; date | Distinguish a text from its editorial assembly |
| Performer; locality; dialect; social position | Avoid making one singer represent a whole civilization |
| Occasion; addressee; action performed | Connect language to actual work, rite, or social negotiation |
| Text; translation; metre; refrain | Preserve grammatical and performative constraints |
| Tune/voice arrangement; recording availability | A printed text alone does not document the original sound |
| Objects/garments/plants named; ambiguity | Couple material references without pretending every noun is a cult symbol |

- **Application:** build a fictional witness ledger before any lyric corpus: repeated formulas may conserve older grammar, but the particular linguistic retention must be demonstrated, not assumed.
- Couple work songs to a validated production chain; couple wedding verses to address terms, gifts, and dress transitions. An apparent botanical “symbol” might also be a real seasonal task or resource.
- Do not invent Baltic quotations, supply fake archive numbers, or present translated modern lyrics as anonymous ancient evidence.

## Naming practices: morphology, documents, and historical layers
- Lithuanian reference material includes inherited compound personal names, shorter forms, Christian names, nicknames, and later hereditary surnames; these are different naming resources, not a single generator. [Dini 2014; Zinkevičius 2010]
- Preserve a name’s inflectional behaviour when designing address, patronymics, or possession; a nominative ending is not a detachable cultural decoration.
- Zinkevičius documents chancery-mediated changes in spelling, sounds, and suffixes: Lithuanian *Vytautas* and Polish *Witold* illustrate related naming forms across documentary traditions.
- Native patronymic formations and Slavic-derived suffixes can coexist; a surname’s suffix alone does not establish the bearer’s ethnicity or first language.
- Canonical baptismal names, vernacular familiar forms, and recorded patronymics can differ for the same person; the register reflects the scribe’s conventions as well as the named household.
- Do not automatically treat every current nature-name or literary heroine’s name as pre-Christian; verify its earliest attestation independently of the antiquity of its component word.
- Latvian personal names must follow Latvian, not Lithuanian, sound and inflection rules; do not flatten *Jānis* and *Jonas* into alternate spellings in a supposedly single language.
- **Application:** generate a spoken form, familiar form, ritual/address form, and document form; define who can change each and when names become hereditary. These are design options, not proposed canon.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **Retention without stasis:** preserve selected old inflectional or poetic features while everyday speech, religious vocabulary, and imported garment terms change through regular histories.
- **Song as distributed memory:** household work, birth, weddings, and seasonal hospitality support overlapping repertoires rather than one authoritative myth book.
- **Calendar–costume coupling:** a wreath or headcloth has meaning through wearer, occasion, and action; seasonal exceptions can expose the rules of ordinary life.
- **Material literacy:** linen/wool processing, finishing, and trade explain both technical vocabulary and the resources required for festive appearance.
- **Layered identity:** community practice can be sincerely maintained, reconstructed, reclassified, and contested without being either timeless or meaningless.
- Handoff to lore-shokunin: submit the chosen locality/era analogue, evidence layer per feature, transformation rules, material constraints, alternatives, and unresolved items; request canon decisions rather than asserting them.

## Provenance cautions and open research
- Checked on 2026-10-17 against linked publisher/catalogue metadata, institutional descriptions, abstracts, and the accessible texts identified above; not every cited book or historical document was collated in full.
- Medieval chroniclers and early modern clergy often wrote within conquest, mission, or confessional polemic. Bias is not a reason to discard every observation; report the author’s purpose and distinguish observation, hearsay, analogy, and retrospective legend.
- Folklore collectors, national institutions, Soviet-era arrangements, and modern revivalists also select and reshape material. Contemporary practitioners remain authorities on their own practice, not automatic witnesses to the medieval past.
- Young’s scepticism and the *Lexicon*’s comparative approach mark a real interpretive dispute. Keep lexical inheritance, narrative comparison, and historical proof as separate propositions.
- Coverage is thinner here for Old Prussian insider ritual, smaller extinct Baltic languages, local Latvian dress reconstruction, and pre-sixteenth-century festival sequences; seek specialist studies and dated objects before narrowing claims.
- Exact medieval Romowe organization, site-specific solstice liturgy, deity-to-textile-sign assignments, and priestly dress codes remain **(to-verify)**; this entry supplies no invented reconstruction of them.
- Never infer a people’s biological ancestry, moral character, or modern political entitlement from linguistic conservatism, a motif, or a garment. This is a method/reference entry and establishes no fictional canon.
