## 1. Scope and calibration

- **Living, plural traditions:** Māori is not a single ancient kingdom, costume, religion, or uniform protocol. Identify iwi, hapū, whānau, locality, occasion, and period before attaching a claim.
- **Public synthesis / local checking / contested** are the calibration levels below. Public synthesis supports comparison; it does not grant authority to perform, reproduce, or interpret someone else's taonga.
- **Thin coverage here:** local karanga and whaikōrero rights, individual moko narratives, specialist karakia, and named pounamu histories. These require named knowledge holders and provenance; this file does not reconstruct them.
- “Relational archive,” “performance register,” and the design matrices are analytic tools used here, not claims that Māori classify their institutions with these English labels.
- Do not recast Māori as extinct, purely rural, uniformly pre-Christian, or culturally unchanged before Europeans. Contemporary urban, diasporic, Christian, secular, and revitalisation contexts belong in the reference frame.

## 2. Language family, sound, and writing

| Layer | Reference base | Coupling consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy of language | Austronesian → Oceanic → Polynesian → Eastern Polynesian; close relationships include Cook Islands Māori and Tahitian (Harlow 2007) | Related languages can share inherited structures without sharing every deity, garment, or protocol |
| Sound | Five vowel qualities with contrastive length; consonant spellings include `ng` and `wh`; vowel-final syllables, not arbitrary consonant clusters (Harlow 2007, chapters 3–4) | A naming generator needs phonotactics and length, not an ornamental macron filter |
| Grammar | Predicate-initial syntax; inclusive/exclusive and singular/dual/plural pronoun distinctions (Harlow 2007, chapters 5–6) | Dialogue can distinguish “we including you” from “we excluding you”; do not infer a collectivist personality from grammar |
| Dialect | Regional differences are real; Kāi Tahu/Ngāi Tahu is a useful southern/standard comparison, not a universal spelling-conversion algorithm | Place, ancestry, and institutional orthography may point to different histories |
| Writing history | Latin-alphabet literacy developed through early nineteenth-century Māori–missionary collaboration and was actively used by Māori authors | Manuscripts and newspapers extend oral knowledge; literacy is not equivalent to colonisers owning the archive |
| Modern spelling | Tohutō/macrons mark vowel length; some communities/institutions use doubled vowels | Record a community's chosen form; do not silently “correct” identity-bearing names |

- Distinguish **linguistic register**, **dialect**, and **language shift**: ceremonial skill is not a separate language, and English dominance is not an inherent “low” variety of te reo.
- Harlow's demographic figures and migration estimates are historical to his 2007 publication, not current statistics or the final archaeological chronology.
- Carving, body marking, landscape, and woven pattern can support memory and recognition without being an alphabet or a fully decipherable writing system. Never invent a pre-contact Māori script to fill that category.

## 3. Te reo registers and tangata whenua protocols

**Frame:** ask *who speaks, where, to whom, under whose mandate, and with which consequences?* Harlow (2007), chapter 1, and McRae (2017), introduction, establish the genres; Mead (2003) is a reference for further tikanga work, not a universal rulebook.

| Genre/domain | Language and performance | Authority/context to retain | Fictional-system abstraction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Everyday kōrero | Conversation, humour, contemporary vocabulary, code-switching | Fluency, age, region, and biography vary | Give people ordinary lives, not continuous ceremonial speeches |
| Karanga | Formal call and response in encounters such as pōwhiri | Commonly a women's responsibility; local training, roles, and circumstances matter | Entry to a place can require an authorised relational exchange |
| Whaikōrero | Formal oratory: remembrance, shared histories, genealogy, allusion, current business | Speaking order and eligibility depend on local tikanga; do not universalise a gender rule | Eloquence needs both linguistic skill and recognised standing |
| Waiata / mōteatea | Song and chant, with inherited imagery and sometimes compressed or archaic diction | A supporting song relates to speaker, people, and occasion | A speech can need a second medium and collective support to complete its force |
| Karakia | Ritual utterance; present practice also includes Christian forms | Purpose, whakapapa, practitioner, and religious setting must be identified | Do not equate every karakia with either a fantasy spell or a church prayer |
| Haka | Forceful delivery of words through voice, rhythm, body, and collective action | Composition, message, performers, audience, and occasion are inseparable | Performance is an argument or relationship enacted, not generic aggression |

- **Tangata whenua** concerns belonging and authority in relation to land; **mana whenua** concerns authority associated with a particular place. Neither is simply an “ethnic VIP” label.
- A Māori visitor may be manuhiri at another community's marae. Urban or institutional marae require their own mandate/context account; do not mechanically map all of them onto one rural descent group.
- For reference scenes, research the hosts' tikanga first: approach, invitation, calls, seating, speaking sequence, waiata support, koha, greeting, and shared food are context-bearing questions, not a universally executable checklist.
- Do not make tangihanga, an ordinary welcome, and an emergency gathering identical. The dead, visiting relationships, and the purpose of the hui change what utterances are doing.
- McRae's sequence **whakapapa → whakataukī → kōrero → waiata** is a useful analytical parallel for some oratory; she explicitly allows variation in order and content. It is not a mandatory speech template.
- Craft transfer: distinguish *knowing the words*, *being able to deliver them*, and *having standing to use them*. An outsider can possess the first two and lack the third.

## 4. Whakapapa as archive, classification, and obligation

**Public synthesis:** genealogy connects persons and groups with ancestors, places, and wider origins; the archive is enacted and interpreted, not merely stored. McRae (2017) is especially useful for the interdependence of whakapapa, sayings, narratives, and songs; Roberts et al. (2004) is further reading on its conceptual scope.

| Archive function | Coupled medium | Useful design question |
|---|---|---|
| Identify relationships | Names, descent lines, recalled marriages and affiliations | Which connection becomes relevant in this encounter? |
| Locate histories | Named landscape, ancestors, narrative episodes | Does a place-name retrieve a story, and who can explain it? |
| Support standing | Recitation, acknowledged descent, local histories | Who accepts a claim, and what other obligations accompany it? |
| Make allusions intelligible | Whakataukī, waiata, oratory | Which names must listeners already know for a short phrase to work? |
| Extend memory across media | Oral teaching, manuscript books, carvings, recordings | What changes when a performance becomes a written or searchable record? |
| Connect humans and wider existence | Cosmological genealogies and relational classification | Is the relationship also ethical or ritual, rather than a biological pedigree? |

- Whānau, hapū, and iwi are related forms of collective belonging, not three fixed census-sized administrative boxes. Affiliation can be multiple; do not force every person into a single exclusive branch.
- Do not use a genealogical count as an automatic calendar: selection, compression, variant lines, and the purpose of a recital need investigation. A fixed “years per generation” conversion is not verification.
- A manuscript's existence does not make its contents publicly reusable. Distinguish custodian, scribe, narrator, descendants, editor, translator, and current access conditions.
- **Not a credulity/falsehood switch:** differing lines may reflect perspective, transmission, or dispute. Establish the particular claim before interpreting variation as either fraud or meaningless “myth.”
- **Design tool, not ethnographic law:** represent an archive item as `{knowledge holder, relationships, place, medium, occasion, access conditions, variant, obligations}`; retain conflicting attestations rather than overwriting them.
- McRae notes that oral compositions may be concise but densely contextual: a name-list can act as the index to a much larger archive. Write supporting reference notes, not supposedly self-decoding indigenous keywords.

## 5. Mythic core: genealogy before a pantheon chart

The following are widespread public reference points, not a single authorised Māori cosmology. Royal (2005) is an orientation source; exact variants and genealogical edges require local verification (to-verify).

| Reference cluster | Common public account | What comparison must not erase |
|---|---|---|
| Te Kore, Te Pō, Te Ao Mārama | States/sequences associated with potentiality, darkness, and the world of light | Enumerations, meanings, and ordering vary; this is not a universal three-step creation diagram |
| Ranginui and Papatūānuku | Sky and earth, ancestral relationships, separation and emergence | Local names, spouses, children, and narrative emphases differ |
| Tāne; Tangaroa; Tāwhirimātea | Forest/birds; sea; winds/weather are familiar associations | Atua are not merely departmental “gods of X” in a game-stat table |
| Tūmatauenga; Rongo; Haumia-tiketike | Conflict/human activity; cultivated foods/peace; uncultivated foods in common summaries | Human practices and relationships matter more than fixed, exhaustive portfolios |
| Hine-nui-te-pō; Mahuika | Death and ancestral relations; fire and its transmission | Do not erase female agency by retaining only male atua |
| Māui traditions | Culture-hero actions concerning fishing up land, the sun, fire, and mortality | Māori versions are not interchangeable with Hawaiian or other Polynesian Māui cycles |
| Local ancestral and place traditions | Waka, named ancestors, mountains, waters, migrations | A landscape is not an empty map onto which “universal myths” are pasted |

- Avoid a compulsory “chief god”: genealogical connection and local emphasis do not require one Olympian-style hierarchy.
- **Contested:** the antiquity, transmission, and local scope of Io traditions require specialist scrutiny. Neither universal primordial monotheism nor blanket missionary fabrication is a safe default; particular claims (to-verify).
- Mythic time and an archaeological date range answer different questions. Do not use a story to manufacture a dated reign, or archaeology to declare that a tradition has no present significance.
- For fictional design, connect origins to duties, environments, naming, and techniques; do not copy sacred names and then assign invented doctrine to Māori people.

## 6. Symbolic system: meaningful relations, not clip-art meanings

| Term/medium | Reference distinction | Calibration against misuse |
|---|---|---|
| Mana | Authority, standing, efficacy, prestige in context | Not a substance measurable as generic fantasy “magic points” |
| Tapu / noa | Relational conditions and restrictions; release or ordinary accessibility in appropriate contexts | Not an exhaustive sacred/evil versus secular/good binary |
| Taonga | Treasured entities with cultural and relational significance | Not restricted to jewellery, nor reducible to sale price |
| Kaitiakitanga | Responsibilities of care and authority in particular relationships and places | “Environmentalism” alone loses the mandate and kinship dimensions |
| Wharenui | A named meeting house may embody an ancestor and gather ancestral representations | Not every building element can be decoded without local knowledge |
| Koru, kōwhaiwhai, tukutuku | Recognisable forms and skilled practices whose meanings depend on use and transmission | Reject universal motif-to-personality or motif-to-rank dictionaries |

- Calibration: the table supplies working English glosses, not exhaustive translations; local applications of these concepts require checking (Mead 2003; McRae 2017; Ngāi Tahu n.d.).
- **Coupling test:** a symbol needs a maker, medium, setting, interpreting community, and history. “Spiral = renewal everywhere” is not sufficient evidence.
- A design may be publicly visible yet remain connected to particular people. Visibility, museum photography, and commercial reproduction do not settle rights or suitability for reuse.

## 7. Haka as a performance register

- Harlow (2007), chapter 1, explicitly lists welcome, political statement, entertainment, and defiance: **“war dance” is an inadequate general definition**.
- Haka is not synonymous with all kapa haka. Song, action song, poi, and other forms belong to a broader performing-arts repertoire; identify the actual genre before describing its delivery.
- **Performance analysis grid:** `{composer/attribution, words, occasion, addressee, collective voice, lead/response, rhythm, gesture, attire, place, audience response}`. This is a craft grid, not a prescribed Māori taxonomy.
- Performance coordinates message and body; do not reverse-engineer meaning solely from stamping, facial expression, or a televised sports encounter.
- Women participate in haka and Māori performing arts; do not make all haka an initiation rite for male warriors. Particular compositions and roles still need local checking.
- Common labels include *peruperu* and *haka taparahi*, but detailed movement/weapon classifications need Kāretu's text and a knowledgeable practitioner (to-verify); this file does not prescribe choreography.
- The transferable mechanism is **collective, embodied speech with an attributable message**. Compose an independent fictional form with its own language and history rather than paraphrasing a real haka.
- Modern composition, competition, schooling, protest, and sporting use are historical layers, not evidence that a tradition is either unchanged or inauthentic.

## 8. Moko as biography: situated identity, not a cipher

- **Public synthesis:** tā moko/tāmoko can express identity, role, affiliation, and a person's lived relationships. “Biography” is a useful analogy only if it includes bearer, practitioner, community, and interpretation.
- **Explicit uncertainty:** Te Papa states that genealogical and status meanings remain unclear in important respects. Do not turn a facial zone into a universally readable lineage, office, marriage count, or achievement score.
- The same source describes adaptation from Eastern Polynesian tattooing: toothed uhi deposit pigment; the development of narrower cutting tools enabled deeply grooved facial work. Do not apply one historical technique to every moko, body location, or era.
- Needles and contemporary equipment do not by themselves make present moko culturally lesser; tool history and cultural authority are separate axes.
- Record **body location, bearer, practitioner, date, tool, process, social occasion, and the account's narrator** before comparing images.
- Distinguish moko kauae, facial work, and other body work; detailed motif interpretation and individual entitlement are outside this entry's evidence (to-verify).
- Colonial hostility and Christianisation disrupted practice unevenly. Avoid a story of complete disappearance followed by invention: women sustained important continuities, and Te Papa credits Sāmoan practitioners with significant revitalisation support.
- The history of ancestral remains and colonial collecting is not a decorative “headhunter” motif. If relevant to reference work, use descendant-led and repatriation scholarship; no invented cases here.
- Fictional transfer: an inscription can be personal and publicly consequential without every stranger being able to read it. Invent new visual forms; do not copy a living person's moko as a character asset.

## 9. Pounamu: prestige through substance, history, and guardianship

- Pounamu is a Māori material category conventionally translated as greenstone/jade, not a universal synonym for every green rock. It includes nephrite and some valued non-nephrite material, notably tangiwai/bowenite; exact geological/type identifications (to-verify).
- Do not reduce prestige to colour or rarity. Extraction place, difficult working, relationships, gifting, named histories, ancestry, and present care can all matter; not every modern pendant has the same biography.
- Uses include tools such as toki, weapons such as mere, and ornaments such as hei-tiki. Function, form, and history must be established for the particular object, not inferred from “green stone.”
- A treasured object can move between people while retaining associations with prior holders. **Not freely substitutable** does not mean physically untransferable, never gifted, or never exchanged.
- Avoid the tourist rule that all pounamu must always be received as a gift; that is not an adequate universal historical or legal account.

| Dimension | Questions for a reference dossier | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| Source | Which deposit, route, and community relationship? | Geography can structure prestige and access |
| Transformation | Who works it, with which tools, labour, and learning? | An object stores craft relations as well as mineral |
| Social life | Is it named; what documented gifts, inheritances, or other transfers? | Value changes with history rather than mass alone |
| Custody | Who cares for it, and on whose behalf? | Possession and unrestricted authority need not coincide |
| Authentication | What evidence connects today's object with its claimed origin? | A mark can certify provenance without disclosing all cultural knowledge |

- **Contemporary legal layer, not timeless custom:** Ngāi Tahu's current guidance says the 1997 Pounamu Vesting Act vested natural-state pounamu within its takiwā and adjacent territorial sea in Te Rūnanga o Ngāi Tahu; the Arahura catchment is the stated exception under Māwhera ownership/management.
- The same guidance recognises Papatipu Rūnanga as kaitiaki under iwi-wide and regional plans; its authentication system uses a unique code to identify stone origin and whakapapa. Do not project the digital system backwards.
- This is not collection advice or a legal ruling. Any real sourcing, collection, export, or reproduction proposal needs the current responsible authority; do not turn this summary into permission.

## 10. Era model and costume/material staging

**Staging aid, not a Māori master chronology:** use intersecting changes in language domain, local politics, technology, religion, and dress. The rows overlap; material use does not change everywhere on one date.

| Stage | Language/archive/performance | Costume/material implications | Evidence caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Settlement and regional adaptation, before sustained European contact | Eastern Polynesian inheritance develops in Aotearoa; oral transmission and local histories | Local fibre, timber, stone, feathers, and skins; changing climate/resources shape techniques | Date settlement and particular objects with archaeological sources; precise calendar here (to-verify) |
| Early encounters and nineteenth-century transformations | Māori literacy, manuscripts, newspapers, Christianity, new political and religious movements | Introduced metal, cloth, and other materials adopted alongside existing practices | Do not stage the entire nineteenth century as a pristine “before” followed by sudden replacement |
| Colonial dispossession and language pressure, later nineteenth–mid-twentieth centuries | Land loss, schooling, uneven language transmission; continued performance and knowledge holding | Mixed dress and technologies; ongoing making and care of taonga | Hostile observers and collector preferences distort what is visible |
| Post-war urbanisation and revitalisation | Urban institutions, language recovery, immersion education, new compositions and media | Modern everyday dress alongside occasion-specific kākahu, moko, and taonga | Revitalisation is not mere revival of a frozen inventory |
| Contemporary differentiated practice | Iwi initiatives, digital archives, broadcasting, intergenerational learning | New materials, inherited techniques, contemporary makers, provenance systems | Date the source; no claim here about current participation numbers |

- **Costume signature:** harakeke is New Zealand flax (*Phormium*), not linen flax (*Linum*); prepared muka fibre and leaf strips support different techniques and effects.
- Distinguish *raranga* plaiting, *whatu* finger-twining, and *tāniko* patterned work; do not equip every maker with a European floor loom. Fine technical comparisons need textile/object documentation (Tamarapa 2011; passage details (to-verify)).
- Distinguish rain-protective pākē, fine muka kaitaka, tasselled korowai, feathered kahu huruhuru, and dog-skin kahu kurī. These are useful type distinctions, not an exhaustive taxonomy or a date-free wardrobe.
- Do not call every prestigious feathered cloak a korowai, or every cloak royal regalia. Maker, named type, occasion, community, and object provenance should drive the description.
- Do not project a modern kapa haka ensemble, piupiu, or every feather-cloak style unchanged into the earliest settlement era; date surviving examples and construction histories separately (to-verify) for a particular staging.
- **Coupling:** what an orator wears may intensify the significance of an occasion, but wearing a cloak or pendant does not automatically confer speaking rights. Social mandate is not a costume unlock.

## 11. Naming practices: relationships before name lists

- Person, group, house, object, and place names can preserve ancestors, events, or remembered relationships; some meanings depend on histories not recoverable by dictionary segmentation (McRae 2017).
- Group names containing **Ngāti**, **Ngāi**, or **Te Āti** often involve descent affiliations. Do not treat these as interchangeable fantasy surname prefixes; exceptions and particular derivations need verification.
- A pepeha may locate the speaker through ancestral and geographical relationships. It is not permission for an outsider to claim a mountain, river, waka, or iwi from a fill-in-the-blanks worksheet.
- Personal names can change, have variants, or coexist with baptismal and English forms in post-contact records. Establish the particular biography before identifying two recorded names as either different people or one person.
- Preserve attested vowel length, local spellings, and the distinction between an archival spelling and a present preferred form; annotate instead of silently harmonising.
- **Fictional method:** decide what kinds of relationships names remember, who can bestow or change them, how contact adds naming layers, and how archives cross-reference variants. Then generate names from an independently designed language.

## 12. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Authority is coupled:** separate belonging, eloquence, ritual training, custodianship, and visible prestige; demonstrate where they align and where they do not.
2. **Archive through relations:** link genealogy, landscape, compressed sayings, song, manuscripts, and objects; retain variant accounts and access conditions.
3. **Performance does work:** give collective speech an occasion, attribution, addressee, and response, not merely an intimidating sound and movement palette.
4. **Material biography matters:** trace maker → source → transformation → transfer → present care; avoid treating every heirloom as fungible money or every taonga as immovable property.
5. **Stage real change:** connect literacy, introduced materials, colonial pressure, urbanisation, and revitalisation without a ladder from “primitive” to “modern.”
6. **Handoff, not canon:** give lore-shokunin design options, dependencies, contradictions, and source/access status. This entry adopts no world-bible people, gods, names, rites, or costume facts.

## 13. Provenance cautions and stop rules

- **Evidence is uneven, not absent:** rich oral, manuscript, object, and contemporary records exist. Early externally dated chronicles and local technical detail are uneven; oral sequence is not automatically a Gregorian chronology.
- Colonial publications may combine local variants, suppress Māori authorship, or translate through Christian/evolutionist assumptions. Harlow specifically warns about Grey and White; McRae emphasises Māori composers, writers, and later interpreters.
- A collector's publication date is not the birth date of a tradition; a modern recording is not proof of recent invention. Conversely, an “ancient” marketing label is not provenance.
- Reject discredited racial-stage narratives and unsupported lost-civilisation claims that deny Polynesian settlement and Māori agency. Motif resemblance is not evidence of Egyptian, Celtic, or other speculative founders.
- Bibliographic checking is not full-text verification: this pass read the Harlow and McRae extracts, Te Papa's moko page, Ngāi Tahu's pounamu page, the orthography landing page, and the cloak publisher description. Other books/articles are identified reference works, not claimed cover-to-cover readings; Te Ara and several catalogue pages blocked direct access.
- Every **(to-verify)** marks a real limit, not an invitation to fill it with plausible detail. Obtain the relevant edition, object record, local attribution, or knowledge-holder guidance before promoting that detail into a deliverable.
- For direct representation, adapted text, reproduced design, named taonga, or staged ritual: stop at the reference boundary and seek the relevant Māori collaborators/authorities. Neither this corpus nor a generic disclaimer supplies consent.
