## Scope, evidence, and working frameworks
- Treat the coast as interconnected towns, islands, villages, and hinterland relationships, not a timeless nation or a single court culture. Lamu/Pate, Mombasa, Zanzibar, and Kilwa are not interchangeable samples.
- Distinguish coastal Swahili identities from the much wider modern population using Kiswahili as a first or additional language. Language competence does not settle descent, class, religion, or town affiliation.
- **Comparative historical linguistics:** classify inheritance through systematic correspondences and shared innovations, not the apparent foreignness of prestigious words (Nurse & Hinnebusch 1993).
- **Ethnogenesis and charter traditions:** ask what an origin claim authorizes, who repeats it, and when it was recorded; historical memory and political usefulness need not exclude one another (Spear 1984).
- **Communicative frame and equivocation:** distinguish a sign's wording from whether participants acknowledge that a message was addressed to someone (Beck 2001: 158–166).
- **Contact-strata worksheet:** inherited structure / borrowing / circulation route / register / earliest evidence. This is a craft heuristic, not a claim that cultures form clean geological layers.
- Evidence checked 2026-10-17: full texts of Beck (2001) and Brielle et al. (2023), WOLD entries, and Gimenez Amoros's review; other consultation limits are recorded in the source register.
- Recheck before close historical imitation: local scholarship, speaker usage, garment dates, manuscript editions, and specific performance recordings. Unresolved detail is stated as such below; silence in the sources consulted is not permission to improvise evidence.

## Language family and contact strata
Kiswahili is a Bantu language of the Sabaki group within Niger-Congo; Arabic lexical influence does not make it Semitic or automatically a creole. Its African linguistic history is independently recoverable (Nurse & Hinnebusch 1993; Brielle et al. 2023).

| Layer or process | Diagnostic evidence | Coupling use; inference to refuse |
|---|---|---|
| Bantu inheritance | Noun classes, concord, verbal morphology, inherited vocabulary | Let ordinary grammar organize borrowed words; do not equate African inheritance with an isolated or static population. |
| Arabic contacts | Religious, literary, commercial, and everyday vocabulary | Different institutions can circulate related vocabulary; an Arabic etymology does not identify a speaker's ethnicity or social rank. |
| Persian and South Asian circulation | Maritime vocabulary with competing proposed relay languages | Separate ultimate word history from immediate donor; a Persian-family word need not document direct Persian settlement. |
| Portuguese and later European contacts | Historically later lexical innovations in the wider contact record | Date a particular word through evidence; political arrival is not automatically its borrowing date (Schadeberg 2009). |
| Intra-Swahili circulation and standardization | Dialect mixture, literary usage, and a Kiunguja-based standard | Present-day standard forms are not transparent transcripts of every medieval port (Schadeberg 2009). |

- Observable integration: Arabic *kitāb* → Swahili *kitabu*, noun classes 7/8; plural *vitabu*. The borrowed form participates in Swahili grammar, not Arabic broken-plural formation (Schadeberg 2009, “kitabu”; plural corroborated by the living dictionary listed below).
- Craft test: make a borrowed sacred object agree with inherited noun-class morphology before adding exotic-looking vocabulary. A loan list alone is not a contact language model.
- Do not apply a fixed “Arabic percentage” to dialogue. Estimates depend on word lists, genre, and what counts as a distinct word; WOLD samples meanings rather than all actual speech.
- WOLD calls *bahari* “sea, ocean” clearly borrowed while marking its referent as present before contact: borrowing a sea-word does not prove borrowing knowledge of the sea.
- WOLD's broad “pre-modern Swahili, 800–1940” age category is not an attestation date for an individual nautical term. Never turn that bin into an eighth-century quotation.

## Writing, register, and naming practices
- Separate Arabic-language religious texts, Swahili written in Arabic-derived scripts (often discussed as Ajami), and modern Latin-script Kiswahili. Script and language are different variables; Kiswahili in Arabic script has no single standard orthography, so exact conventions vary by writer and manuscript (Raia 2021; Mumin & Versteegh 2014).
- Arabic-script Swahili poetry and later Latin-script standardization belong to different institutional histories; changing script does not itself demonstrate a change of religion. Background: Nurse & Spear (1985); manuscript-level examples in Raia (2021) and Luffin, in Mumin & Versteegh (2014).
- Naming worksheet: personal name / patronymic chain / honorific / household or lineage affiliation / town identification / nickname / documentary spelling. Do not force these into a universal given-name–surname template.
- Muslim personal-name repertoires and *bin* / *binti* patronymic elements intersect with local speech and histories. A name of Arabic origin is not an ethnic certificate; onomastic studies document Swahili naming strategies such as nicknaming (Taji 2024), but no coast-wide frequency table or single rule-set by town and era is claimed here.
- Documented anchors: *Ali bin al-Hasan* in the Kilwa historical discussion (Brielle et al. 2023); *Siti binti Saad* in taarab history (Topp Fargion 2014, via Gimenez Amoros 2015). Do not derive a universal naming law from two biographies.
- A dynastic descent label, a performance name, and a cloth's *jina* (“name,” including its inscription) perform different social jobs. Keep their record types separate even when the same language labels them “names.”
- For a fictional system, specify who may shorten a name, invoke an ancestor, or use a title; keep the choices as proposals for lore-shokunin rather than biographies of invented rulers.

## Shirazi origin traditions: memory, status, and competing evidence
- “Shirazi” refers to traditions of origins associated with Shiraz/Persia; do not silently substitute “Arab.” Founding accounts concern different towns and social claims, not one independently documented coast-wide migration.
- Spear (1984) is a central critical treatment. Read origin traditions as historical evidence requiring interpretation, neither literal passenger lists nor worthless inventions.
- The Kilwa Chronicle tradition was committed to writing in the sixteenth century, substantially later than the medieval beginnings it describes (Brielle et al. 2023). The two surviving versions—the Portuguese *Crónica* printed in João de Barros’s *Décadas* (1552) and the Arabic *Kitāb*—have a contested relationship: Delmas (2017) questions the presumed single original manuscript.
- Scarcity matters: surviving dynastic narratives do not give equally detailed access to fishing households, rural communities, enslaved people, or women's accounts. Absence from a ruler list is not absence from history.

| Evidence stream | What it supports | What it cannot establish alone |
|---|---|---|
| Origin narratives and genealogies | How belonging and legitimate precedence were narrated; possible memories of migration | Exact voyage, founder biography, or uninterrupted hereditary rule |
| Archaeology | Local settlement continuity alongside changing trade, mosques, tombs, and imported goods | The language or descent identity of every occupant from an imported bowl |
| Historical linguistics | African/Bantu linguistic inheritance plus contact histories | Individual ancestry, marriage arrangements, or a dynasty's truthfulness |
| Ancient DNA | Particular sampled people's ancestry affinities and modelled population interactions | Cultural invention, legal legitimacy, ethnic authenticity, or the literal truth of every chronicle episode |

- Brielle et al. (2023) support African–Asian ancestry interactions beginning by about 1000 CE, including Persian-associated ancestry and strongly sex-biased contributions in sampled groups. This complicates a claim that all Persian connections were imaginary; it does not restore a foreign-founders theory of Swahili civilization.
- Their sampled coastal individuals date chiefly to the thirteenth–eighteenth centuries, often from prominent Muslim burial contexts; Kenya is better represented than several other coastal regions. Earlier contact dates are modelled, not dates printed on sampled founders' graves.
- Genetic proxies are not medieval passports. The study cannot by itself identify consent, coercion, household language, or precise marriage institutions; do not convert sex bias into a romantic or violent universal founding story.
- Craft transfer: let a genealogy, an inherited grammar, a house's material record, and family migration memories answer different questions. Their partial agreement and disagreement are productive; neither makes descent a biological entitlement.

## Mythic and religious core: avoid inventing a pantheon
- The major historical religious framework is Islam, with a monotheistic cosmos, prophetic history, sacred texts, and religious learning—not an interchangeable set of “sea gods” (Pouwels 1987; Brielle et al. 2023).
- Shirazi ancestry narratives are origin/legitimacy traditions, not a creation myth or evidence that historical founders were worshipped as deities.
- Coastal spirit-possession traditions deserve their own local histories. Giles (1987) explicitly re-examines “marginality” explanations; do not reduce participants to a uniform category of excluded or powerless people.
- Keep analytical categories distinct: God, prophets, saints, Islamic jinn, ancestors, and named local spirits must not be flattened into “pantheon members.” Detailed equivalences, local terms, ritual lists, and doctrinal disputes are thinly covered here; for documented coastal possession cults see Alpers (1984) and Larsen (2008).
- Sacred geography can be a design question—who maintains a tomb, authorizes a ritual, or disputes a place's status—but a generic “syncretic island cult” is not an adequate historical description.
- Do not reconstruct pre-Islamic coastal religion by treating a modern possession repertoire as an unchanged survival. Continuity requires evidence, not an “African substrate” label.

## Symbolic systems: social indexing rather than a decoder ring
| Sign-bearing system | Ask about | Coupled consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Founder genealogy | Narrator, audience, occasion, claim to precedence | Mythic history becomes a political speech act, not merely a list of ancestors. |
| Cloth inscription | Wording, wearer/giver, recipient, occasion, denied intent | Language becomes public dress while preserving room for disputed address. |
| Poetic performance | Poet, singer, patron, audience, rival, venue | The apparent speaker need not be the author or the social target. |
| Imported objects and coral-built monuments | Access, ownership, local manufacture, display context | Oceanic connection and status must be tested against locally rooted production. |

- Beck (2001: 162–164) shows that kanga patterns and inscriptions are not permanently paired: the same design can carry different wording and vice versa. Do not assign every flower, color, or border an invariant message.
- Design rule: record denotation / social association / contested interpretation separately. Borrow the mechanism of indexing relations, not an invented universal “Swahili symbolism.”

## Era model: overlapping local transformations, not civilization levels
Dates below are approximate orientation, not synchronized stages for every settlement; the first three rows follow the archaeological-historical synthesis in Brielle et al. (2023).

| Working horizon | Coupled changes to stage | Anachronism check |
|---|---|---|
| Later first millennium CE | Coastal farming/fishing settlements, regional exchange, Indian Ocean contacts, early Muslim presence | Do not require a fully stone-built town or a late printed costume to signal oceanic connection. |
| Roughly eleventh–fifteenth centuries | More elaborate coral-built mosques/tombs, expanding towns and trade, varied urban/rural relationships | Do not infer that stone construction or Islam required the replacement of local populations. |
| Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries | Portuguese intervention; later Omani power, differently experienced by different towns | Do not narrate a single simultaneous collapse or make every coastal Muslim Omani. |
| Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Zanzibar-centred commerce, slavery and emancipation, imported printed cotton, developing taarab institutions | Printed kanga and orchestral taarab belong here, not automatically in medieval Kilwa. |
| Twentieth century onward | Recording, changing patronage, nationalism, broadcast/electronic media, heritage markets | “Traditional” dress or music can be recent, contested, and dynamically remade. |

- The late horizons use Beck (2001) and Topp Fargion (2014), the latter consulted through Gimenez Amoros (2015); do not project Zanzibar's political chronology onto Lamu or the whole coast.
- Stage each system separately: political control, donor vocabulary, script, cloth supply, and performance venue can change at different speeds. A later cloth can carry an older saying without proving an ancient garment.

## Taarab: poetic register, participation, and historical change
- Taarab is an Indian Ocean-connected performance tradition, especially important to Zanzibar and other coastal centres; it is not simply Arabic music with translated words. Compare Topp Fargion (2002, 2014).
- The researched history includes elite patronage, musical clubs, women performers and audiences, recordings, and different ensemble practices. The nineteenth-century court-origin account is not a complete history of all coastal music.
- Siti binti Saad's performances and commercial recordings from the 1920s onward are important to vernacular taarab history (Topp Fargion 2014, via Gimenez Amoros 2015: 211–212). Recording began with the 1928 Bombay sessions for Gramophone, followed around 1930 by Columbia in Zanzibar and Odeon in Mombasa (Denning 2015); full discographic detail is not reproduced here.
- Register worksheet: ordinary lexical meaning / poetic metaphor or proverbial wording / rhyme and verse organization / understood target / audience response. Verify a particular text's metre rather than imposing one template on every taarab song.
- A love lyric can be interpreted through a rivalry, grievance, or social relationship; that interpretation is not recoverable from translation alone. Record the performance context before claiming who a song attacks.
- Distinguish a composed text's speaker, its singer, the person requesting or sponsoring it, and listeners appropriating it. Those roles need not coincide.
- *Kidumbak* and *mpasho/mipasho* belong to discussions of changing participation and competitive expression; they are not synonyms for the entirety of taarab. Gimenez Amoros (2015: 212–213) reports these distinctions from Topp Fargion.
- The review describes distinctions in dress expectations between orchestral events and kidumbak contexts: costume can regulate access to performance, not merely illustrate its lyrics. Do not universalize that contrast beyond the studied setting.
- “Old is gold” and newer electronic forms express arguments over value and authenticity. Avoid a decline narrative in which contemporary women or young musicians merely corrupt an originally pure foreign style.
- Craft transfer: define who controls the venue, who commissions words, who may respond, and which clothing makes attendance affordable or prestigious. Change these institutions across eras before changing only the instruments.

## Kanga: speaking textiles and deniable address
- Kanga are printed cotton wraps, prominently used by women, with a border (*pindo*), central field (*mji*), and usually a proverbial inscription or “name” (*jina*) (Beck 2001: 157).
- Their late-nineteenth-century history intersects with slavery, emancipation, belonging, trade, and fashion. Beck discusses printed imitation of elite needlework as both an affiliation claim and a continuing marker of hierarchy (2001: 157–158).
- Exact first manufacture, first printed inscription, and single-inventor narratives require comparison of collections and archival evidence; the earliest extant printed examples date to 1886, and pre-1883 commercial records are largely lost (Ryan 2018). Do not present an attractive origin anecdote as settled fact.
- Calling these “proverb cloths” does not mean every inscription is an ancient traditional proverb, every wrap bears text, or every wearing is a deliberate message.

| Communicative layer | Evidence to record | Why interpretation stays open |
|---|---|---|
| Material sign | Pattern, inscription, orientation, folds, visibility | Text may be partially hidden; the design may prompt a memorized “name.” |
| Transaction | Bought, worn, displayed, or given; by whom and to whom | A gift has a different address structure from a garment glimpsed in a crowd. |
| Social occasion | Recent quarrel, advice, congratulations, ordinary fashion | The same words need not point to the same event or person. |
| Communicative frame | Whether a sender acknowledges intending a message | Denial can itself become part of the dispute. |
| Consequence | Hurt, reconciliation, accusation, refusal, uncertainty | Plausible deniability is not guaranteed immunity from social sanction. |

- Attested example: *Ataka yote hukosa yote*, “Who wants all, usually loses all” (Beck 2001: 158–161). The published case concerns a gift read as blame during marital breakdown; the giver denied communicative intent.
- Transfer the structure, not the ethnographic subjects: an apparently general maxim can be understood as targeted reproach while the giver claims it was simply a gift. That is a model of indirect address, not a secret cipher.
- Beck's account allows participation through remembered pattern–inscription associations; inability to read letters does not necessarily prevent understanding a kanga's message (2001: 163–164).
- Wearing a cloth inside-out or concealing its text can concern the communicative frame; it does not guarantee silence, since observers may infer an attempted avoidance (Beck 2001: 164–165).
- Craft application: specify who remembers designs, who prints inscriptions, how gifts circulate, and what happens when someone publicly names the supposed target. Couple literacy, textile technology, privacy, rank, and conflict.
- Calibration: Beck's fieldwork centres on 1990s Mombasa. Treat the analysis as a well-documented interactional model, not a timeless rule for all East African women or an uncomplicated celebration of empowerment.

## Costume and material signature
| Surface | Usable reference distinction | Required limit |
|---|---|---|
| Kanga | Printed cotton; visible design plus potentially remembered text; garment and gift | Historical print supply and local use matter; not a medieval default. |
| *Kanzu* and *kofia* | Robe/tunic and cap vocabulary; clothing, gender, occasion, and status require separate records | Cut, embroidery, and colour conventions vary by era and town; Zanzibar-side dating and discourse are documented in Fair (1998) and Hirji (2005), not reconstructed here. Modern glosses do not date a silhouette. |
| *Nguo* | General clothing/cloth vocabulary; WOLD finds no evidence of borrowing | An inherited word can name imported material; native vocabulary does not prove local manufacture. |
| Coral-built mosques/tombs and imported ceramics | Material connections can be displayed through locally rooted settlements and practices | Durable elite remains disproportionately survive; do not dress every resident as an elite merchant. |

- Schadeberg (2009) rates *kofia* “clearly borrowed” but *kanzu* only “perhaps borrowed.” Preserve that difference rather than asserting that both garments and their words arrived together from Arabia.
- Clothing worksheet: textile fibre → production/printing → merchant access → price → cut/wrapping → occasion → inscription → who can interpret it. Treat historical restrictions as questions until sourced, not automatically as sumptuary law.

## Dhow trade vocabulary: a contact network, not one boat type
“Dhow” is useful as an outside umbrella, not a sufficient local vessel classification. Distinguish craft type, propulsion, cargo, crew role, port vocabulary, and circulation route; the maritime-culture literature does provide hull typologies and vocabulary (Prins 1965, 1970; Walsh 2021) that are not reproduced here.

| Swahili item | Working sense | Provenance and coupling caution |
|---|---|---|
| *chombo*, plural *vyombo* | Vessel; also implement/tool | WOLD records ship/tool senses and no evidence of borrowing. Polysemy need not be a mystical cosmology. |
| *tanga* | Sail | WOLD: no evidence of borrowing. “No evidence” is not proof of an ultimate etymology or the sail's invention place. |
| *jahazi* | Ship; more specific vessel application needs local context | WOLD: clearly borrowed; competing source-word candidates (Arabic *ğahāz*, Persian *jahāz*, Hindi-Urdu *ğahāz*), with no single immediate donor chosen. |

- Entry-level anchors: Schadeberg's WOLD [“bahari”](https://wold.clld.org/word/72141734733972818), [“chombo”](https://wold.clld.org/word/72141734802701878), [“bandari”](https://wold.clld.org/word/72141734600576503); remaining forms are retrievable from the same vocabulary.
- Lexical extensions checked only against living dictionary entries: *nahodha* “captain,” *kaskazi* “north monsoon/north wind,” *kusi* “south monsoon/south wind.” The monsoon reversal that structures coastal sailing is documented (Library of Congress, “Africa in the Indian Ocean World”: southward sailings November–January, northward April–August); historical local senses, single-donor routes, and month-by-month calendars are not adjudicated here.
- Sailing depends on monsoons, currents, season, route, and vessel. Do not infer an itinerary solely from a wind-name gloss or give every coast the same departure month.
- Craft transfer: allocate vocabulary by task and network—crew, sailmaker, harbour official, cloth merchant, religious scholar—not by an assumption that every foreign word belongs to a foreign person.

## Application workflow: build a coupled proposal, not canon
1. Choose a particular town-scale analogue and historical horizon; state which neighbouring communities and ocean routes are relevant.
2. Specify inherited grammatical structures; add a small domain-tagged loan set with donor confidence and evidence dates kept separate.
3. Draft competing accounts of belonging as narrative functions: household memory, elite genealogy, public teaching, and outsider chronicle. Leave their truth status undecided.
4. Connect a textile's production and price to who can wear or gift it; specify the speaking-textile convention only if the fictional institutions support it.
5. Connect a performance register to audiences, venue access, clothing costs, sponsorship, and possible deniability; do not paste kanga rules mechanically onto all songs.
6. Change at least two linked institutions across a proposed era transition—for example print distribution and inscription circulation—not just a costume color or ruler's name.
7. Run an interpretation test: can identical words produce ordinary advice, targeted reproach, and denied intent in different contexts without changing their dictionary meaning?
8. Hand lore-shokunin options, assumptions, contradictions, and source confidence. Only the sibling's canon process may choose what becomes true of a fictional world.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- A locally inherited grammar can sustain far-reaching commercial and sacred vocabularies without becoming “half another language.”
- Genealogy, archaeology, language, and biological ancestry are different records; origin traditions gain narrative force without any one record settling all the others.
- A textile can carry socially consequential language through wearing, memory, gifting, and denied intent; its material supply is part of the communicative system.
- Poetry, music clubs, dress expectations, and audience participation can share a political history without being one unified aesthetic code.

## Provenance cautions and thin areas
- Colonial foreign-founder accounts exaggerated external authorship and marginalized African evidence; reversing them into a claim of no migration is not a sound correction. Keep African continuity and Indian Ocean interaction together.
- Dynastic chronicles are scarce, retrospective, selective evidence. Modern ethnography is not a transparent window into medieval institutions; Zanzibar is not a proxy for every port.
- Ancient-DNA samples are geographically and socially selective. Never use ancestry proportions as a measure of cultural ownership, authenticity, or a person's rightful place in society.
- Detailed spirit taxonomies, local naming frequencies, pre-nineteenth-century clothing cuts, precise vessel classifications, and earliest inscription histories are thinly treated here; where relevant literature is identified it is cited above (Larsen 2008; Taji 2024; Hirji 2005; Walsh 2021; Ryan 2018), and no invented details fill the gaps.
- Distinguish consulted texts, bibliographic confirmations, secondary reviews, and provisional lexical checks. Page-specific claims require the cited page, manuscript, object record, or recording—not a plausible-looking reference list.
- All application guidance is method and reference knowledge. Nothing in this entry establishes fictional canon; unresolved choices remain for lore-shokunin.
