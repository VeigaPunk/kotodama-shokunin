## 1. Scope and calibration

- **High confidence:** Tang was linguistically and religiously plural; written elite culture, pronunciation norms, and everyday speech are different objects. “Middle Chinese as lingua franca” is useful only after making those distinctions (Norman 1988; Pulleyblank 1984; Lewis 2009).
- **Model-dependent:** reconstructed Middle Chinese is not a recording, and alternative reconstructions disagree on phonetic values. A reconstruction must carry its author, stage, and notation; do not assemble an inventory from incompatible systems.
- **Interpretive:** “cosmopolitan empire” describes substantial contact and appropriation, not equal citizenship, uninterrupted tolerance, or a society without slavery, conquest, hierarchy, and ethnic hostility (Lewis 2009; Schafer 1963).
- **Design method:** the coupling tables below are this entry's analytical tools, not purported Tang theories. Historical description does not authorize copying an entire people into a fictional counterpart.
- Modern Pinyin is used for lookup where supplied; it does **not** reproduce Tang pronunciation. Chinese characters identify terms across editions but do not fix one spoken realization.

## 2. Era staging: institutions change before the aesthetic label does

| Stage | Historical anchors | Coupled change to investigate | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Formation, 618–649 | Tang inherits Sui institutions and much older northern/southern cultural strata | Rhyme learning, official writing, aristocratic lineage, military power, and court dress need not share the same origin | Making all Tang systems new inventions of 618 |
| Gaozong/Wu and restoration, 649–705 | Buddhist sponsorship and court competition; Wu's Zhou dynasty, 690–705, interrupts Tang rule | Translation authority, sacred legitimation, women's political position, and display can change together without moving uniformly | Erasing Wu's separate dynastic claim; inferring ordinary women's freedom from one sovereign |
| Xuanzong and the pre-rebellion court, 712–755 | Especially visible courtly fashion, foreign music, and literary patronage | Imported forms acquire prestige through court adoption and artisan reproduction | Treating Chang'an luxury as the empire's daily material baseline |
| Rebellion and reconstruction, 755–early ninth century | An Lushan/An-Shi rebellion, 755–763; Two-Tax reform, 780 | War, displacement, provincial power, and fiscal reorganization alter patronage and textile circulation | A single overnight end to international contact or to hu fu |
| Later Tang, c. 820–907 | Buddhist suppression in 845; subsequent religious continuities; regionalization and renewed warfare | Confiscation, changed sponsorship, provincial literary networks, and uneven survival of institutions | A uniform “late decadence” costume or the extinction of Buddhism |

- These are overlapping working windows, not a replacement for political chronology. “Early/High/Middle/Late Tang” literary labels do not divide every region, garment, or religious practice at the same dates (Lewis 2009; Cai 2008; Chen 2019).
- For a fictional era sheet, date changes separately: **sound / vocabulary / recruitment / ritual patron / production / clothing silhouette**. One event may affect all six through different delays.

## 3. Language family, writing, and the qualified lingua-franca model

| Layer | Tang-relevant description | Worldbuilding operation |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogical affiliation | Chinese belongs to the Sinitic branch of Sino-Tibetan; internal Sino-Tibetan subgrouping is not settled by calling Chinese “isolating” | Separate ancestry from grammatical type and political allegiance |
| Spoken varieties | Regional Chinese varieties coexist with mobility and prestige speech; the boundaries and mutual intelligibility of specific Tang varieties are incompletely recoverable | Specify which speakers need accommodation or interpreters; do not assume modern dialect borders |
| Middle Chinese | A historical linguistic label, especially associated with the phonological distinctions encoded by the rhyme tradition; early and late stages must be distinguished | Give the conlang a dated sound system rather than a generic “ancient Chinese sound” |
| Qieyun tradition | Lu Fayan's *Qieyun* (601, Sui) organizes literary pronunciation through rhyme categories and fanqie spellings; its northern/southern distinctions are not a simple census of one city's speech | Let examination norms preserve distinctions absent from some candidates' home speech |
| Court/prestige speech | Spoken supraregional accommodation and prestige norms are plausible and historically significant; equating them exactly with the entire Qieyun system is contested | Define a court koine separately; exact seventh-century Chang'an phonetics remain model-dependent |
| Literary Chinese | A learned written medium used across regions and beyond the empire; not an unchanged transcription of contemporary conversation | Give literate elites a shared written register without making their spoken languages identical |
| Contact languages | Sogdian, Turkic languages, Sanskrit and other Indic languages, Tibetan, and others enter different commercial, military, diplomatic, and religious networks | Assign a language to an institution and route, not to a generic “foreign quarter” |

- The strongest lingua-franca analogy is **a network of elite written communication plus prestige reading/speech practices**, not universal fluency in a uniform language named “Middle Chinese” (Norman 1988; Pulleyblank 1984; Sen 2003).
- Chinese writing is morphosyllabic, not a language-free ideographic code: characters commonly relate a syllable to a morpheme, with extensive phonetic components. Knowing characters alone does not supply syntax or erase language barriers (Norman 1988).
- Regular, running, and cursive hands offer different legibility and performance possibilities; seal forms have specialized uses. A brush manuscript, inscription, and seal impression need not share a visual register.
- Fanqie indicates a character's reading using the initial of one character and the remainder of another. It is a learned phonological technique, not an alphabet and not modern Pinyin.
- Ordinary conversation, women's speech, artisan speech, and many non-elite regional registers are less directly documented than elite writing; detailed reconstructions of their social distribution are **(to-verify)** rather than licensed by literary dialogue.

## 4. Buddhist translation: an inherited vocabulary expands and branches

- Chinese Buddhist vocabulary had accumulated for centuries before Tang. Tang translation projects intensify, revise, and institutionalize that inheritance; “vocabulary explosion” is a qualitative description here, not an invented count of Tang coinages (Mair 1994; Sen 2003).
- Xuanzang returned in 645 and led major court-supported translation work; Yijing's return in 695 belongs to another important translation network. Translators, assistants, patrons, textual collections, and learned readers form an infrastructure, not a lone sage producing transparent equivalents (Sen 2003).

| Mechanism | Historical examples | Coupling and caution |
|---|---|---|
| Phonographic borrowing | 佛 “Buddha”; 菩薩 “bodhisattva”; 般若 “prajñā” | Indic Buddhist names/terms become Chinese lexical forms through transmission and adaptation; these examples predate Tang, and not every route is direct Sanskrit-to-Chinese |
| Semantic specialization | 業, ordinary “work/deed,” acquires the Buddhist technical sense “karma”; 空, “empty,” serves technical discussions of emptiness | The ordinary and doctrinal senses coexist; do not translate karma as simple destiny or emptiness as nihilism |
| Translated compound | 佛性, “Buddha-nature” | Chinese word-formation supports disputed doctrinal categories; the compound is not a Tang invention, nor proof that all schools agreed on its interpretation |
| Mixed formation | 阿賴耶識, the Yogācāra term for ālayavijñāna | Transcribed ālaya plus translated “consciousness”; a specialist register can retain foreign prestige and local grammatical transparency together |
| Competing translation choices | Older and newer renderings, transliteration variants, glosses, and commentarial distinctions | A revised term may mark a lineage, curriculum, or claim to textual authority rather than a change in everyday speech |

- Do not reduce early Chinese Buddhism to “Daoist words pasted over Indian religion.” Similar vocabulary can support accommodation, polemic, or reinterpretation; specify text, translator, school, and period.
- Mair's (1994) influential argument links Buddhism to the legitimation and growth of written vernaculars. Treat it as a historical explanatory thesis, not a monocausal law that Buddhism invented vernacular Chinese or national languages.
- Lexicon ledger fields: **source form → intermediary, if known → adapted sound → written representation → gloss → institution → first attestation → later senses**. Unknown intermediary or first attestation stays **(to-verify)**.
- Fictional application: let an imported doctrine create rival technical words, specialist pronunciation, new ritual jobs, donor inscriptions, and a demand for books/textiles. Do not make every imported word instantly common or every borrowed term magically efficacious.

## 5. Examination register: language as a selective institution

| Register/institution | What it rewards or performs | Coupled consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Classical learning; mingjing examinations | Knowledge and interpretation of canonical texts, with formats changing over time | Schools and commentary traditions control access to authoritative wording |
| Jinshi examinations | Literary composition, including poetry and fu in important phases, alongside other requirements such as policy questions; no single timeless Tang syllabus | Rhyme learning, allusion, patronage, copying, and leisure become career resources |
| Parallel prose, pianwen | Balanced phrasing, literary diction, allusion, and formal display in elite writing | A court document can advertise cultivation while remaining difficult for outsiders |
| Classical-prose advocacy, guwen | Mid-Tang writers including Han Yu and Liu Zongyuan advocate alternative classical models and purposes | Register reform is a cultural and political argument, not simply an instruction to write casually |

- Examinations grew in prestige but did not constitute a modern universal meritocracy or the only road to office. Family position, hereditary privilege, recommendation, and personal networks remained consequential (McMullen 1988; Lewis 2009).
- Separate **passing an examination / receiving an appointment / holding a rank / possessing permission to display that rank**. They are not one automatic event.
- A fictional assessment sheet should name the judging authority, permitted texts, sound/rhyme norm, genre, taboo vocabulary, and cost of preparation; record which groups cannot realistically train.
- Do not import the later eight-legged essay into Tang. Do not back-project the Song examination system's scale and social effects unchanged (McMullen 1988; Wilkinson 2015).

## 6. Regulated verse: sound categories become social capital

- Tang poets develop earlier experiments in tonal and parallel composition into powerful regulated forms; not all Tang poetry is regulated verse. Li Bai, Du Fu, and Wang Wei are not interchangeable demonstrations of one aesthetic (Cai 2008).

| Feature | Working description | Design implication |
|---|---|---|
| Lüshi 律詩 | Normally eight lines, usually five or seven syllables/characters per line | A compact prestige form can require extensive schooling |
| Jueju 絕句 | Four-line forms; regulated quatrains exist, but the label alone does not guarantee every strict regulated constraint | Distinguish genre name from a validator's actual rule set |
| Pailü 排律 | Extended regulated verse with additional couplets | Formal mastery can be lengthened for occasions without inventing a different sound system |
| Tonal opposition | Level, ping, contrasts with oblique, ze; the latter groups the traditional rising, departing, and entering categories | These categories are not equivalent to the four numbered tones of modern Mandarin |
| Entering category, ru | Associated with stop-final syllables, reconstructed with endings such as -p, -t, -k | A prestige meter can fossilize distinctions lost in a later spoken variety |
| Rhyme | In standard lüshi, usually level-tone rhyme on even-numbered lines, with the first line optionally rhyming | A local pronunciation may conceal a historically licensed rhyme |
| Parallelism | The middle couplets, lines 3–4 and 5–6 of lüshi, normally require semantic/syntactic correspondence | Landscape, office, time, motion, and emotion become paired semantic resources |

- Tonal patterns vary by five-/seven-syllable form and opening pattern; licensed substitutions and exceptions exist. This table is not a complete historical scansion algorithm (Cai 2008; Norman 1988).
- Do not judge Tang rhymes with Pinyin alone, and do not silently substitute a later standardized rhyme inventory for the particular Tang practice being discussed.
- Fictional construction order: establish the sound-stage → define rhyme classes → choose line length → define tonal opposition → specify parallel slots → identify schools and exceptions → name the audiences that reward mastery.
- If the invented language is not tonal, adapt the **institutional mechanism** using stress, quantity, alliteration, or another real contrast; do not label an unrelated system “Tang meter.”
- Couple verse with dress through the occasion: an examination, court audience, monastery dedication, and private gathering authorize different performances. Shared elite competence does not mean one outfit or one poetic register everywhere.

## 7. Mythic and ritual core: overlapping authorities, not one closed pantheon

| Authority/cult field | Tang-relevant core | Language/material coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Imperial and ancestral rites | Heaven, Earth, imperial ancestors, and authorized territorial powers support rulership and ordered relationships | Calendars, official ritual wording, designated spaces, and graded ceremonial equipment make hierarchy actionable |
| Buddhist communities | Śākyamuni, Amitābha, Avalokiteśvara/Guanyin, and other buddhas/bodhisattvas; karmic rebirth, merit, relics, salvation, and multiple cosmologies; prominence varies by place and school | Translation choices, image inscriptions, sutra copying, donations, and monastic clothing link doctrine to production |
| Daoist institutions | Revealed scriptures, ritual specialists, celestial powers, sacred geography, and longevity practices; the ruling Li house claimed association with Laozi | Dynastic genealogy can privilege a sacred figure and specialist textual authority |
| Local and household practice | Ancestors, ghosts, protective powers, and landscape cults; documentation is uneven and elite accounts often judge them | Offerings, epitaphs, shrine names, ritual specialists, and local narratives need not fit one official taxonomy |
| Imported religious communities | Zoroastrian, Manichaean, and Church of the East communities are attested under Tang | Distinguish community, period, script, patronage, and legal status; “foreign religion” is not one institution |

- “Three teachings” is a useful historical classificatory idiom, not evidence of three sealed modern denominations. Participation, patronage, borrowing, and polemic cross boundaries (Lewis 2009).
- Teiser's (1988) Ghost Festival study shows Buddhist monastic offerings and filial/ancestral obligations operating together in the seventh month of the lunisolar calendar. Mulian/Maudgalyāyana narratives offer a coupling of family duty, afterlife suffering, ritual action, and communal support.
- Do not project every later Mulian drama detail backward into Tang: distinguish early accounts, medieval manuscripts, later theatre, and modern festival practice.
- Do not import the complete celestial cast of later novels as a Tang pantheon, or make an Indic Buddhist deva equivalent to a universal creator god.
- In fiction, specify who licenses a rite, who pays, who translates its formula, what it promises, and what material object is necessary. Similar promises can produce rival institutions rather than a neat merger of gods.

## 8. Symbol systems: contextual readings rather than a motif dictionary

| Form/code | Historically grounded reading domain | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Lotus imagery | Buddhist iconography and ritual; purity and sacred birth are relevant readings | Identify image, inscription, and setting before assigning a particular doctrine |
| Dragons | Royal/cosmic imagery and water-related powers occur in different contexts | No automatic “dragon = emperor,” and no unexamined later claw-count rank rule |
| Purple/scarlet/green/blue official garments | Rank and authorized display are regulated, but rules and exceptions change | Date the edict and office; this entry does not prescribe one dynasty-long color-to-rank chart |
| Pearl roundels and animal medallions | Textile design participates in Iranian/Central Asian and wider exchange networks | Pattern resemblance alone cannot identify the wearer's ethnicity or the textile's workshop |
| Wuxing and directional correspondences | Learned cosmology, calendrics, and political interpretation | Five phases are relational processes/correspondences, not five fantasy substances with universal color meanings |
| Written sacred forms | Sutra copying, dhāraṇī, and Daoist talismanic practices give writing different ritual functions | Do not flatten distinct textual/ritual technologies into generic “Chinese runes” |

- Lewis (2009), Chen (2019), Sen (2003), and Schafer (1963) supply different contexts, not an interchangeable master key. A symbolic reading needs a dated object/text and an identified user.
- Design test: for any emblem, distinguish **authorized meaning / fashionable imitation / outsider interpretation / material constraint**. Let these disagree without treating the culture as inconsistent.

## 9. Costume and material signature: hu fu is a relationship, not a uniform

- Hu fu 胡服, “Hu/foreign dress,” is a historical umbrella and evaluative label, not the name of a single stable ethnic costume. The content of “foreign” depends on the observer, date, and context (Chen 2019).

| Material/display field | Tang-relevant signature | Coupling to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Equestrian and hu-fu-associated styles | Robes/jackets, trousers, boots, belts, and headgear in changing combinations; elite women also adopt riding and male-coded styles | Mobility, court fashion, gender performance, and foreign prestige can intersect without sharing one cause |
| Official men's ordinary court dress | Round-collared robes, belts, and headwear occur within changing rank and occasion systems | An administrative category can be visible, but color alone does not establish a person's actual appointment |
| Elite women's fashion | Skirts, upper garments, shawls, coiffures, and changing proportions; some depictions feature broad sleeves and exposed upper chest | Date the ensemble; do not turn a fashionable elite image into every woman's daily or ritual dress |
| Silk economy | Silk textiles circulate through tax, payment, gifts, diplomacy, religious donation, and clothing | The same cloth can be fiscal substance, sacred offering, and prestige surface |
| Production | Fiber, weave, patterning, dye, finishing, and skilled labor constrain what can be imitated | A copied motif need not imply an imported finished garment |
| Non-elite clothing | Lower-cost plant-fibre cloth, repair, reuse, and occupational requirements deserve separate investigation | This entry cannot supply one empire-wide peasant outfit; regional cuts and social distribution are **(to-verify)** |
| Monastic dress | Robe ideals, donations, regulation, textile availability, and local practice interact | Do not assign every Buddhist monk one timeless robe color or equate ritual ideals with all surviving garments |

- Chen (2019) triangulates texts, tomb imagery, figurines, and textiles to reconstruct a fashion system. Tomb attendants and painted garments represent funerary choices, not photographs or complete sewing patterns.
- Women's adoption of hu fu is not proof of universal emancipation; increased sartorial agency can coexist with political and legal restrictions. Nor does wearing an imported style prove foreign ancestry.
- Avoid three common anachronisms: Ming/Qing-style animal rank badges as a generic Tang system; the Qing queue as “ancient Chinese”; and the modern tangzhuang jacket as direct evidence of Tang cutting practice.
- Garment record fields: **date / place / wearer-status / occasion / layer order / fastenings / fiber / weave / colorant / motif / source type / certainty**. Exact pattern reconstruction without an excavated garment is **(to-verify)**.

## 10. Naming practices: one person, several authorized labels

| Name layer | Tang-relevant practice | Coupled use |
|---|---|---|
| Family and personal name | Family name precedes personal name; compound surnames exist, as in Shangguan Wan'er | A two-character string is not automatically “given name + surname”; record characters and segmentation |
| Courtesy name, zi | Especially documented among elite men; Li Bai's courtesy name is Taibai, Du Fu's Zimei | Address choices index social relation; do not assign every inhabitant a complete elite naming kit |
| Office/place labels | Writers can be identified through office, origin, or association | A label can change with career or reception without a legal name change |
| Imperial identifiers | Personal, temple, posthumous, and reign names have distinct functions; Tang rulers can use several reign titles | “Xuanzong” is a temple name, not the emperor's spoken personal name or one continuous reign-era label |
| Name avoidance, bihui | Imperial and familial taboos influence speech/writing, substitutions, and textual transmission | Sacred/political authority can alter a lexicon, inscription, or reading without ordinary sound change |
| Women's and non-elite names | Surviving texts unevenly preserve personal names, kin relations, titles, and status labels | Documentary absence is not proof that people lacked names or that one elite male pattern was universal |

- Use Wilkinson (2015) for the name categories and textual cautions; check a dated biography/inscription before applying a specific avoidance rule. Exact local enforcement and substitutions are **(to-verify)** here.
- Do not impose a universal later lineage-generation naming scheme on all Tang families. For migrants, keep original name, Chinese transcription, adopted name, and official record form as separate fields where evidence permits.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take

1. Build a **prestige stack**, not one national tongue: home speech → accommodation speech → literary writing → examination reading → ritual specialist language. State who learns each and at what cost.
2. Build a **translation institution**: patrons, multilingual labor, disputed source texts, competing terms, recitation, manuscript circulation, and material donations; let vocabulary show the history of that institution.
3. Build an **auditable aesthetic gate**: a sound-dependent verse form and its training institutions select elites; an appointment and its dress privileges expose a different gate. Passing one need not unlock the other.
4. Build **uneven cosmopolitanism**: imported garments, instruments, terms, and gods can travel by different routes and be accepted at different rates. Foreign prestige and hostility can coexist.
5. Build **materially funded ritual**: kin duty, afterlife expectation, official legitimacy, silk wealth, and labor can meet in the same donation without everyone sharing a theology.
6. Build **dated symbolic disputes**: an emblem's authorized interpretation, fashionable use, and local sacred meaning may diverge; record the interpreter instead of deciding one meaning for all contexts.
7. Build **era lag**: pronunciation changes while rhyme norms persist; court styles spread after their political moment; outlawed institutions leave words and objects behind.
8. Hand lore-shokunin an **option packet**, not canon: historical mechanism + sources + fictional alternatives + consequences + uncertainty + decisions reserved for the world-bible owner.
