## Scope and calibration
- Diné are living peoples and a contemporary sovereign nation, not a vanished civilization or a reusable “Southwestern aesthetic.”
- Diné bizaad is the language name; Diné refers to the people. Navajo remains an established public and institutional name; follow an individual's stated preference.
- **Supported** below means corroborated by the cited public source; it does not mean universal across families, specialists, communities, or eras.
- **Interpretation** marks a comparative design method, not a Diné claim. `(to-verify)` marks a genuinely unresolved detail rather than disguising it as fact.
- This entry is strongest on publicly shared weaving knowledge, relational philosophy, and contemporary testimony; it is deliberately thin on chantway-specific knowledge and early historical reconstruction.
- Public accessibility is not permission to reproduce sacred knowledge. The craft boundary here excludes ceremonial diagrams, songs, prayers, ritual instructions, and invented claims of cultural authorization.

## Language family, writing, and register
| Layer | Supported description | Coupling consequence / misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Genealogy | Diné bizaad is Southern Athabaskan, also called Apachean, within the wider Athabaskan/Na-Dené grouping. [L87; secondary cross-check below] | Southwestern geography does not make it genealogically Hopi or Zuni; contact and descent are different relationships. |
| Sound contrasts | The standard introductory account distinguishes high and low tone, vowel length, and nasalization; basic vowel qualities are a, e, i, o. [L87; secondary cross-check] | A name's diacritics encode contrasts, not ornamental “exotic” lettering. |
| Writing | Practical writing uses a Latin-based orthography; doubled vowels mark length, acute accents high tone, and ogoneks nasalization. [L87; secondary cross-check] | Preserve meaningful characters in reference notes; do not strip nasalization and tone when generating look-alike names. |
| Consonants | ł represents a voiceless lateral fricative; apostrophe-like marks participate in writing glottal/ejective distinctions. [L87; secondary cross-check] | ł is not an accent on ordinary l; punctuation substitution can change a word. |
| Word building | Verb-centered, elaborate prefixal morphology carries grammatical distinctions including person, aspect, and mode. [L87; secondary cross-check] | A heap of English nouns with Navajo-looking suffixes is not an Athabaskan-style naming grammar. |
| Linguistic register | Pitch level contrasts in phonology are not the same thing as social register, formality, or ceremonial authority. | Model grammatical tone and speech situations as separate design variables. |
| Social positioning | P23 describes an introduction that identifies family relations and expands for a Diné audience. | Register can change how much relationship information is appropriate, not merely how ornate the vocabulary sounds. |
| Specialist speech | The public sources distinguish ordinary life, instruction, and healer-led ceremonial contexts. [H15; B23-H] | Do not manufacture a “sacred dialect” by translating a prayer or lowering the speaker's pitch. |
| Literacy and history | L87 is a revised modern reference; contemporary written forms are not evidence that the same spelling existed centuries earlier. | Stage literacy, schooling, and media histories separately from oral continuity. |

- Secondary phonological cross-check: [“Navajo language,” sections “Phonology,” “Orthography,” and bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navajo_language), consulted 2026-10-17; use L87 or a qualified speaker for production-level forms, not this mutable summary alone.
- Tone is not a universal property of Athabaskan languages: Proto-Athabaskan is reconstructed without tonal contrasts, and several daughter languages (e.g. Ahtna, Koyukon, and the Pacific Coast group) remain non-tonal; particular analyses of Diné tone and its history remain specialist work (Rice 1999; Krauss 2005).
- **Interpretation:** for a fictional language, choose its own segment inventory, morphology, and sound history before inventing names; then define everyday, public-formal, pedagogical, and restricted speech situations without copying Diné expressions.
- Hard linguistic determinism is not supported: complex verbs do not prove that all speakers share one metaphysics, nor does grammar compel an unchanging culture.

## Hózhǫ́, emergence, and mythic relationships
- H15 prints **Hózhó**; this entry also uses the assignment's form **hózhǫ́**. Do not silently normalize quotations; exact lexical/inflected forms for new Diné sentences require verification `(to-verify)`.
- H15 treats hózhó as an expansive way of living and state of wellbeing involving thought, speech, conduct, and relationships—not simply “physical beauty.”
- H15's **Hózhó Wellness Philosophy** and **Hózhó Resilience Model** are named contemporary scholarly formulations. Their six attributes and three domains are the authors' analytic models, not a timeless numerical catechism.
- The six attributes in that analysis are spirituality, respect, reciprocity, discipline, thinking, and relationships; the model groups its domains as harmony, respect, and spirituality. [H15]
- Do not translate disharmony into a single evil deity, or imply that illness proves moral failure. H15's cultural-health framework is not a clinical efficacy trial or a license to blame patients.

| Publicly discussed relationship | Reference foothold | Craft consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Earth-Surface People and Holy People | B23-H discusses ongoing relations between human and holy beings; J02 links collective identity, creation, language, and law. | “Pantheon” is a comparison heading, not proof of a Greek-style bureaucracy of gods with one job each. |
| First Man and First Woman | B23-H situates Diné journeys and adaptation in relation to these figures. | Emergence and movement can organize place and memory; do not turn a narrative into a dated migration chronicle. |
| Changing Woman | B23-D's basket discussion identifies connections to Changing Woman and women's lives. | Attend to personhood and relationships; a generic “fertility goddess” label loses the source's specificity. |
| Spider Woman and Spider Man | B23-H relates weaving teaching and loom construction through source-attributed origin accounts. | Craft can connect knowledge, tools, responsibility, and cosmology; retain variant attribution rather than fuse every telling. |
| Earth, sky, land, and human obligations | J02 §205 recognizes reciprocal obligations toward creation; Tapaha describes a loom as a microcosm. [B23-H] | Landscape is not a set of replaceable background props or a decorative motif mine. |

- “Central figure” depends on the practice and narrator: a weaving-focused exhibition's emphasis on Spider Woman is not evidence that every Diné ceremony has the same center.
- No chantway sequence, complete creation narrative, or definitive roster of holy beings is supplied here; expertise and permissions for those subjects are outside this entry.

## Sandpainting as ritual technology: sensitivity rules
- B23-H describes sandpaintings as ephemeral dry-colored images made by **hataali**, identified there as healers, in certain ceremonies. “Certain” matters: sandpainting is not a synonym for all Diné ritual.
- **Ritual technology** here is an external analytic label for coordinated material, image, specialist knowledge, and healing purpose—not an Indigenous technical term, a magic-system taxonomy, or a claim of biomedical efficacy.
- Distinguish an image's visual form from its ceremonial conditions of use. Knowing a published design is not knowing the practice or possessing authority to perform it.
- Temporariness belongs to this practice's material logic; permanent reproduction is not simply a better storage medium. Specific timing, patient interactions, and termination procedures are not supplied here `(to-verify for any particular ceremony)`.
- B23-H documents disagreement and perceived danger around circulating ceremonial imagery, as well as decisions by individual Diné weavers to produce ceremonial-theme textiles.
- Do not erase those artists' agency by claiming that every permanent sacred-theme image is forbidden; equally, one artist's choice does not give outsiders blanket authorization.

| Decision | Entry's operational boundary | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Need an example of coordinated healing practice | Discuss relationships among medium, trained practitioner, occasion, and community support at a high level. | Enough for comparison without reproducing a rite. |
| Found an archival ceremonial drawing | Record its provenance and restriction question; do not trace it into a fictional diagram. | Publication and collection history do not establish current community consent. |
| Want to “make it safe” by altering a design | Do not offer a changed-color or changed-detail recipe. | Source-described protective decisions are situated Diné practices, not an outsider's bypass. |
| Need fictional ritual art | Invent independent images, vocabulary, materials, histories, and authorization rules. | Transfer a problem of social organization, not an identifiable ceremonial composition. |
| Need an actual Diné portrayal | Seek paid, appropriately situated Diné expertise and agree on the scope of representation. | This reference note cannot confer permission or replace consultation. |

- Never supply chants, sacred figure templates, ceremonial sand layouts, or instructions for invoking beings; never relabel a copied sandpainting as a generic “mandala.”
- Avoid the omnibus label “shaman”; retain the source's description of a particular practitioner and do not conflate distinct healing roles.

## Weaving vocabulary as worldview and material practice
| Term or working vocabulary | Bounded reference meaning | Coupling insight |
|---|---|---|
| **dah iistłʼǫ́**, in P23's title | The title is translated “My Journey with the Loom”; a word-by-word etymology is not established here `(to-verify)`. | A tool can organize a life history, not merely label equipment. |
| **biil éé’** | B23-D identifies a woman's rug dress, illustrated by a two-panel garment with neck and arm openings. | Textile structure becomes dress, bodily protection, kin connection, and public presentation. |
| **hózhó / hózhǫ́** | P23 links weaving instruction, protocols, and harmony; H15 supplies the broader relational framework. | Beauty involves how making and living are conducted, not just the final surface. |
| **kʼé** | Relationships and obligations, not simply a synonym for a clan label. [H15; J02] | Learning, naming, care, and transmission can be interdependent. |
| **Na’ashjéii Asdzáá** | Spider Woman in the spelling used by B23-H. | Name and teaching belong to particular narrative relationships; not a deity-name generator. |
| Warp, weft, shed, heddle, batten, comb | English technical vocabulary used in B23-T; no unverified Diné translations are invented here. | Distinguish structural threads, the opening worked through, and tools that separate or pack them. |
| “Sectional weaving lines” / “lazy lines” | B23-T identifies joins arising from working in sections. | A construction trace is not evidence of laziness, incompetence, or a deliberate ritual defect. |
| “Chief,” “Child's,” “Two Grey Hills” | B23-D/V and P23 document collector terminology, market formats, and regional production histories. | A textile label may encode trade and outside classification rather than native rank or wearer age. |

- B23-T records particular weavers' regard for tools as animate, inherited, and sacred; attribute this to the speakers rather than declare every tool universally sacred in the same way.
- P23 makes family connection concrete: inherited designs, identifiable hands, memories of comb rhythms, household income, discrimination, and teaching all inhabit weaving practice.
- **Causal chain to model:** grazing conditions → sheep/fleece → preparation labor and water → yarn/dye access → weaving decisions → exchange and family livelihood. [B23-E; P23]
- That chain does not reduce cosmology to environmental necessity: a maker's explanation, a historian's account, and material constraints are distinct kinds of evidence.
- Do not universalize the popular “every Navajo rug contains an intentional mistake” story. An observed irregularity needs maker-specific evidence; do not invent a spiritual explanation for it.
- Do not infer a clan or ceremonial status from a rug's pattern. Nor should a seller's regional label erase the artist's identity or copyright/permission questions.

## Era staging and costume/material signature
| Historical staging lens | Material and dress anchors | Worldbuilding use / caution |
|---|---|---|
| Earlier histories; chronology uneven | Oral histories, clan histories, archaeological evidence, and colonial descriptions answer different questions. [D07 overview; B23] | Exact dates for ancestral movements or the onset of Diné weaving are not settled here `(to-verify)`; do not invent an eyewitness chronicle. |
| Spanish-colonial and subsequent pastoral transformations | B23-E relates Navajo-Churro history to sheep introduced from Spain and to later Diné breeding and land-based practice. | “Traditional” can incorporate introduced animals; do not stage wool pastoralism as unchanged from remote antiquity. |
| Nineteenth-century wearing textiles | B23-D/V document woven shoulder blankets, intertribal exchange, and biil éé’ dress construction. | Separate cut, weave, wearer, and market; a valuable blanket is not an automatic Diné chiefly insignia. |
| 1864–1868 forced removal and imprisonment | B23-H identifies Bosque Redondo exile and return under the 1868 treaty. | Coercion changes land access and livelihoods; do not aestheticize forced displacement as a convenient fashion transition. |
| Late nineteenth/early twentieth centuries | Commercial yarns, synthetic dyes, rail-linked tourism, smaller market textiles, and rug production coexist with older practices. [B23-T/V] | New colors and formats require supply routes and customers; their presence is not proof of cultural decline. |
| Twentieth-century family and trader histories | P23 describes restrictions on her mother's color choices and differences in household resources. | Regional “styles” can include unequal market power; distinguish constraint from the weaver's own preference. |
| Contemporary lifeways and art | P23 and B23 show workshops, kin-based teaching, conservation, male weavers, and digital practices. | Never freeze every person in an 1880 outfit or make modern media incompatible with tradition. |

- **Dress construction anchor:** the documented biil éé’ consists of joined panels; a sash or concho belt controls the waist. Material structure and wearer movement should inform a fictional silhouette before ornament. [B23-D]
- **Wearing anchor:** the documented shoulder blanket is draped and may be pinned; narrower-banded women's examples complicate the male-only implication of “chief.” [B23-D]
- **Specific image anchor:** B23-D reproduces an 1874 portrait of Juanita with a dress, concho belt, ornaments, and blankets; it is one documented ensemble, not a uniform for all Diné women.
- Velvet blouses, gathered skirts, jewelry chronologies, and region-specific combinations need object-level dating and Diné dress expertise before use here `(to-verify)`; no universal costume package is inferred.
- Physical dating can defeat stylistic labels: B23-V describes a purported 1850 blanket whose synthetic red dye cannot predate 1878. Stage **material availability**, **design revival**, and **collector attribution** separately.
- “First/Second/Third Phase Chief Blanket” is a collector/dealer typology, not a Diné civilizational era sequence. [B23-V]

## Clan, kʼé, introductions, and personal naming
- Start with the distinction: **kʼé is relational conduct and connectedness**; **clan affiliation is one institutional way relationships are identified**. Neither is merely a picturesque surname system. [H15; J02]
- Matrilineal affiliation does not erase paternal relationships. P23 distinguishes being born **into** the mother's clan and born **for** the father's clan.
- P23 gives her own Water Edge Clan and Two Waters Flow Together Clan affiliations; preserve her published spelling and attribution rather than manufacture a standardized Diné form from their English glosses.

| Analytical slot | Relationship to the speaker | What not to infer |
|---|---|---|
| Mother's clan | Clan into which the speaker is born. [P23, note 1] | Not interchangeable with an English legal surname. |
| Father's clan | Clan for which the speaker is born. [P23, note 1] | Not a second identical membership slot under a bilateral surname model. |
| Maternal grandfather's clan | Another publicly articulated genealogical relation. [P23, note 1] | Do not substitute the maternal grandmother's clan by guessing from English kin terms. |
| Paternal grandfather's clan | Another publicly articulated genealogical relation. [P23, note 1] | The four slots are an analytical pedigree, not the universal order of an introduction. |

- **Order calibration:** P23, final note 1, explicitly places the paternal grandfather's clan before the maternal grandfather's. Retain that testimony; exact local ordering and Diné formulae for a different audience remain `(to-verify)`, not silently “corrected.”
- That same note describes expansion to eight maternal/paternal clans for an all-Diné audience, along with place raised and a formal closing. Treat this as Pete's account, not a mandatory script for every person or occasion.
- **Two different fours:** J02 §204(B) names four foundational clans and descendant clans; P23's four relationship slots concern an individual's introduction. These are not the same classification.
- Clan histories can connect kin, place, origin, and memory; D07 explicitly argues for histories transmitted through matrilineal clans. Individual clan etymologies and origin narratives require their own sources `(to-verify)`.
- **Personal names are separate:** B23-D discusses Asdzáá Tł’ógi / Juanita and attributes “Lady Weaver” as a gloss; its wording does not license generating new Diné personal names by arbitrary noun compounding.
- Public names, English/Spanish names, clan references, and any restricted naming practices must not be collapsed. No secret-name inventory or ceremonial naming procedure is offered.
- Marriage rules, related-clan groupings, adoption, and exceptional circumstances require specialized verification `(to-verify)`; four labels alone are not a marriage-permission algorithm.
- **Interpretation:** a fictional genealogy should separately store descent affiliation, paternal ties, grandparents, residence, audience, and reciprocal duties; generate its own introduction grammar rather than use Diné clans as fantasy houses.

## What a worldbuilder can take
- **A coupling method, not a kit:** connect speech, relationships, craft teaching, material resources, historical change, and public self-representation; invent the fictional particulars independently.
- **A relational design question:** what must a speaker establish about obligations and kin before a formal encounter can proceed? Test different audiences, unknown ancestry, and diaspora without treating real Diné people as deficient cases.
- **A craft design question:** who makes tools, who teaches, who supplies fibers, who sets prices, and whose name survives in an archive? Let those answers affect language, dress, inheritance, and symbolism together.
- **A ritual design question:** how do authorization, temporary materials, specialist learning, community responsibility, and limits on display fit together? Keep the fictional system independent of actual sandpainting imagery.
- **An era design question:** which garment elements can persist while fiber sources, transport, markets, and political conditions change? A revival is not automatically a chronological error.
- **A handoff to lore-shokunin:** provide alternative coupling models, source confidence, unresolved questions, and excluded/restricted material. Do not assert fictional clan names, gods, rituals, costumes, or history as world-bible canon.

## Provenance cautions and stop rules
- H15 is a Diné-led scholarly wellness formulation, not a universal survey of all Diné religious positions, nor evidence for curing particular illnesses. Its own caution about cultural experts remains load-bearing.
- P23 is first-person expertise, not a census; B23 explicitly includes several makers and curatorial interpretations. Never average their voices into “the Navajo believe.”
- D07 challenges histories that privilege male leaders and colonial records over women's and matrilineal histories. “Sparse chronicle evidence” is not “no Diné knowledge”; oral histories need attribution and their own interpretive care.
- Museum acquisition records can contain incorrect dates, missing makers, coercive collecting histories, and obsolete racial or gender assumptions. B23-V's dye/date contradiction is a concrete warning, not a hypothetical concern.
- Do not rank natural dyes as spiritually authentic and commercial dyes as corrupted by default: P23 describes a weaver's frustrated desire for brilliant color under trader restrictions.
- Do not turn male/female cosmological language into an exhaustive contemporary gender taxonomy; B23-E documents male weaving and challenges outsider assumptions. Further gender-specific claims remain `(to-verify)`.
- Distinguish a public national emblem, a family-shared story, a market textile, and a restricted ceremonial composition; similar imagery does not make their permissions or functions identical.
- Stop at unverified etymologies, universal ritual claims, and claims of community permission. Obtain a better source or retain `(to-verify)`; neither an English gloss nor an old ethnographic plate fills the gap.
- Recheck living-source guidance and permission scope for each actual project. This is a reference-and-method entry, not cultural authorization, legal advice, a ceremonial manual, or canon.
