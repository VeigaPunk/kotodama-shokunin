## 1. Calibration and the unit of comparison

- **Established:** the empire was multilingual; Quechuan languages predate it; knotted-cord accounting and highly valued textiles are materially attested. [A04; D15; M62]
- **Supported reconstruction:** royal estates, ancestor service, solar legitimation, and ranked garment use emerge from intersecting documentary and material evidence; their precise rules vary by place and source. [D15; N99; C90; B18]
- **Debated:** the speech-encoding capacity of particular khipu traditions; a universally applicable split-inheritance rule; single-cause explanations of expansion. Treat these as models, not solved mechanisms. [H17; H21; N99; D15]
- “Inca” can designate a ruler, the Cuzco elite, an imperial style, or an empire containing many peoples. Specify which before assigning a language, garment, or belief to “the Inca.” [D15]
- Tawantinsuyu/Tahuantinsuyu conventionally means “the four parts together”; it is a political ordering claim, not proof of cultural uniformity. [D15]
- Scope here is strongest for imperial institutions and colonial descriptions of them; village-specific ritual speech, ordinary personal naming, and pre-imperial court languages are thinner.

| Analytical framework | Question it makes usable | Limit |
|---|---|---|
| Contact sociolinguistics [A04] | Who uses an administrative language, with whom, and for which tasks? | A lingua franca is not everybody's home language. |
| Murra's cloth-centered political economy [M62] | How do production, redistribution, gifts, and rank reinforce each other? | Prestige circulation is not automatically a market-money system. |
| Royal-estate / split-inheritance model [N99; D15] | Which rights remain with a dead ruler's household, and which pass with office? | An explanatory model is not an exceptionless inheritance statute. |
| Khipu philology [H17; H21] | What can an object encode, and what must a trained interpreter supply? | Colonial/community examples cannot independently decipher imperial objects. |
| Material-culture biography [B18; M06] | Can a form survive while its wearers, materials, and meanings change? | Formal resemblance alone does not establish unchanged belief. |

## 2. Language family, imperial communication, and contact

| Layer | Historical reference | Fictional design question |
|---|---|---|
| Quechuan family | A differentiated family, not a single language invented in Cuzco; Quechua I / Quechua II is a common broad classification. [A04] | Which differences predate imperial rule? |
| Imperial lingua franca | Quechua served supra-local administration and communication, without replacing all local languages. [A04; D15] | Which officials must learn it, and which communities need interpreters? |
| Other Andean languages | Aymaran languages are a distinct family; long contact with Quechuan complicates simple language–people maps. [A04] | Are shared words inherited, borrowed, or imposed by office? |
| Court-language reconstruction | Colonial accounts report a special elite language (*lengua particular*); identifying it with Puquina is a modern hypothesis built on sparse, mostly colonial documentation, and specialists disagree about it. [A04; D15; CP13; CP21; EN26] | Do not assume a securely reconstructed Puquina court register; no period-specific court vocabulary is supplied here. [EN26] |
| Colonial general languages | Missionary teaching, administration, grammars, and dictionaries reshaped the reach and standardization of indigenous languages. [A04] | Does the later written standard preserve, select, or replace earlier usage? |

- Typological reference: many Quechuan varieties are strongly suffixing and largely head-final, with rich person/possessor marking and evidential distinctions; consult a specified variety before borrowing forms. [A04, ch. 3]
- Do not make evidentiality a biological truth detector, or infer that speakers cannot lie or disagree about witnessed events.
- Do not project every feature of modern Cuzco Quechua onto the family or onto an undocumented imperial standard; aspiration and glottalization are not uniform family-wide features. [A04]
- Use separate lexicon columns for local cultivation, imperial labor assessment, court praise, and colonial religious translation; a shared everyday word need not have identical institutional meanings.
- Contact mechanism for fiction: provincial elites acquire court vocabulary while households retain local speech; titles travel farther than complete grammatical systems.
- Orthographic rule: choose a working spelling convention, retain historical variants in source notes, and never treat colonial spelling variation alone as evidence of different ancient pronunciations. [A04; D15]

## 3. Khipu: record system before a writing verdict

- Khipu / quipu: cord-based records, characteristically with a main cord, pendant cords, and sometimes subsidiary cords; materials include cotton and animal fiber. [H21]
- Many surviving examples encode numbers in a positional decimal system; cord grouping and non-numerical properties also matter. A readable number is not a complete administrative document. [H21]
- Color, fiber, twist/ply, attachment, knotting, sequence, and grouping provide possible information-bearing contrasts; do not assign each a universal dictionary value. [H17; H21]
- Khipu kamayuq / quipucamayoc denotes a specialist associated with making or interpreting records; an archive requires trained people as well as stored objects. [D15]
- Colonial accounts describe historical recitations and other non-accounting uses, but the relationship between a recitation and what the cords themselves encoded remains a separate question. [D15; H17]

| Claim | Calibration | What would establish more? |
|---|---|---|
| Knots and positions record numerical values | Established for many accounting khipus | Reproducible readings matched to object structure and context. |
| Non-numerical cord contrasts classify information | Supported; exact meanings are often context-specific | Independent correspondences across records and known categories. |
| Some khipus functioned as narrative communications | Historical testimony and particular later traditions support investigation | Demonstrate what was encoded versus supplied by shared knowledge. |
| All imperial khipus spelled Quechua sentences | Not established | A replicable sign-to-language mapping across independent imperial objects. |
| Three-dimensional objects can constitute writing | A legitimate theoretical position, dependent on the definition of writing | State whether the criterion is durable inscription, conventional meaning, or recoverable speech. |
| A complete, general-purpose imperial narrative decipherment exists | Not established by the sources used here | Require a published decoder, independent tests, and secure object dating. |

- **Hyland's Collata case:** two community-preserved, eighteenth-century khipus are described as epistles; her analysis proposes possible logosyllabic encoding and lineage-name sequences. This is a specific argument, not a decipherment of all Inca khipus. [H17; H21]
- **Later khipu boards:** cords alongside alphabetically written names could record labor and ritual obligations; their hybrid format developed after conquest. Do not place these boards unchanged in a fifteenth-century imperial office. [H21]
- A narrow grammatological definition requires encoded linguistic expression; a broader inscription approach includes conventional non-phonetic records. Keep the criterion visible rather than ranking peoples as “with” or “without civilization.”
- Craft transfer: decide what a stranger can recover from the object, what a trained keeper can recover, and what only the contributing community remembers.
- Design a test record with separate quantity, category, contributor, occasion, and authorizing witness fields; ask which fields are materially recoverable. This is an invented design exercise, not an attested khipu layout.
- Material failure modes: faded dyes, broken cord order, replacement fibers, missing subsidiary cords, and loss of trained keepers can damage different parts of a record's intelligibility.

## 4. Mythic core: solar sovereignty inside a populated landscape

| Being / institution | Court-facing reference | Coupled system and caution |
|---|---|---|
| Inti | Sun and royal ancestor; central to state cult and dynastic legitimacy. [C90; D15] | Solar descent authorizes rule; it does not erase other powers. |
| Qorikancha / Coricancha | Major Cuzco temple complex associated especially with the Sun. [C90; D15] | Architecture, precious materials, offerings, and political centrality reinforce one another. |
| Wiraqucha / Viracocha | Creator-associated figure in colonial narratives, with varying relationships to other divine figures. [C90] | Do not synthesize accounts into one uncontested theological hierarchy. |
| Mama Quilla / Mama Killa | Moon-associated divine figure in recorded court religion. [C90] | Lunar and gendered relations need a specified narrative, not a universal masculine-sun/feminine-moon law. |
| Illapa | Thunder/lightning and rain associations. [C90] | Agricultural dependence and military/royal symbolism can intersect without making every storm a war omen. |
| Pachamama | Earth-associated power within Andean ritual relationships. [C90] | Contemporary meanings and practices are not an unchanged transcript of imperial theology. |
| Wak'a / huaca | Powerful beings, places, objects, and ancestral presences; “idol” is a distorting colonial reduction. [C90; H21] | Terrain can hold claims, obligations, and agency, not merely illustrate a myth. |
| Royal ancestors | Deceased rulers remained socially consequential through bodies, attendants, property, and ceremony. [D15; N99] | Ancestor service is an economic and political institution, not decorative necromancy. |

- Origin narratives include Ayar-sibling emergence traditions and accounts centered on Manco Capac and Mama Ocllo; preserve their differing narrators and emphases rather than force one canonical sequence. [C90; D15]
- Inti Raymi belongs to a calendrical cycle of ritual obligations; for reconstruction, distinguish a colonial description from a modern staged festival. [C90]
- State religion also involved coercion and sacrifice; do not sanitize domination into harmless sun symbolism or treat violent rites as the whole religion. [D15; C90]
- Application: let the ruling dynasty sponsor a solar genealogy while a valley's water source has older ritual custodians; specify negotiations over offerings, attendance, language, and cloth.

## 5. Symbolic systems: ordering relations, not a universal decoder

| Pattern | Historically useful reading | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Four suyu | Chinchaysuyu, Antisuyu, Qullasuyu/Collasuyu, and Kuntisuyu/Contisuyu ordered the imperial domain around Cuzco. [D15] | Four identical ethnic blocs or modern borders. |
| Hanan / Hurin | Upper/lower divisions organized aspects of Cuzco's social and ritual order. [D15; C90] | Assuming every binary distinction has the same membership or hierarchy. |
| Ceque / siq'i system | Shrine relations, ritual responsibilities, and named groups organized a sacred landscape around Cuzco. [C90] | Reducing every line to a road, astronomical sightline, or territorial boundary. |
| Gold and solar display | Precious material could articulate sacred and political value. [C90; D15] | Treating ritual gold as circulating coinage. |
| Toqapu / tocapu | Bounded geometric textile motifs communicated distinction within structured designs. [B18] | A supposedly solved alphabet or a universal motif-to-word table. |
| Named terrain | Places anchor origin claims, remembered events, and obligations. [D15; H21] | Inferring a single religion from a scenic mountain image. |

- Tourist packages of “condor–puma–serpent = three worlds” or a single chakana decoder are **not an adopted historical framework here**: the Quechua term's attested senses and later resemantization are studied in their own right [AH21], and the tripartite hanan–kay–uku scheme reaches modern writing through a scholarly tradition that itself has alternatives [PG26]. Specific pre-Hispanic attestations and regional meanings still require a named source.
- That caution does not deny traditional animal symbolism or stepped motifs; it rejects an unsupported universal key.
- Craft principle: symbols work through authorized use, arrangement, audience, occasion, and material—not just a list saying “red means power.”

## 6. Era model: stage institutions, not a costume collage

| Stage | Language / record ecology | Ritual / material staging |
|---|---|---|
| Pre-imperial formation | Quechuan and Aymaran histories precede the empire; Cuzco's growth was a process, not a linguistic creation event. [A04; D15] | Distinguish inherited Andean textile skills from later imperial conventions. |
| Imperial expansion, fifteenth–early sixteenth centuries | Administrative communication spans diverse communities; specialists and resettlement connect distant populations. [A04; D15] | Estates, state-sponsored ritual, labor demands, and prestige gifts expand together. |
| Late imperial succession conflict | Competing dynastic claims affect which histories are authoritative. [D15] | Ancestor households and factions are not a single unified royal interest. |
| Spanish invasion from 1532; Cuzco seized 1533 | Interpretation and colonial transcription alter the archive's audiences and filters. [D15] | Do not date all institutional change to a single battle. |
| Neo-Inca resistance ending in 1572; colonial reorganization | Indigenous languages and khipus continue within changed power relations. [B18; H21] | Elite dress can preserve old forms while acquiring new legal and ceremonial meanings. |
| Later colonial and modern communities | Language shift may coexist with retained textile names and techniques. [M06] | Imported garments, synthetic yarn, Christian practice, and local invention can become tradition. [B18; M06] |

- Pachakuti's conventional accession date of 1438 is a historical reconstruction, not an archaeologically exact start for all imperial institutions; individual reign dates and expansion sequences require calibration. [D15]
- Keep an internal institutional chronology beside archaeological period labels: conquest of a province, arrival of a weaving workshop, and adoption of an administrative language need not coincide.
- A present-day pollera, bowler hat, or performance costume is not automatically evidence for an imperial wardrobe. Modern indigenous identities are living histories, not failed preservation. [B18]

## 7. Costume and material signature: cumbi is not simply money

- Cumbi / qumpi / qompi: spellings associated with highly valued fine cloth. Do not confuse **qumpi**, fine cloth, with **chumpi**, a belt; translations and historical spellings need attention. [M62; M06]
- Murra treats cloth as central to state power, obligations, rewards, and social differentiation. “Currency” is useful only if its particular function is specified. [M62]
- Keep **prestige wealth**, **transferable payment/gift**, **unit of account**, and **general medium of exchange** separate; the first two do not establish the last two.
- Fine cloth was not merely “Inca banknotes”: garment quality, recipient, producer, occasion, and rights of use could matter as much as quantity. [M62; B18]
- Do not infer that all exchange in every province was state redistribution, or that valuable textiles imply a uniform cloth-price schedule. [D15; M62]

| Object / process | Material-social signature | Craft implication |
|---|---|---|
| Unku / uncu | Male tunic; basic form and cloth quality must be distinguished. [B18] | Rank can change through weave and ornament without a radically different cut. |
| Aqsu / anaku, lliqlla, chumpi, tupu | Wrapped garment, mantle, belt, and fastening pin in women's dress descriptions; terminology varies regionally. [B18] | Model the assembly and fastening system, not just an outfit silhouette. |
| Maskaypacha / mascapaicha | Royal forehead fringe, subsequently reinterpreted in colonial elite display. [B18] | The same visible sign can shift from singular sovereignty to lineage/class distinction. |
| Cotton and camelid fibers | Region, access, technique, and intended use influence cloth; “all wool, all alpaca” is too simple. [M62; B18] | Connect herding and cultivation to specialist vocabulary and supply routes. |
| Fine-cloth production | Specialized labor, including chosen women and other weavers, supported state institutions. [M62; M06] | Separate gendered work arrangements from the claim that only one gender ever wove. |
| Tapestry and other fine weaves | Tapestry is important, but Meisch's belt discussion cautions against making it the exclusive definition of qumpi. [M06] | Specify structure, technique, and quality independently. |
| Gifts and offerings | Cloth moved through political rewards, ceremonial transfers, and sacred use. [M62] | A transfer may create allegiance or acknowledge status rather than settle an open-market price. |

- Labor levy, finished-cloth obligation, and redistributive gift are different transactions; do not translate every one as a cash “tax.” [M62; D15]
- Meisch's northern Peruvian sara-belt case links an early colonial weaving description with later techniques, names, and interpretation; she explicitly identifies unresolved color meanings and distinguishes fabric structure from production technique. [M06]
- Use that as a coupling method: document what survives independently—name, weave, motif, wearer, material, ritual role—rather than claim that survival of one proves survival of all.
- Fictional design option: issue prestigious cloth in graded allotments, require witnessed transfers, and restrict some patterns to officeholders. These rules must be invented explicitly, not attributed wholesale to Tawantinsuyu.

## 8. Split inheritance and political linguistics

- **Conventional model:** the successor acquired rulership, while a deceased ruler's estate and household resources remained associated with that ruler and his descendants/servitors, sustaining ancestor service. [N99; D15]
- **Panaka / panaca:** a royal kin-group term used in reconstructions of these institutions; do not equate it without qualification to a European surname, clan, ministry, or private corporation. [D15]
- The model helps explain separate royal estates and a successor's incentive to establish his own resources; it does **not** prove that every conquest was compelled by inheritance. [N99; D15]
- Avoid “eldest son automatically inherits the throne”: succession involved political eligibility, alliances, and competition; property arrangements and succession selection are distinct questions. [D15]
- Modern “private ownership” language can mislead: distinguish rights to labor, produce, residence, ritual custody, and representation before deciding what an estate-holder owned. [N99; D15]
- Calibration: reconstructed practice is not an imperial law code; consistency across reigns and the model's causal weight in expansion remain open to examination.

| Institutional coupling | Historical anchor | Fictional linguistic mechanism—not an attested Quechua rule |
|---|---|---|
| Office versus dynastic person | Rulership and ancestor-associated resources were distinguishable. [N99] | Separate the title of current office from personal and estate names in legal formulae. |
| Speech on behalf of the dead | Royal ancestors acted socially through attendants and descendants. [D15] | Require a named speaker and witnesses whenever an ancestor's household makes a claim. |
| Rival histories | Royal kin groups preserved accounts favoring their ancestors. [D15] | Maintain competing praise names, genealogical recitations, and authorized versions. |
| Materialized representation | Bodies, residences, gifts, and ceremonies sustained royal presence. [D15; N99] | Make the right to display a garment or carry a record part of authorization to speak. |
| Provincial negotiation | Imperial institutions worked through unequal relations with local groups. [D15] | Distinguish a translated demand, a local obligation, and a court-approved record of assent. |

- This is **political pragmatics**, not a claim that split inheritance caused Quechua morphology or evidentiality.
- For each fictional transaction, list speaker, represented person/office, entitled audience, property right, material token, and rival interpretation; a grammar alone cannot resolve institutional disputes.

## 9. Naming practices and controlled borrowing

- Source spellings vary: Pachakuti/Pachacuti, Atawallpa/Atahualpa, and Yupanki/Yupanqui are not automatically different people. Preserve source forms alongside a normalized index. [D15]
- Royal records distinguish given names, titles, and names used as rulers; D'Altroy's conventional king list is a usable comparison, not a universal personal-name template. [D15, table 1.1]
- Qhapaq/Capac, Inka/Inca, and Yupanki/Yupanqui recur in elite naming; do not mechanically concatenate translated honorifics to generate ordinary villagers' names. [D15]
- Sapa Inka is a sovereign title, not a personal surname; Mama and Qoya/Coya also need status and context rather than indiscriminate use as female-name decorations. [D15; M06]
- Layer place names and estate names separately from personal names; a name's current spelling or apparent Quechua segmentation does not establish its original language. [A04]
- A proposed etymology of a particular royal or place name is a hypothesis until a historical-linguistic source supports it, not a datum; [EN26] catalogues the usual failure modes (post-hoc semantic claims, unmotivated correspondences, isolated etymologies corroborated by no wider pattern), and even the name *Inca* has competing specialist etymologies [CP21]. Avoid folk translations assembled from a modern dictionary.
- Fiction workflow: specify ordinary naming, office titles, accession names, ancestor-house labels, and colonial transcription as separate systems; invent their forms within the fictional language.

## 10. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Uneven integration:** one administrative language can connect a diverse empire without producing one religion, ethnicity, or wardrobe.
2. **Distributed archives:** a record can combine durable material distinctions, specialist training, public testimony, and ritual authorization; specify the contribution of each.
3. **Wearable political economy:** cloth production, recipient status, sacred offerings, and labor obligations can form one institutional circuit rather than separate decorative categories.
4. **Plural sacred sovereignty:** a dynasty's celestial ancestor can coexist uneasily with powerful local places and their custodians.
5. **Ancestors with resources:** continued ancestor households generate disputes over representation, property, commemoration, and the versions of history that get repeated.
6. **Asynchronous eras:** imperial styles, local languages, colonial legal titles, and inherited weaving techniques can change at different speeds.
7. Handoff to lore-shokunin: supply sourced reference, confidence, causal mechanism, explicitly invented alternatives, and a contradiction checklist; leave adoption and canon to lore.
