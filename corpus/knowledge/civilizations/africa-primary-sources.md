## 1. Scope: a source landscape, not “the African tradition”
- “Sub-Saharan” is a coverage label here, not a language family, cultural unit, or boundary isolating the Sahara and Indian Ocean. Use a named locality, language/variety, institution, genre, and date for each actual claim.
- The examples span West Africa, Cameroon, coastal East Africa, and southern Africa; they do not exhaust any of those regions. No cross-continent grammar, pantheon, costume, or social hierarchy is inferred.
- **Primary witness:** a particular manuscript, utterance, performance recording, object, excavation context, or administrative document. Its evidentiary value depends on the question, not simply the creator’s identity.
- **Mediating record:** a catalogue, transcript, translation, edition, museum description, field report, or project page. These provide access and interpretations; do not silently substitute them for the witness.
- An excavation report records the excavators’ observations and interpretations; the underlying deposits are a different evidence layer. A colonial report is primary for an official’s statement, not automatically for the people described.
- Working method: preserve **attestation / interpretation / comparison / design-only transformation** as separate fields, following `../culture-material/material-culture-inference-method.md` and the citation profile.
- Do not replace the colonial chronicle with an equally privileged “pure oral voice.” The copying, commissioning, collecting, recording, and translating histories remain evidence [RAI; VA; SING; BLL-H].

## 2. Arabic-script Ajami: manuscripts with identifiable custody
| Route / witness | Language, genre, and access layer | What it supports; limit |
|---|---|---|
| BU NEH Ajami galleries [AJ] | Hausa, Fula, Mandinka, Wolof; selected manuscript images, Latin-script transcriptions, translations, and recorded readings | A comparative entry point across four traditions; the selection is not the complete literature of any language |
| EAP1042, Casamance [MAND] | Mandinka Ajami; project describes religious, didactic, commercial, genealogical, medical, and local-history materials | A genre-search map; distinguish planned preservation from completed item-level access |
| EAP1042/32/5 [MAND-I] | Bilingual Arabic–Mandinka miscellany including correspondence, notes, poem, and divination material | The catalogue explicitly requires dual literacy; do not call the entire item either monolingual Arabic or one coherent treatise |
| EAP334/1 [WOL-I] | Wolofal manuscript, catalogued as likely written in the 1980s from its contents; incomplete and damaged | Ajami is not only an ancient archive; an inferred content-based date remains an inference |
| HMML’s Malian partners [MALI] | Family collections in Timbuktu, Djenné, and other locations; Arabic, Fulfulde, Bambara, and other local languages are listed | Search by library and actual item language; “Timbuktu manuscript” does not itself mean Ajami |
| Swahili witnesses in the Allen collection [RAI, pp. 65–71] | Arabic-script Swahili verse; manuscripts, typescripts, copies, and colophons in the Dar es Salaam discussion | A demonstrable copying network and textual variants; the article is the inspected layer, not a fresh reading of its witnesses |

- **Ajami identifies a writing practice:** non-Arabic languages written in modified Arabic scripts. Arabic-language scholarship and Ajami can coexist in one collection or document [AJ; MAND-I].
- Separate `language | script/conventions | hand | genre | intended reader | oral realization`. Sharing an Arabic-derived script does not establish a language family or one standardized orthography [AJ; RAI].
- Start from the image, then identify whose transcription, vocalization, and translation are supplied [AJ]. Treat a modern recitation as a dated reading event; it does not by itself establish the original author’s pronunciation or delivery (entry-local evidence rule).
- Raia’s colophon discussion distinguishes copyist and owner from author; their names cannot be collapsed into a single “creator” field [RAI, pp. 69–70]. Preserve those roles in any naming-system comparison.
- Manuscript age, narrated event, later copy, and digitization are different dates. The modern Wolofal witness and Swahili copy chains make an “all manuscripts are medieval” default untenable [WOL-I; RAI].
- For damaged or incomplete witnesses, state the missing pages and unreadable area before reconstructing a phrase. EAP1042/32/5 records water damage and ink staining; EAP334/1 records missing pages [MAND-I; WOL-I].
- A digitized family library does not thereby transfer ownership or cultural authority to the digitizer. Record original custodian, digital custodian, and any supplied access conditions separately [MALI; CARE].

## 3. Other written and spoken archives: avoid an Ajami-only map
| Route | Primary material reached | Linguistic use and guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Bamum Script and Archives Project, EAP051 [BAM] | Palace-held manuscripts and digital copies; the report describes early script variants and cataloguing | A distinct script archive, not an Ajami collection; log the witness’s script stage rather than normalizing it silently |
| Digital Bleek and Lloyd [BLL] | Southern African notebooks, lexical records, drawings, photographs, correspondence; the description identifies ǀxam and !kun material among its holdings | Link a form to a notebook/page and recorder’s conventions; do not merge distinct languages under the old catalogue’s umbrella labels |
| ELAR collection 0836 [SANYE] | Sanye documentation in Kenya, catalogued under “Dahalo”; conversations, narratives, songs, interviews, and ethnographic discussion | Description reports Swahili explanations alongside Sanye speech and incomplete annotation; identify which language each tier actually represents |
| ELAR discovery portal [ELAR] | Audiovisual language-documentation collections and associated metadata | A route to speaker-attributed bundles; catalogue visibility does not guarantee unrestricted access to every recording |

- Keep endonym, catalogue label, language identifier, and administrative label in separate fields. The Sanye description explicitly discusses a preference differing from its catalogue heading [SANYE].
- Historical spelling is an attestation, not an automatic phonetic transcription. Preserve the source form and transcription scheme; require a phonological analysis before deriving pronunciation (`../linguistics/historical-linguistics.md`).
- Elicited word lists, conversation, prayer, epic, and metalinguistic explanation sample different registers. A specialist performance cannot stand in for everyday grammar (`../linguistics/sociolinguistics-registers.md`).
- Southern African notebook material is not a transparent primordial worldview: UCT’s finding aid documents Bleek’s access to prisoners and later custodial arrangements [BLL-H, Wilhelm Bleek biography]. This constraint must accompany, not erase, the named speakers’ testimony.
- In a usable linguistic packet require `speaker/authorized attribution | variety | date/place | genre | source locator | original form | transcription/gloss | translator | restriction`. This is a lane checklist, not an assertion that every listed archive supplies every field.

## 4. Oral epics and recording projects: retain the event
| Project / witness route | What the inspected description identifies | What must remain separate |
|---|---|---|
| Verba Africana: “Épopée de Soundjata (Sunjata Epic)” [VA, item 3] | A filmed example associated with the Kéla version and particular narrative episodes | This described performance, other Sunjata versions, and the medieval events narrated |
| Singing Storytellers / Sunjata Project [SING] | Hawa Kassé Mady Diabaté’s singing, Lassana Diabaté’s balafon, Chérif Keita’s poetic English rendering, Lucy Durán’s production, and subsequent films | Live performance, narrative paraphrase, filmed edit, exhibition excerpt, and later published project account |
| Verba Africana’s adjacent genres [VA, items 4–16] | Taarab, ngoma, Ewe migration accounts, and Karamojong tales/songs | These are not all epics; a comparative archive is not evidence of one pan-African genre |

- The inspected Verba Africana descriptions explicitly label several ngoma recordings as planned performances rather than recordings during their associated rituals [VA, items 6–9]. Reenactment is a provenance field, not a reason to discard the recording.
- Ostashewski describes the Cape Breton Sunjata production as deliberately concise and explains that Keita’s spoken contribution is poetic rendering/paraphrase rather than direct translation [SING, “Poetic translation and paraphrase”; translation discussion]. Do not treat its English text as a word-aligned linguistic transcript.
- A recording date anchors what was performed then. It does not establish when every episode, formula, instrument, or garment first entered the tradition; historical inference needs other dated witnesses.
- **Performance comparison checklist:** keep narrator/singer, accompanist, responder/audience, patron or commission, place, occasion, language, recorder, translator, editor, and circulation permissions visible.
- Preserve a timestamped passage together with response, repetition, instrumental contribution, and omitted segments where these matter. A prose plot summary cannot replace evidence about timing, participation, or register.
- Variant is not automatically corruption. Ask whether an omission or emphasis follows audience, length, family repertoire, political setting, or editorial choice; test the explanation rather than supplying a universal rule [SING; RAI as a separate textual comparison].
- Do not use a modern performance alone to identify a medieval ruler’s exact words, historical religious consensus, or an unchanged social rank system. The sources establish performances and reported traditions, not those broader propositions.
- Living testimony has attribution and circulation conditions. Do not reproduce restricted ritual texts or intimate biographies merely because an older collector printed related material [ELAR; SANYE; CARE].

## 5. Archaeological and material evidence: context before interpretation
| Evidence route | Inspectable or discoverable material | Supported question; unsupported leap |
|---|---|---|
| Songo Mnara project [SM] | Spatial survey, excavation, soil chemistry, botanical remains, ceramics, coins, fauna | How activities were distributed; an imported object does not name a resident’s language or ancestry |
| Songo Mnara 2011 report [SM11] | Recorded excavation units, house deposits, open spaces, and burial observations | A particular field season’s context and interpretations; pending analyses are not final results |
| Jenné-jeno / Hambarketolo / Kaniana report route [JEN] | Catalogue lists stratigraphy, pottery, other materials, fauna, and botanical/osteological chapters | Where to seek primary Malian excavation evidence; the catalogue summary is not a substitute for the report’s tables or dating arguments |
| Digital Benin catalogue and provenance [BEN] | Dispersed objects, institutional records, images, and linked provenance names | Object biography and collection history; an accession date is not a manufacture date or excavated context |
| Digital Benin’s Edo-language and oral-history routes [BEN] | Object designations, use/production explanations, and narrated knowledge | Compare institutional labels with locally grounded accounts; present explanations do not automatically date every earlier use |
| Bleek–Lloyd drawings and photographs [BLL] | Attributed visual records alongside notebooks | Depicted or drawn forms with a creator/context; not a universal costume chart or a key to all rock imagery |

- Do not equate preserved monumental buildings with the whole settlement: the Songo Mnara report uses geophysical and artifact-distribution evidence to investigate earthen buildings and open-space activity as well as stonehouses [SM11, “Geophysical Survey”; “Open Spaces”].
- Worked anchor: the report records a coin-and-carnelian deposit beneath the floor of SM017 and shell objects in SM023, then suggests structured deposition connected with houses [SM11, “House Excavations,” p. 6].
- **Observation:** reported material, location, and association. **Interpretation:** a proposed meaning of deliberate placement. **Not established:** the words spoken, a named household deity, or the language used by the depositors.
- A garment claim needs evidence for its actual dimension: fibers/technique, cut, pictured wearing, lexical designation, and social restriction are different questions (`../culture-material/textile-technology.md`). Beads alone do not reconstruct a complete dress ensemble.
- A material/word coupling must date the object context and the lexical witness separately. Trade in an object is not proof that its present-day name travelled with it (`../linguistics/language-contact.md`).
- Keep laboratory determination, calibrated estimate, archaeological phase, and historical period label distinct. Do not synchronize sound changes, cult changes, and clothing changes to one ceramic boundary (`../culture-material/era-periodization-models.md`).
- No new date, dynasty, artifact attribution, or ethnic assignment is established by this inventory. The Jenné-jeno catalogue route especially requires inspection of the underlying report before a load-bearing material-history claim.

## 6. Colonial archives: biases to test, not a blanket rejection
| Filter | Evidence anchor / diagnostic question | Required handling |
|---|---|---|
| Administrative purpose | CO correspondence, legislation, and statistical returns are distinct record genres [CO, §4] | Record the official purpose and speaker; a rule is not proof of compliance |
| Unequal institutional coverage | TNA mainly holds central-government records; many local administrative and court records remained elsewhere [CO, §§2, 7] | Do not interpret a failed London search as a continent-wide absence |
| Retention and destruction | Registers can identify correspondence destroyed under statute [CO, §6.2] | Distinguish no record located / record once listed / surviving text inspected |
| Relocation of records | FCO 141 contains records transferred from former colonies [CO, §9] | Trace originating office and custody, not merely present shelf location |
| Selection and duplication | Confidential Print collated selected correspondence [CO, §4] | A printed extract and its source letter are not two independent witnesses |
| Bureaucratic categories | Does “tribe,” “chief,” “custom,” or a census heading reproduce an administrative classification rather than an actor’s own description? | Retain original label with context; do not promote it to an intrinsic cultural unit |
| Interpreter and copyist invisibility | Raia identifies poets, scribes, agents, and women custodians behind collected Swahili texts [RAI, pp. 66–70] | Credit contributors; a European collector’s catalogue heading is not sole authorship |
| Coercive collection setting | Prison access and custody feature in the Bleek finding aid [BLL-H] | Carry conditions into interpretation; neither unmediated voice nor automatic fabrication is warranted |
| Dispersal and harmful metadata | Digital Benin describes 1897 looting/dispersal and warns about derogatory colonial catalogue terms [BEN, “About”; “Sensitive Content”] | Separate object description, acquisition claim, and quoted harmful label; do not reproduce the label as neutral terminology |
| Search and translation loss | Which spellings, exonyms, former territory names, and translated genre labels were searched? | Log variants and missing linguistic competence before declaring a source tradition absent |

- These are reading tests. They do not establish that every colonial sentence is false, that every community account agrees, or that a locally authored manuscript is free of patronage and selection.
- Check official claims against local-language texts, named testimony, object biographies, and archaeological contexts where those address the same question. Independence depends on different witnesses, not different websites.
- Do not silently repair an official’s wording or convert an English “king,” “priest,” or “clan” into a verified local semantic category. Preserve the original term, translator, and context before comparison.

## 7. Minimum evidence before a source becomes a design premise
| Proposed premise | Minimum check | Stop condition |
|---|---|---|
| Pronunciation or grammatical form | Dated/located attestation plus transcription conventions and appropriate linguistic analysis | Image, spelling, or English translation alone |
| Ajami orthographic convention | Particular witness and hand, language identification, editorial/transliteration explanation | Assuming Arabic spelling or another language’s convention applies unchanged |
| Epic episode or formula | Named performance/version, timestamp or transcript locator, language and translator | Only an unattributed prose retelling |
| Narrative as history | Dating argument and independent evidence relevant to the particular event | Treating the recording date as the event date |
| Named object’s function | Object identifier/context plus evidence for that use and its date | Museum type label or modern gloss alone |
| Garment and social rank | Dated material/image evidence plus contextual evidence for the rank association | Elite depiction generalized to everyone |
| Trade-driven lexical borrowing | Separate object chronology, linguistic comparison, and plausible contact pathway | A foreign-looking object or word treated as the whole argument |
| A population-wide belief | Multiple located witnesses and attention to genre, disagreement, and social reach | One court, missionary, performer, or collector made representative |

1. Define one claim and its scale: a person, institution, town, language variety, or time horizon—not “Africa.”
2. Locate the witness and identify what was actually consulted: image, recording, transcript, catalogue, translation, or later study.
3. Capture `source ID | item/page/time/context | creator roles | language/script | genre | place | custody | restrictions`.
4. Separate event/content date, witness-production date, copy/recording date, and edition/digitization date; retain qualifiers such as the catalogue’s “likely.”
5. Label each assertion as observed/reported, interpreted, compared, or design-only; list what the source cannot establish.
6. Check dependencies and one relevant alternative witness; shared source letters, copies, or recordings do not independently corroborate one another.
7. Record a missing check as an explicit research gap; do not assert an uncertain historical detail merely to complete a row.
8. Route source authentication or deeper discovery to chousa-shokunin; keep this lane’s output to linguistic-cultural coupling and its evidence packet.
9. Apply the handoff contract only after the source limits and proposed fictional transformation are visible; historical confidence never supplies canon authority.

## 8. Transfer mechanisms, not borrowed identities
- **Script/register ecology:** design-only option—give a fictional institution different writing and recitation practices, with a defined readership and copying economy. [AJ; RAI] supply comparative mechanisms, not words, sacred texts, or a ready-made religion.
- **Variant-aware memory:** design-only option—allow narrators to select episodes for different audiences, then record who authorized an edition. [SING] grounds the possibility of adaptation, not a universal law of oral societies.
- **Material/lexical asymmetry:** design-only option—let the object record and the written vocabulary have different survival histories. Do not fill the resulting gap by assigning a real culture’s deity, costume, or etymology.
- **Uneven archives:** design-only option—show how court selection, family custody, household debris, and a later collector produce partially overlapping records. Mechanisms can be compared without merging the traditions represented here.
- A useful coupling record therefore says `reference mechanism | bounded source claim | proposed fictional change | rejected inference | evidence still needed | NON-CANON`. It does not rename a real people and export their institutions wholesale.

## 9. Calibration and honest gaps
- **Established at the inspected level:** the named manuscript/recording routes and their reported scopes [AJ; MAND-I; WOL-I; MALI; BAM; VA; BLL; SANYE]; Songo Mnara’s published field observations and explicitly provisional interpretations [SM; SM11]; Digital Benin’s stated catalogue/provenance scope [BEN]; TNA’s described record arrangements [CO]. This is bounded source-map confidence, not certification of all contents.
- **Mainstream methodological safeguards used here:** language/script separation, version and genre control, context before material interpretation, and separation of witness from edition (`../linguistics/writing-systems.md`; the lane’s citation and material-inference entries). The tables operationalize these safeguards rather than propose a universal theory of cultures.
- **Contested or interpretation-dependent:** the historicity of individual epic episodes, meanings of particular deposits, community-wide representativeness, and reconstructions from damaged text. No verdict on any specific unresolved episode or reading is asserted here.
- **Rejected defaults:** Africa lacked written sources; all Arabic-script material is Arabic; every performance is an unchanged medieval transcript; imported goods identify foreign founders; the colonial file or museum catalogue is a neutral census of a culture. The counterexamples and evidence limits above defeat these shortcuts, not every narrower historical claim.
- **Heuristic only:** minimum-source checks and fictional archive-ecology mechanisms. Passing a checklist does not settle truth, cultural permission, or artistic quality.
- **Uninspected:** the actual Ajami manuscripts, oral-performance videos, ELAR recordings, and individual museum objects. Catalogue descriptions are not presented as direct linguistic readings; no new translation, reconstruction, or motif equivalence is offered.
- **Geographic frontier:** detailed Horn/Geʽez, Great Lakes, Congo-basin, southern African excavation, and Malagasy source inventories are not supplied. The selected routes do not close the entire sub-Saharan source frontier.
- **Other missing work:** item-level dating/authentication, performance-by-performance consent, national/community archive holdings beyond the examples, detailed phonological histories, and object-specific textile evidence. These gaps are not silently replaced by colonial summaries.
- **Permission boundary:** CARE is an ethical framework; archive access, image licensing, community authority, and permission to adapt are separate checks [CARE; ELAR; SANYE]. No rights clearance or community endorsement is claimed.
