## Scope and calibration

- Scope: principally Balinese Hindu speech and ritual communities; neither every resident of Bali nor every community called Bali Aga shares one system. Regional, lineage, occupational, generational, and institutional differences are load-bearing.
- **Established description** below means supported for the documented setting; **interpretive model** means a scholarly explanation, not a native rule; **craft transfer** means an original design procedure, not a claim about Bali.
- Central coupling: a person can belong to a household, temple congregation, irrigation association, and performing group simultaneously. Their obligations intersect without those organizations being identical. [Lansing and Kremer 1993; Huang and Rockwell 2023.]
- Do not posit an ancient master code from which speech levels, all cloth patterns, every irrigation date, and every musical form mechanically descend.

## Language family, writing, and the social grammar

- Balinese is Austronesian, within Malayo-Polynesian; avoid treating Sanskrit vocabulary or a Brahmi-derived script as evidence that the language is Indo-Aryan. Finer subgrouping is not needed for this craft comparison. [Arka 2003; Unicode 2025.]
- *Basa Bali* is the local language label; *bahasa Bali* is Indonesian. *Alus* and *andap* name distinctions within Balinese, not two unrelated languages.
- Aksara Bali is a Brahmic abugida with inherent vowels, vowel signs, and consonant conjuncts; it descends through the regional Kawi script tradition. It can also write Old Javanese/Kawi and Sanskrit. Script, language, literary register, and religious authority are separate variables. [Unicode 2025, §§17.3, 17.9.]
- *Lontar* names palm-leaf manuscript books/material practice, not a single holy book or a language. Musical signs and sacred *modre* signs connect writing to performance and ritual without making every written character sacred in the same way. [Unicode 2025, §17.3.]
- **Framework:** Arka’s social predicates/pragmatic structure track social information attached to expressions alongside syntax and meaning. Model speaker, addressee, and people referred to separately; honorific reference is not exhausted by being polite to the listener. [Arka 2005.]

| Lexical distinction | Documented function | Fictional design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| *Andap* | Ordinary/non-deferential vocabulary, distinct from explicitly coarse vocabulary in Suwija’s classification | Give intimates usable everyday speech; “low” need not mean hostile |
| *Alus singgih* | Honors an elevated referent | A verb can honor an absent person as well as the addressee |
| *Alus sor* | Humble/deferential reference to oneself or a lower-positioned participant | Do not honor oneself merely by selecting the grandest synonym |
| *Alus mider* | Refined words usable in both honoring and humbling constructions | Some polite vocabulary is shared, not assigned to one social direction |
| *Mider* | Words without alternative level forms in the given classification | Not every noun needs a full honorific paradigm |
| *Alus madia* | Intermediate lexical forms | Allow interactional accommodation instead of only two switches |
| *Kasar* | Coarse/harsh vocabulary in Suwija’s scheme | Distinguish intentional insult from familiar conversation |

- Table basis: Suwija 2017, pp. 16–19. These are lexical classes, not seven impermeable speech communities. Terminology differs: Arka 2003 initially uses *kasar/alus* as coarse two-register shorthand; do not silently equate every author’s categories.
- Hereditary *wangsa*, age, religious office, professional position, intimacy, and occasion can create competing claims to deference. Suwija explicitly discusses modern office as well as descent; reduce neither to a simple occupation-to-caste lookup.
- Craft transfer: annotate dialogue with `speaker → addressee; actor/referent; relationship; venue; intended stance`. Test a commoner elder, a younger titled official, and a ritual specialist in changing venues before inventing pronoun tables.
- A robe or title may suggest how someone expects to be addressed; it does not make that expectation uncontested. Specific real Balinese sentence choices stay `(to-verify)` until a speaker check; do not fabricate “authentic” dialogue.

## Naming practices: several identifiers, not one surname slot

- C. Geertz’s person-label analysis distinguishes personal names, birth-order labels, kin terms, teknonyms, and status titles. Use this as a framework for multiple social identifications, not a claim that Balinese people lack individuality. [C. Geertz 1973, chapter 14.]

| Channel | Common reference pattern | Guardrail |
|---|---|---|
| Birth order | Wayan, Made, Nyoman, Ketut are familiar first-through-fourth labels | Variants and repetition beyond four vary; not a complete population naming algorithm |
| Gender marker | *I* and *Ni* occur as male/female prefixes in many names | Do not require these exact prefixes across all title groups |
| Descent/status title | *Ida Bagus*, *Ida Ayu*, *Anak Agung*, *Cokorda*, *Gusti* occur in differentiated title traditions | Title is not proof of present occupation, wealth, priestly initiation, or personal beliefs |
| Personal name | An additional individual identifier can accompany other components | A final word is not automatically an inherited family surname |
| Teknonym | “Father/mother of …,” later grandparental reference, identifies someone through descendants | Keep address forms separate from official recorded names |

- Naming evidence: C. Geertz 1973, chapter 14 records local variation in these birth-order labels and the restart of the cycle after the fourth child; Suwija 2017 supplies contemporary name/title examples. Record a specific village's variant set from local informants before direct representation.
- Craft transfer: store `recorded name`, `household address`, `ritual title`, and `public honorific` independently. A plot can change one without changing the others; lore decides whether the proposed system enters canon.

## Mythic core and symbolic organization

- Balinese Hindu traditions include Śaiva and other Hindu materials, Buddhist inheritances, local sacred places, and ancestor relationships. “A preserved Indian pantheon” and “a primitive animism beneath civilization” are both inadequate period models. [Huang and Rockwell 2023, pp. 175–177; H. Geertz 2004.]
- Siwa/Śiva, Wisnu/Viṣṇu, and Brahma appear in ritual and narrative traditions; their relation to particular temple deities, ancestors, and supreme divinity cannot be replaced by a universal family tree.
- Dewi Danu/water-goddess traditions and Dewi Sri/rice associations connect subsistence with divine relationships. A deity’s domain is not a bureaucratic monopoly over every instance of that substance. Names, identifications, and narrated genealogies vary by temple community and remain `(to-verify)` for the community being written. [Lansing and Kremer 1993; Hauser-Schäublin et al. 1991.]
- A performance-origin narrative reported through I Nyoman Sedana describes divine performers restoring order through *wayang*. Huang and Rockwell discuss its renewed use after the 2002 bombings: myth is an actionable resource in new crises, not merely an ancient plot summary. [Huang and Rockwell 2023, p. 182.]

| Named scheme | Coupled domains | Calibration and misuse to avoid |
|---|---|---|
| *Sekala/niskala* | Tangible/seen and unseen dimensions; offering, sound, place, efficacy | Established terms with varied explanations; not simply secular “reality” versus imaginary “superstition” |
| *Bhuana alit/bhuana agung* | Human microcosm and larger cosmos | Documented practitioner comparison; do not derive every garment fold from one universal body map |
| *Kaja/kelod* | Mountainward/seaward orientation, spatial valuation, movement | Topographic frame defined by the locally sacred mountain, not a fixed island grid: south Bali reads kaja as north and north Bali reverses it, and eastern-Bali villages reorient kaja to local peaks such as Bisbis/Seraya [Putra et al. 2020] |
| Threefold and ninefold spatial schemes | Ranked zones/worlds; center and surrounding directions | Documented cosmological resources; distinguish *tri loka*, *tri mandala*, and *sanga mandala* rather than using the names interchangeably |
| *Poleng* | Checked cloth marking bodies, trees, shrines, or charged locations | Pattern is not a fiber or weave type; exact protective meaning and use depend on context |

- Symbolic sources: Huang and Rockwell 2023, especially pp. 177–184; Hauser-Schäublin et al. 1991; Putra et al. 2020 documents village-level variation in the kaja/kelod and kangin/kauh axes. Detailed directional deity/color tables and any community’s explanation of *rwa bhineda* remain `(to-verify)`; black/white must not become a universal white-good/black-evil cipher.
- **Interpretive versus historical claim:** *Tri Hita Karana* links divine, social, and environmental relationships in modern discourse. Scholarship situates its named formulation in post-independence reform, particularly the 1960s; older institutions do not establish the same antiquity for the slogan. [Roth and Sedana 2015, through Huang and Rockwell 2023, pp. 175–177.]
- UNESCO’s description of an “ancient” THK philosophy is therefore calibrated as heritage rhetoric, not repeated as a settled medieval textual attestation. Modern formulation is neither evidence of fraud nor proof that all underlying relationships were newly invented.

## Calendars: intersecting clocks, not one festival year

| Clock | What it organizes | Coupling and design check |
|---|---|---|
| *Pawukon* | A 210-day cycle, thirty seven-day *wuku*, with intersecting shorter week counts | Recurrent ritual identities; many temple anniversaries return before a solar year has passed |
| *Saka* calendar | Lunisolar months and lunar observances; Nyepi belongs to this annual framework | Lunar/seasonal relations cannot be computed by treating 210 days as a solar year |
| Cultivation schedule | Crop variety, crop stage, rainfall, water allocation, pest management | Timing must remain responsive to fields and catchments, not merely a festival’s recurrence |
| Gregorian/civic calendar | Modern administration, schooling, employment, tourism | Ritual obligations can collide with work schedules without either calendar disappearing |

- Calendar basis: C. Geertz 1973; Lansing and Kremer 1993; Huang and Rockwell 2023. Many odalan follow pawukon; annual/lunar temple schedules also need distinguishing. The exact rule for a named temple is `(to-verify)` before dating its festival.
- *Odalan/piodalan* is a temple’s recurring anniversary celebration, not an island-wide holiday shared by every temple. It brings together worship, communal contributions, sociability, and performance. [Dibia 1985, through Huang and Rockwell 2023, pp. 178–181.]
- Do not turn “six months” in an ethnographic paraphrase into six Gregorian months: identify the underlying cycle. Nor should a calendar program implement every Balinese week count as an independent `day modulo n`; the actual calendrical rules require separate verification.
- Craft transfer: define a rite’s recurrence, preparation period, duration, and scheduling authority separately. A repeat interval says nothing by itself about how long the festival lasts or who can postpone preparations.

## Subak: calendar–water–myth coupling with an ecological constraint

- A *subak* is an irrigation association, not simply “the village.” Its members and temple links follow irrigation relationships and can cross settlement boundaries. Rules and coordination concern shared water, maintenance, labor, cropping, and ritual obligations. [Lansing and Kremer 1993; Brooks et al. 2017/2018.]
- Material chain: source/water intake → weir/tunnel/canal → terraces → downstream users. Organizational and sacred links overlap this chain without reproducing a single state administrative chart.

| Coordination choice | Ecological benefit | Cost or failure pressure |
|---|---|---|
| Everyone plants on one date | Broad synchronization can interrupt pest habitat through shared fallow | Peak irrigation demand can exceed supply |
| Each grower plants independently | Individual timing can spread water demand | Continuous nearby rice habitat can sustain pests |
| Neighboring groups coordinate, with appropriate offsets | Can balance shared fallow against water availability | Requires negotiation, monitoring, maintenance, and adaptation |

- This is the Lansing–Kremer model’s trade-off, not a guarantee of universal optimality. Their paper describes actual neighboring systems with offset starts and ritual ties, then models alternative coordination scales. Do not convert simulation fit into a complete origin chronicle.
- Temple meetings, agricultural rites, sacred-water exchanges, and calendar templates make obligations legible and recurring. Sacred water is not just a picturesque synonym for canal water: ritual relationships can authorize cooperation beyond the immediate hydraulic connection. [Lansing and Kremer 1993, especially pp. 100–103.]
- **Contested history:** strong bottom-up self-organization accounts are challenged by scholarship emphasizing kings, land, redistribution, and political control. Brooks et al. review both and explicitly identify gaps in written and archaeological evidence; historical origin scenarios remain hypotheses, not witnessed events.
- **Era pressure:** Green Revolution varieties and policies changed cultivation schedules and disrupted coordination in documented settings. The lesson is not “all new seed is wrong,” but that new crop durations can invalidate an existing scheduling bargain. [Lansing and Kremer 1993; Brooks et al. 2017/2018.]
- Craft transfer: design a water-allocation conflict first, then a rite that makes promises public, a calendar that repeats the meeting, and terminology distinguishing rights, duties, sacred transfers, and theft. Do not reduce worshippers’ commitments to secretly disguised engineering.

## Odalan dress and material signatures

- Treat an odalan as a change of social situation: preparation work, procession, worship, officiating, performance, and spectatorship need not use the same ensemble or access rules. Dibia’s religious/social/theatrical distinction is useful, but is not a universal sequence of scenes.
- The table is a reference vocabulary, not a mandatory outfit checklist. Local draping, color rules, and permissions need documentation; distinguish a garment from the cloth technology used to make it. [Hauser-Schäublin et al. 1991; H. Geertz 2004.]

| Term or technology | Material/construction | Wearer/use and coupling |
|---|---|---|
| *Kamen/kamben* | Wrapped lower-body cloth; cotton, silk, or later commercial fabrics | Men’s and women’s ceremonial ensembles, with differing local drape conventions; not a single tailored skirt pattern |
| *Saput* | Additional outer cloth, woven or commercially produced; fiber is a separate specification | Layering in many men’s formal ensembles marks ceremonial presentation; not interchangeable with every waist sash |
| *Udeng* | Folded/tied headcloth, including cotton cloth | Common in men’s ceremonial dress; no claim that every male resident wears one continuously |
| *Kebaya* | Sewn blouse, including cotton, lace, and contemporary fabric versions | Common contemporary women’s temple-going dress with lower wrap and sash; its specific adoption history is not projected into medieval Bali |
| *Selendang*/waist sash | Narrow textile, including cotton or silk, worn/tied at the waist | Ritual readiness and controlled presentation in relevant ensembles; a sash alone neither grants priestly office nor establishes social rank |
| *Endek* | Weft-ikat patterning: resist-dyed weft threads | Ceremonial and changing everyday/fashion uses; technology is not an immutable caste badge |
| *Songket* | Supplementary-weft patterning, often using metallic-looking threads | Costly ceremonial display; historical restrictions and present access must be staged separately |
| *Prada/perada* | Applied gold/gold-colored decoration, not inherently a weave | Splendor across performance and ceremonial objects; glitter is not evidence of solid-gold fiber |
| *Geringsing* | Double ikat associated particularly with Tenganan Pegringsingan | Community-specific ritual textile traditions; not generic “Balinese festival fabric” or a costume requirement at all odalan |
| *Poleng* | Check pattern, woven or printed on different substrates | Cloth can dress a shrine/tree or a ritual role as well as a person; not a universal worshipper uniform |

- White is prominent in many worship and priestly contexts, but “white clothes = priest,” “all odalan = white,” and “black always means funeral” are unsafe decoding rules. Specific officiant garments, headdress folds, and prohibited combinations are `(to-verify)` locally.
- Textile baseline is Hauser-Schäublin et al. 1991; blouse chronology and village-level permissions are thinner in this entry. Exact dates, legal restrictions, and who may wear a named heirloom remain `(to-verify)` rather than inferred from a general survey.
- A useful corroborating object is [Cornell’s Balinese dance ensemble, CFTC #275a–e](https://exhibits.library.cornell.edu/in-search-of-costumes-from-many-lands/feature/balinese-dance-ensemble), an undated exhibition record discussing a 1930s collection and citing Hauser-Schäublin et al. Its songket/perada stage assemblage is not evidence of ordinary odalan worshippers’ dress.
- Craft transfer: require every costume specification to state `term + fiber/technique + occasion + wearer/authority + era`. Design both a washable work version and a costly presentation version before adding rank symbolism.

## Gamelan: gong-cycle structure and cosmological comparison

- *Gamelan* names an ensemble tradition, not one instrument or one unchanging island-wide sound. *Gong kebyar* emerged around 1915 and draws on older court and village resources; its dramatic contrasts are historical innovations, not an unchanged ancient default. [Tenzer 2000b, §§1.2–1.5.]
- **Colotomic structure** is a scholarly analytical term for recurring instrumental punctuation. In relevant court-derived repertoires, smaller divisions fit into larger cycles whose strongest arrival is marked by the large gong. [Tenzer 2000b, §§1.3, 2.2–2.3.]

| Musical resource | Technical observation | Coupling available to a worldbuilder |
|---|---|---|
| Gong arrival | Marks the culminating/end–beginning point of a recurring span | Make an arrival socially consequential rather than treating every first beat as the strongest |
| Slower melodic strata | *Calung* and *jegogan* articulate progressively sparser melodic levels in Tenzer’s examples | Several time scales can coordinate without playing the same surface rhythm |
| *Kotekan* | Interlocking parts produce a composite melodic layer | A skill can belong to a relationship between performers rather than a solo virtuoso |
| *Ngubeng/majalan* | Balinese concepts of circling/stasis and proceeding/motion used in melodic analysis | Permit movement inside recurrence; “cyclic” does not mean nothing develops |
| Ensemble-specific tuning | Pitch intervals and pitch levels vary between sets | Do not assign every Balinese ensemble one universal Western scale or fixed concert pitch |

- Technical basis: Tenzer 2000b, §§2.1–2.6. An eight-beat example can have melodic levels every beat, every second beat, and every fourth beat, converging at gong; that is an example, not a law fixing every cycle’s length.
- **Cosmological interpretation:** Huang and Rockwell connect nested musical cycles, agricultural calendars, repeated collective work, and offerings through “relational practices.” Their interviews ground the connection in people who farm and perform, not merely in an outsider spotting similar diagrams. [2023, pp. 178–185.]
- Keep three claims distinct: music has nested timing; participants interpret music through sacred relationships; a scholar proposes that musical and calendrical structures are analogous. Neither the first nor the second proves an exact numerical identity between them.
- Triadic worlds, ninefold spatial schemes, and gong cycles may be brought into particular interpretations, but no identified repertoire, text, or practitioner behind a mapping such as “one gong = one god” or “210 days = 210 beats” appears in the sources checked here — such a mapping remains `(to-verify)` until one is produced.
- **Calibration:** a timeless “Balinese mind” deduced from circular music is an overgeneralization. Tenzer explicitly includes linear/asymmetrical organization and change; Huang and Rockwell explicitly discuss debate over circular temporality. Older anthropological fascination with an island outside history must not become a design premise.
- Craft transfer: specify who commissions, maintains, learns, and is authorized to perform a set; then decide which occasions make its sound an offering, a public display, or both. Sound alone cannot disclose the complete ritual context.

## Era model: stage recombination rather than cultural replacement

| Research stage, not a universal native periodization | Language/myth question | Material/performance consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Early medieval, before the fourteenth century | Date inscriptions and distinguish their language from later manuscript copies; examine Hindu–Buddhist and local relationships | Early dress reconstruction is thin; perishable cloth and retrospective accounts cannot supply a complete outfit |
| Majapahit-linked and later court histories, fourteenth–nineteenth centuries | Trace particular literary, priestly, and title traditions instead of one total “Javanization” event | Court patronage, textile exchange, and village traditions interact; do not assume a modern kebaya ensemble |
| Colonial conquest and administration, nineteenth–early twentieth centuries | Ask which official, court, collector, or scholar described status and customary institutions | Colonial archives/photos are situated records; staging and collecting helped select what outsiders saw as tradition |
| Early twentieth-century artistic change | Identify regional musicians and new repertoires rather than treating all innovation as foreign corruption | Kebyar and changing performance dress make “traditional” styles visibly historical |
| Post-1945 reform and arts education | Stage national-language use, Hindu doctrinal reform, new institutions, and THK terminology | Conservatories, public presentation, and religious standardization recouple inherited practices |
| Late twentieth century onward | Trace tourism, ecological pressure, work schedules, migration, and renewed local claims | New fabrics, seed varieties, venues, and livelihoods alter cost and access without automatically ending ritual commitments |

- Staging basis: H. Geertz 2004; Tenzer 2000b; Huang and Rockwell 2023. These are research partitions, not dated language stages or a substitute for a local chronology.
- *Babad* and lineage narratives may make claims about sacred ancestry and political legitimacy; scholarship reads them as dynastic genealogies and historical narratives rather than year-by-year records [Creese 1991]. Verify recensions and compare inscriptions/archives before treating a narrated founder or migration as a precise date.
- Do not stage Bali Aga communities as living fossils or presume that “pre-Majapahit” means isolated from Hindu, Buddhist, Islamic, or later state relationships. Huang and Rockwell’s notes explicitly flag the limits of that anthropological division.

## What a worldbuilder can take

| Transferable mechanism | Method artifact to prepare | Counter-test |
|---|---|---|
| Social relations alter word choice | Speaker–listener–referent matrix with neutral, honorific, humble, and insulting options | Does the system distinguish an absent honored person from the present listener? |
| A resource network is also a ritual network | Map of water flows, memberships, temples, rights, and sanctions | Can neighboring farms share a deity without one village or one ruler controlling everything? |
| Several clocks coexist | Recurrence rules plus a seasonal cultivation schedule | Can a festival drift through civic dates without moving the rainy season? |
| Cloth marks both people and places | Garment/technique/permission lexicon with dated variants | Can the same check pattern occur on a shrine and an official without implying identical status? |
| Music coordinates layered participation | Repertoire-specific cycle sketch and performance-authority notes | Are asymmetry, innovation, and secular enjoyment possible alongside sacred use? |
| Heritage language changes its object | Separate “date of institution” from “date of its explanatory doctrine” | Can a new slogan organize an older practice without proving the slogan ancient? |

- Apply to a fictional culture by changing the ecology, kinship, authority structure, and history first; derive language and material consequences from those choices. Renaming Dewi Danu, copying Tenganan cloth, and importing all honorifics is not a new cultural system.
- Assemble one proposed scene across domains: a resource meeting, its register choices, a recurring rite, a work/presentation dress contrast, and the authorized musical role. Record deliberate departures from the reference rather than disguising them as Balinese facts.
- Handoff to **lore-shokunin**: method rationale, source trail, proposed alternatives, temporal/geographic scope, unresolved permissions, and contradictions. Lore alone decides fictional entities, history, and canonical rules; this entry makes none of those decisions.

## Provenance cautions and limits

- Directly inspected open material: Arka 2003 (relevant grammar passages), Suwija 2017, Lansing–Kremer 1993, Brooks et al., Tenzer 2000b, Huang–Rockwell 2023, Unicode, UNESCO, and the Cornell object discussion. Publisher/registry metadata is not represented as a full reading of every listed print work.
- Arka 2005’s abstract and publication record were checked; its detailed formal analysis should be checked in the article before implementing it. Hauser-Schäublin 2003, Roth–Sedana 2015, and Dibia 1985 are explicitly mediated through inspected scholarship above, not fabricated direct quotations.
- Textile survey metadata and independent museum corroboration were checked, but specific page-level proofs of every local costume permission were not. Do not upgrade the vocabulary table into an attested costume reconstruction without that work `(to-verify)`.
- Knowledge is thinnest here for early dress, local ritual exegesis, historical distributions of speech forms, and chronicle manuscript transmission. Written/archaeological gaps constrain subak origin stories; present ethnography cannot silently fill a millennium of missing evidence.
- Colonial-era descriptions can privilege courts, Brahmanical elites, collectors’ categories, and exoticized bodies. Contemporary heritage and tourism accounts can erase conflict or project recent terms backward; cross-check both rather than treating either as an unfiltered native voice.
- Descriptive frameworks are situated: Arka’s lexical-functional analysis, Suwija’s prescriptive taxonomy, Lansing–Kremer’s adaptive model, and Huang–Rockwell’s relational interpretation answer different questions. None supplies a comprehensive, uncontested theory of Bali.
- Do not use discredited racial/civilizational ladders, a universal “primitive mentality,” or pop “timeless spiritual Bali” as explanatory frameworks. Resemblance between nested diagrams is not historical proof, and sacred availability is not permission to reproduce restricted texts or performances.
