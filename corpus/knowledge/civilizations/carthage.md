## 1. Scope and evidence architecture

- **Carthaginian** identifies a city/polity context; **Punic** usually identifies western Phoenician language/culture. Neither means every western settlement was politically controlled by Carthage at every date.
- Keep Carthage, western Phoenician settlements, Numidian contexts, and Roman Tripolitania as separate provenance fields. Comparison is useful; silent geographical substitution is not.
- Working framework: **triangulate inscription, deposition, and literary representation**. Agreement can strengthen an inference; one category must not silently supply what another cannot show.

| Evidence channel | Relatively strong for | Does not establish by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Punic votive inscriptions | divine titles, donor names, formulae, offices | complete theology, ordinary speech, cause of an infant's death |
| Tophet urns and stratigraphy | cremation, deposited remains, successive use | a single motive or uniform ritual throughout the site's history |
| Osteology/dental histology | age estimates within methodological limits | intention, parental consent, a narrative of the death |
| Greek/Roman histories | what an author reports and how outsiders classify practices | an unmediated Punic explanation of those practices |
| Roman-period bilingual inscriptions | language choice and institutional coexistence at a dated place | survival of every pre-conquest institution unchanged |

- Most surviving Punic texts are short and formulaic. Continuous native chronicles and myth narratives are scarce in the evidence available here; this entry cannot reconstruct a Carthaginian creation epic or full festival cycle.

## 2. Language family, daughterhood, and writing

- Classification: **Afroasiatic → Semitic → Northwest Semitic → Canaanite → Phoenician → western varieties conventionally called Punic** (Krahmalkov 2000, foreword; 2001).
- “Daughter” is a useful design model, not a claim that a new language appeared on a single foundation date. Western settlement, local transmission, contact, and changing written conventions produced differentiation over time.
- Distinguish the Tyrian/Sidonian background of western expansion from other Phoenician varieties such as Byblian; an eastern dictionary example is not automatically an attested Carthaginian usage.
- Phoenician/Punic writing uses a **right-to-left consonantal alphabet**, conventionally counted as twenty-two letters. Unwritten vowels are not evidence that speakers lacked vowels.
- The Canaanite vowel shift conventionally described as *ā > ō* belongs to the inherited comparative background; do not invent it as a uniquely Carthaginian innovation.
- Punic/Neo-Punic evidence includes weakening or loss of some guttural distinctions and increasingly variable vowel notation. Date and locality matter; avoid a universal “drop every guttural” sound-change rule.
- **Neo-Punic** is also a palaeographic/editorial designation. Script development and spoken-language change need not coincide with the destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE.
- Latin-letter Punic supplies additional vowel evidence, but transcription conventions and manuscript transmission intervene. A Roman spelling is not a phonetic recording.
- Modern forms **Tanit/Tinnit**, **Baal Hammon**, and **Melqart/Milqart** include editorial vocalization choices; preserve consonantal forms alongside readable names when building a reference ledger.

| Design variable | Historically grounded constraint | Fictional-culture application, not a historical claim |
|---|---|---|
| Sacred orthography | conservative consonantal spellings can outlast pronunciation | retain an old divine spelling while everyday names change |
| Port contact | Punic interacted with other Mediterranean and North African languages | assign loans by occupation, route, generation, and prestige |
| Register | votive formulae overrepresent formal language | generate dock speech separately from shrine inscriptions |
| Script shift | language and alphabet can change at different speeds | permit an inherited language in a conqueror's script |

## 3. Naval-trade vocabulary: attestations before atmosphere

- Krahmalkov's dictionary combines periods and regions and normalizes spellings; its foreword explicitly acknowledges the resulting risks. Keep each inscription locator and the dictionary's Phoenician/Punic/Neo-Punic distinction.
- The table gives **consonantal forms or verbal roots**, not reconstructed pronunciations or ready-to-use inflected sentences. Krahmalkov (2000), respective headwords, is the lexical source throughout.

| Form | Gloss / semantic field | Attestation and calibration |
|---|---|---|
| `ḥbl` | sailor | CIS I 3189.3, Punic: Milkyaton identified by occupation; dictionary p. 175 |
| `sḥr`, feminine `sḥrt` | merchant | RES 1575.3–4; RES 1229.1–2; KAI 92, Punic; lexical feminine is attested, not a census of commercial gender roles |
| `mkr` | seller; compare verbal root `m-k-r`, sell | CIS I 4874.3, seller of flax/linen; KAI 75.5, selling; distinguish occupation noun from verbal paradigm |
| `ksp` | silver, money | CIS I 5522.2–4, Punic; do not collapse weighed metal, accounting value, and minted coin into one technology |
| `š-q-l` | weigh out | Tripolitanian inscription 51 (86), lines 1–2, Neo-Punic; later regional evidence, not a Carthaginian naval command |
| `brrm` | interpreted as cargo vessels | Byblos inscription 13.3, Phoenician, in Krahmalkov's reading; eastern comparative evidence, not a secure Punic name for a warship class |
| `pšt` | flax/linen | CIS I 4874, Punic; a commercial/material term linking traders, textile supply, and dress |

- Naval lexicon design should separate **hull/rigging**, **rowing and command**, **coastal navigation**, **cargo and measures**, **contract/payment**, and **port authority**. Their borrowing histories need not match.
- “Trireme,” “quinquereme,” and “admiral” are convenient translation labels, not proof of corresponding Punic lexemes. Exact Punic equivalents, pronunciation, and rank distinctions remain `(to-verify)` before quotation.
- Do not fill gaps by copying Hebrew forms solely because they are cognate. Comparative reconstruction, dictionary gloss, and locally attested word are different evidence classes.
- Land wealth belongs in the model too: Diodorus 20.8.3–5 describes estates, vines, olives, livestock, and irrigation around Carthage. This is an outsider's portrayal, but it directly challenges a sea-only caricature.
- Application: derive freight terms from what actually moves, payment terms from the accounting regime, and ceremonial textile vocabulary from the same supply chain. Maritime specialization does not entail a language with only nautical metaphors.

## 4. Cult core and the limits of reconstructed mythology

| Cult element | Secure working base | Calibration / coupling use |
|---|---|---|
| Tanit/Tinnit, `tnt pn bʿl` | prominent female divine recipient in Punic dedications, often paired with Baal Hammon | `pn bʿl` is conventionally rendered “Face of Baal”; the title's theological force is not transparent |
| Baal Hammon, `bʿl ḥmn` | prominent male recipient of votive dedications | the etymology of `ḥmn` is disputed; do not build doctrine on one attractive etymology |
| Melqart and Tyre | Diodorus 20.14.1–3 reports Carthaginian offerings to Tyrian Heracles | Heracles is a Greek identification; distinguish the Greek category from the local divine name |
| Eshmun and Astarte | temple personnel attested in CIS I 252.4–5 and CIS I 3779.5–6, respectively | evidence for cult institutions, not a complete pantheon genealogy; Krahmalkov, s.v. `ʿbd` |
| Dido/Elissa traditions | foundation memory has multiple literary afterlives | Virgil's Dido–Aeneas narrative is Roman epic, not surviving Punic sacred scripture |

- Common dedication segments include `lrbt ltnt pn bʿl` and `wlʾdn lbʿl ḥmn`: “to the lady Tanit Face-of-Baal” and “to the lord Baal Hammon.” Compare CIS I 4901, 4904–4905 in Krahmalkov, s.v. `ʾdn`.
- `ndr`, vow, and “he heard his voice” formulae connect petition, named dedication, and expected response. The formula records a ritual relationship without narrating every rite performed.
- **Baal** is also a title, “lord.” Different Baal epithets must not be merged into one universal deity merely because the title recurs.
- Do not equate Tanit and Astarte automatically, or treat Greek/Roman identifications as proof of identical myths. **Interpretatio graeca/romana** translates and negotiates divine identities.
- Tanit as a universal “Great Mother,” Baal Hammon as a timeless “sun god,” and a tidy fertility-couple cosmology are insufficiently supported shortcuts. Specific functions require specific evidence.
- Method transfer: construct a cult around recipients, epithets, vow verbs, authorized personnel, and material offerings before inventing a creation narrative. Label any narrative invented to fill the historical gap as invention.

## 5. Tophet archaeology and child-sacrifice calibration

- **Tophet** is a modern archaeological label borrowed from a biblical term, not an established Punic self-name for the Carthage precinct. Do not import biblical Jerusalem's entire ritual system through that label.
- At Carthage the precinct contains urn deposits with cremated very young human remains, animal remains, or both, together with votive monuments. These physical observations are not contingent on accepting every literary account (Schwartz et al. 2010).
- An urn, a stele, an individual, and a ritual event are not interchangeable counting units. Commingling, repeated pyre use, disturbance, preservation, and excavation history affect associations.

| Question | Evidence / scholarly position | Responsible status |
|---|---|---|
| Were remains cremated and deposited ritually? | urns, burned bones/teeth, precinct context, votive monuments | well supported; cremation is not itself a diagnosis of killing |
| Were some deaths sacrificial? | literary reports, dedicatory language, animal comparisons, selected age profiles | a substantial evidence-based interpretation; not merely a modern sensational invention |
| Were all human interments sacrificial? | Schwartz et al. (2010) identify prenatal/perinatal remains and reject systematic sacrifice as the explanation of the sample | contested; their conclusion does not prove that sacrifice never occurred |
| Do age estimates support selection? | Smith et al. (2013) challenge the competing age assessments and argue for sacrifice | age methods, heating effects, and comparison populations are load-bearing, not incidental details |
| How should evidence be combined? | Xella et al. (2013) argue for sacrifice using the wider contextual record | neither skeletal age alone nor hostile literature alone settles every deposition |
| What was the frequency and social distribution? | requires dated contexts, representativeness, and defensible denominators | not recoverable here as a population rate or a universal household obligation |

- Schwartz and Smith worked on substantially overlapping excavated material. They are not two wholly independent archaeological populations whose conclusions can be counted as votes.
- Age estimation depends on tooth development, skeletal measurements, heat alteration, and neonatal-line interpretation. Neither an absent neonatal line nor a small bone automatically supplies a cause of death.
- `mlk`/“molk” terminology and sacrificial translations need philological scrutiny. Krahmalkov's explicit “child sacrificial victim” renderings incorporate interpretation; they are not interpretation-free translations of every monument.
- Diodorus 20.14.4–7 reports elite child sacrifice during the crisis of 310 BCE, using “Cronus” and a bronze-image mechanism. He wrote later; this is not an eyewitness report by Diodorus.
- Treat the bronze figure as a literary claim to test, not as the default drawing of an excavated installation. Do not extrapolate one reported crisis into the routine of every century.
- Greek/Roman rivalry and Christian polemic require source criticism, not automatic dismissal. Conversely, archaeological remains do not make every lurid textual detail true.
- Calibration here represents identified positions in the 2010/2013 debate, not a quantitative statement of current scholarly consensus; a newer comprehensive literature update remains `(to-verify)`.
- Application: design vow obligations, infant-death categories, memorial practices, substitution rules, and outsider accusations as separate variables. Never make real Carthaginians a shorthand for an inherently cruel fictional people.

## 6. Symbolic system: forms are not a translation dictionary

| Motif / carrier | Reference value | Guard against |
|---|---|---|
| So-called “sign of Tanit” | conventional modern label for a recurrent figure commonly combining a triangular/trapezoidal body, crossbar, and disk/head | treating every variant as a literal portrait or assuming the modern label supplies its ancient name |
| Crescent, disk, hand, vegetal/geometric motifs | contextual repertoire on Punic monuments; consult dated examples in Lancel (1995) | assigning each motif one universal astronomical, fertility, or protective meaning |
| Divine names beside images | stronger contextual anchor than visual resemblance alone | assuming adjacency makes each image a named god's portrait |
| Egyptianizing elements | Mediterranean circulation and local adaptation; compare the Met (2004) Levantine overview | calling every such object an import, or every user a follower of Egyptian theology |
| Repeated votive formula and donor lineage | writing itself participates in the monument's public display | reducing symbolic culture to isolated icons |

- Exact motif chronology, workshop distributions, and particular costume-bearing monument identifications are `(to-verify)` against object catalogues before visual reconstruction; the table is not an iconographic concordance.
- Use the contextual method in `material-culture-inference-method.md`: record carrier, findspot, date, inscription, deposition, and audience before inferring meaning.
- Fictional application: a shrine sign, merchant's mark, grave motif, and state emblem can overlap without being interchangeable. Invent rules of authorized reuse, not a single “Carthage aesthetic” sticker set.

## 7. Era model: stage institutions, not civilizational essence

- These broad stages are a design reference scaffold, not identical linguistic, artistic, or religious boundaries (overview: Lancel 1995; linguistic caution: Krahmalkov 2000).

| Horizon | Historical anchor | Coupled changes to model |
|---|---|---|
| Foundation / early settlement, ninth–eighth centuries BCE | Tyrian connection; traditional 814 BCE is a transmitted foundation date, not an excavated timestamp | ancestral naming, migrant/local contacts, imported and locally produced objects |
| Regional consolidation, sixth–fifth centuries BCE | growing western political networks; local variation remains | bureaucratic registers, offices, civic cult visibility; verify each feature's actual date |
| Fourth–third centuries BCE | war, trade, agrarian wealth, Mediterranean artistic interaction | differentiated occupational speech, elite display, cult investment; not an era of purely “Eastern” forms |
| Wars with Rome, 264–146 BCE | distinct wars and settlements; destruction in 146 BCE | disrupted networks, changing military expenditure and political memory, not automatic language extinction |
| Roman refoundation, late first century BCE onward | a new Roman urban framework at Carthage | new institutions and visual idioms; local language/cult survival must be demonstrated, not presumed |
| Roman African continuities, first–fifth centuries CE | Neo-Punic inscriptions, Latin-letter Punic, late-antique literary testimony | script choice, civic bilingualism, altered naming and cult identification; region-by-region evidence |

- “Romanization” should be decomposed into citizenship, office, language, script, cult, and dress. One Roman name or garment does not prove all dimensions changed together.
- Do not use the circular naval harbor as a timeless foundation-era city plan; its precise construction phases and appearance require an excavation-specific source `(to-verify)`.

## 8. Costume and material signature: build from supply and context

| Anchor | What can be used | What remains uncertain |
|---|---|---|
| Punic linen seller, CIS I 4874 | `pšt` joins textile material to occupational naming and market exchange | exact weave, cut, wearer class, and ceremonial exclusivity |
| Earlier/later tophet urn differences | Schwartz et al. describe earlier decorated red-clay urns versus more uniform later yellow-clay vessels in their sample | a clay-color change is not proof of an ethnic or caste replacement |
| Amulets/small objects in urn processing | funerary/votive selection can differ from daily adornment | a deposition is not a complete outfit or a population-wide wardrobe |
| Phoenician purple-dye production | Met (2004) supplies a broader Levantine comparison | not evidence that all Carthaginians wore purple or that a specific Punic sumptuary law existed |
| Broad purple stripe, KAI 126.7–9 | a Neo-Punic/Latin text records a Lepcis civic honor; Krahmalkov cites the parallel Latin | Roman-period provincial status display, not a pre-Roman Carthaginian dress code |

- Detailed Carthaginian garment cuts, priestly headwear, and gendered draping are a **thin-coverage area in the sources checked here**. Verify the so-called priest/priestess sarcophagi and their dates in a monument catalogue before drawing outfits `(to-verify)`.
- Do not turn sacred or elite funerary representation into a national uniform. Separate image convention, ritual dress, labor clothing, season, wealth, and access to imported fabrics.
- For fiction, follow **fiber → preparation → weaving/dyeing → trade → garment → rank/rite**. Choose the actual cuts independently and label them as design choices; no historical reconstruction is implied.
- Couple a textile seller's vocabulary to freight and money terms, a votive donor's dress to public visibility, and a Roman civic honor to bilingual office vocabulary. Shared supply chains can connect these without making them identical.

## 9. Naming practices and their linguistic-cultural coupling

| Pattern / example | Reference anchor | Design lesson |
|---|---|---|
| `ḥnbʿl`, Hannibal/Annobal | Krahmalkov, s.v. `ḥnbʿl`, gives blessing analyses involving Baal and different pronominal interpretations | a name can be a compressed divine petition, not merely a label |
| `ḥnʾ`, Hanno | Krahmalkov, s.v. `ḥnʾ`, shortened blessing-name type | shortened names need not signal secularization or loss of family piety |
| `ʿbdmlqrt`, Abdmilqart | “servant of Melqart”; attested personal-name element and names in the inscription corpus | distinguish a theophoric personal name from proof of an individual's occupation or legal status |
| `X bn Y bn Z` | patronymic chains; compare CIS I 4901, 4904–4905 | genealogical identification is not automatically a hereditary surname system |
| `X bt Y` | daughter relationship; compare the Aristibaal dedication under Krahmalkov, s.v. `qrqyn` | retain women's attested relational naming; do not infer complete social rules from one inscription |
| `qrt ḥdšt`, Qart-Hadast | “New City,” Krahmalkov, s.v. `qrtḥdšt` | a founding-era relational place-name can become opaque and acquire foreign exonyms |
| Annobal Rufus, son of Himilcho Tapapius | IRT 321, Lepcis, 1–2 CE | inherited and Roman name elements can coexist; do not force a binary Punic/Latin identity |

- Name formation should preserve the distinction between ordinary vocabulary, inherited name stock, divine titles, and foreign transcriptions. A name's conventional spelling can fossilize an older linguistic stage.
- Keep a ledger of **inscribed consonants → proposed analysis → conventional reading → Greek/Latin rendering → place/date**; alternative analyses remain attached rather than silently overwritten.
- For fiction, borrow the mechanism—blessing name, patronymic, civic affiliation, later administrative overlay—not the entire inventory of famous generals. Derive new forms from the fictional language's own grammar.

## 10. Roman-era Punic survivals: multiple clocks

- The destruction of Carthage ended a polity, not the language throughout North Africa. Neo-Punic and Latin-letter Punic evidence must be geographically distinguished (Krahmalkov 2000; Jongeling 2008).
- **IRT 321** is a specific anchor: at Lepcis Magna a theatre lintel dated 1–2 CE has Latin followed by Neo-Punic, described by its editors as a literal translation. Annobal Rufus combines local lineage, the office `sufes`, and Roman imperial/priestly titulature.
- This proves institutional bilingual display at Lepcis; it is not direct evidence for the contemporary speech of every inhabitant of Roman Carthage.
- Latin-letter Punic is language survival with alphabet change, not necessarily the same thing as code-switching. Adams (2003) provides the bilingualism/contact framework; Krahmalkov's foreword identifies the Tripolitanian corpus.
- Augustine's late-antique testimony is important but socially situated. Krahmalkov cites *Epistulae ad Romanos inchoata expositio* 13 for the reported `Chanani` identification; check the primary edition and context before quoting its wording `(to-verify)`.
- Do not infer a numerical Punic-speaking majority, uniform rural monolingualism, or a precise extinction date from isolated testimony.
- Baal Hammon's Roman-period identification with Saturn is recorded in Krahmalkov, s.v. `bʿl ḥmn`; Tanit/Caelestis identifications require their own dated cult evidence `(to-verify)` before asserting uninterrupted local continuity.
- Application: let personal names, shrine language, public inscriptions, legal status, and daily clothing each have a different persistence curve. Continuity and institutional transformation can occur together.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take

1. **Daughter-language staging:** one inherited grammar, localized sound changes, conservative cult spellings, and later script replacement; keep each change dated and socially located.
2. **Votive public culture:** divine titles + petition/vow verbs + donor genealogy + material monument; a generative system with no need to invent a recovered epic.
3. **Trade–dress coupling:** material terms, sellers, freight measures, payment, and display share a production chain; maritime commerce can coexist with substantial agrarian wealth.
4. **Layered names:** theophoric petitions, shortened forms, patronymic chains, exonyms, and administrative overlays; neither names nor outfits are reliable standalone ethnic tests.
5. **Contested ritual history:** separate physical deposits, technical inference, insiders' formulae, outsiders' accusations, and later memory. Decide fictional practices independently rather than laundering a disputed historical claim into fact.
6. **Handoff discipline:** give lore-shokunin a mechanism, source locator, era/place envelope, uncertainty tag, and explicit invented elements; this entry supplies no world-bible canon.
