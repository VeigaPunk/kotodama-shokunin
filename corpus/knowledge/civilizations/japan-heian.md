## 2. Era model — stage institutions, not a timeless aesthetic

- **Scope:** roughly 794–1185, with 1192 sometimes used for the political transition; Heian-kyō is the court centre, not a representative sample of the archipelago. Japanese of this span is conventionally **Early Middle Japanese**, approximately 800–1200 (Frellesvig 2010).
- Use overlapping political, linguistic, religious, and material timelines; none changes overnight at a dynasty-style boundary (Shively/McCullough 1999).

| Working stage | Institutional emphasis | Language/symbol/dress coupling | Reconstruction caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early Heian, eighth–ninth centuries | Reworked ritsuryō institutions; continental learning; Tendai and Shingon establishments | Chinese textual prestige remains strong; rank dress changes from inherited code forms | Do not put the fully developed eleventh-century women's ensemble into every 794 scene |
| Middle Heian, tenth–eleventh centuries | Regency households and court patronage; imperial waka anthologies | Kana prose, kanbun records, poetic exchanges, and competitive dress operate together | “Native culture” is not the disappearance of Chinese learning or external contact |
| Late Heian, late eleventh–twelfth centuries | Cloistered rule, competing patrons, estates, increasingly powerful warriors | Court precedent and dress expertise develop; ceremonial silhouettes and practices continue changing | Do not transplant later samurai uniformity or Edo kimono conventions backward |
| After the political transition | Court institutions persist beside warrior governments | Anthology authority, names, ceremonial knowledge, and robe conventions are transmitted and revised | A Kamakura copy can witness an older practice, but is not automatically contemporary evidence |

- **Design inference:** synchronize a script reform, wardrobe change, and new patronage system only where a causal link is supplied; otherwise let their dates diverge.
- **Coverage limit:** court diaries and literature are unusually rich for elite display; rural speech, everyday dress, artisans' own explanations, and many provincial practices are much thinner in this entry's evidence base.

## 3. Language family and writing — digraphia is an access system

- **Family:** Japanese belongs to Japonic, together with the Ryukyuan languages. Literary Chinese is a contact/prestige resource, not evidence that Japanese is genealogically Chinese. A larger “Altaic” or “Transeurasian” affiliation is disputed, not a settled design premise (Frellesvig 2010).
- Japanese is predominantly head-final, with postpositions and extensive suffixing; historical phonology, inflections, and honorific usage must be staged rather than copied from present-day textbook Japanese.
- **Kanbun/kana digraphia** is a useful modern analytic shorthand; actual writing includes intermediate and mixed practices, not two sealed ethnic or gender languages (Frellesvig 2010; LaMarre 2000).

| Written practice | Typical authority/access | Coupling handle | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kanbun: texts in Literary Chinese and locally adapted forms | Administration, learned composition, records, Buddhist learning | Literacy training and office confer access to textual authority | Assuming every writer conversed fluently in contemporary spoken Chinese |
| Kundoku: reading Chinese texts through Japanese grammatical procedures | Trained readers; glossing and reading traditions | One written text can support a different language's ordering and supplied grammar | Treating it as a separate alphabet or an automatic literal translation |
| Hiragana: cursive developments from phonographic character use | Waka, correspondence, prose, especially celebrated women's writings | Calligraphy, paper, poetic competence, and social intimacy share a surface | “Only women used kana”; men also wrote it |
| Katakana: abbreviated character forms, strongly associated with annotation | Scholastic and religious reading contexts | A specialist notation can later gain broader functions | Modern katakana = foreign words projected unchanged into Heian practice |
| Mixed inscription and decorated writing | Objects, letters, manuscript pages, performance settings | Script shape and placement participate in meaning | Reducing a poem to disembodied dictionary glosses |

- The association of Chinese learning with men and kana with women is socially consequential, but **not a biological partition of competence**; Murasaki's diary complicates the opposition (Bowring 1996).
- Tsurayuki's kana preface and the anthology's Chinese preface offer a concrete dual-authority model; neither implies that waka lived outside learned continental traditions.
- **Application:** specify who learns each script, who pays for instruction and paper, which genre admits code-switching, and when conspicuous learning is socially risky.

## 4. Kokinshū poetic vocabulary — an indexed network, not decorative nouns

- The commission date 905 is conventional, not proof that every poem, editorial decision, or surviving manuscript dates to that day; textual states and later transmission matter.
- The anthology's seasonal books and love sequence make **arrangement** part of meaning. Read adjacent poems and headnotes, not only isolated “best quotes” (Rodd/Henkenius 1996; Lewis Cook's [UVa introduction](https://jti.lib.virginia.edu/japanese/kokinshu/intro.html)).
- The dominant short waka has a 5–7–5–7–7 sound-unit pattern: not five English metrical lines, thirty-one written characters, or three-line haiku. Historical pronunciation controls the count.

| Diction cluster | Semantic work available | Cross-system coupling | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ume, uguisu: plum and bush warbler | Early-spring recognition, anticipation, scent and voice | A seasonal message can be echoed by robe colours and a flowering branch | “Nightingale” in older translations is not a safe biological identification |
| Sakura, hana, kasumi: cherry, blossom, haze | Flowering, concealment, waiting, loss | Visibility of blossoms parallels partial visibility of dress and person | Hana is context-dependent; not every occurrence means cherry |
| Momiji: autumn foliage | Change, richness, scattering | Leaf colour, textile description, and emotional change can reinforce one another | Not a universal red paint chip |
| Tsuyu, namida, sode: dew, tears, sleeves | Moisture makes emotion materially legible | A tear-wet sleeve is both a garment and a poetic surface | Do not turn every literary sleeve into a literal diary report |
| Matsu: pine / waiting | Homophony supports linked botanical and emotional readings | A branch, pattern, and phrase can activate the same wordplay | Build the homophony in the fictional language first |
| Iro, utsurou: colour, alteration/fading | Appearance and human attachment can be brought into relation | Dye, bloom, age, and desire share a vocabulary without becoming identical | Translation may erase polysemy or add a modern colour category |

- **Named devices:** _kakekotoba_ = a pivot exploiting overlapping readings; _engo_ = associated-word networks; _makurakotoba_ = conventional epithetic expressions; _utamakura_ = accumulated poetic place associations. They are not interchangeable names for “symbolism.”
- **Checkable example:** Ono no Komachi, Kokinshū II, no. 113, [Japanese text](https://jti.lib.virginia.edu/japanese/texts/RodKoki/vol3.html): _hana no iro_ and _utsuri_ frame fading; _furu_ and _nagame_ permit temporal/rain and gazing/long-rain readings. Consult an annotated translation for the grammatical interlock.
- A pivot is not permission to invent an etymology: a convincing fictional equivalent needs compatible sounds, syntax, and two plausible semantic paths.
- **Do not retrofit:** a modern formal _kigo_ dictionary, generalized “wabi-sabi,” or Motoori Norinaga's later systematic account of _mono no aware_ is not a ready-made tenth-century rulebook.
- The kana preface attributes efficacy to poetry, including moving heaven/earth and affecting unseen beings; treat this as a situated poetics, not proof that all Japanese speech was thought to cast spells.

## 5. Mythic and religious core — overlapping jurisdictions

| Layer | Reference core | Coupling use | Boundary |
|---|---|---|---|
| Earlier myth-genealogy | Kojiki: Izanagi/Izanami generate islands and deities; death and purification establish boundaries; Amaterasu's light and descendants connect to imperial ancestry; Susanoo's transgression and serpent-slaying connect conflict to regalia | Genealogies authorize lineages, sites, ritual relationships, and narrated origins | A 712 court compilation is not a complete Heian pantheon inventory (Heldt 2014) |
| Court kami rites and local cults | Multiple shrines, officiants, sacred places, purification practices | Sacred authority can be local and office-specific rather than one universal priesthood | Avoid a single timeless, institutionally separate “Shinto church” |
| Tendai/Shingon and esoteric ritual | Early-Heian Buddhist establishments; Lotus traditions in Tendai, Dainichi/Mahāvairocana in Shingon; texts, images, initiation and patronage | Sacred language and restricted expertise connect ritual, politics, and material display | These are emphases, not exclusive deity lists; a Buddhist image cannot be assigned a kami equivalent by visual similarity alone |
| Pure Land devotion | Amida and rebirth in a western paradise become prominent in elite late-Heian patronage | Direction, architectural orientation, poetry, and hopes after death can interact | Not everybody follows one doctrine or the same chronology of decline |
| Dangerous spirits and protection | Pacification, illness, misfortune, divination, and ritual responses | Political grievance can acquire ritual consequences | Do not use fictional possession scenes as a census of belief |

- **Secure broad structure, variable local relationships:** kami worship, Buddhist practice, and divinatory expertise overlap; later categories such as “religion” and “Shinto” require historical qualification (Hayashi/Hayek 2013, pp. 2–7).
- _Honji suijaku_ names Buddhist-original/kami-manifestation relationships, but particular equations have histories; do not assign a completed medieval chart to every Heian shrine.
- The Kojiki supplies an earlier mythic repertoire; liturgy, shrine records, chronicles, diaries, and dated images are needed to establish what a particular Heian institution did with it.
- **Application:** assign each cult a patron, language/register, site, ritual competence, and contested claim; overlap is more productive than distributing every god into exclusive “departments.”

## 6. In'yō gogyō — a correlative grammar, not a universal magic chart

- **In'yō** (陰陽, yin/yang) and **gogyō** (五行, five phases) are related conceptual resources; **Onmyōdō** names historically situated expertise and institutions, not simply their sum (Hayashi/Hayek 2013, pp. 2–3).
- Major (1993) supplies the Chinese background matrix below; it is a comparative template. A correspondence's presence in China does not itself verify its use in a specific Heian rite.

| Phase | Japanese label | Direction | Conventional season | Colour family |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Wood | moku, 木 | East | Spring | Blue/green, 青 |
| Fire | ka, 火 | South | Summer | Red, 赤 |
| Earth | do, 土 | Centre | Seasonal transitions; late summer in some arrangements | Yellow, 黄 |
| Metal | kin, 金 | West | Autumn | White, 白 |
| Water | sui, 水 | North | Winter | Black, 黒 |

- **Relations matter:** generating sequence wood → fire → earth → metal → water → wood; overcoming sequence wood → earth → water → fire → metal → wood. These are correlative relationships, not evidence of physical causation.
- **Do not collapse pentads:** Buddhist _godai_ commonly uses earth, water, fire, wind, space; that is not the wood/fire/earth/metal/water list.
- The Onmyōryō combined responsibilities involving astronomy, calendars, timekeeping, and divination. Court Onmyōdō subsequently involved changing patronage and hereditary expertise, including Abe and Kamo specialists; it was not an unchanging guild of fantasy magicians.
- Directional prohibitions and _katatagae_ (changing direction by taking another lodging/route) make calendar knowledge affect travel, visits, and narrative timing; an exact day's calculation requires its actual system `(to-verify)`.
- **Contested:** whether Onmyōdō is best classified as technique, art, or religion; Hayashi/Hayek explicitly reject a simple equation with imported Daoism and a simple “golden age then decay” story.
- **Application:** record who computes a correspondence, for whom, by which textual lineage, and with what exception. Yin is not “evil women,” yang is not “good men,” and phase colours do not dictate every garment's meaning.

## 7. Costume and material signature — ensemble, surface, labour

- Women's elite layered dress is commonly called **jūnihitoe** retrospectively; “twelve layers” is not a reliable inventory for every date or occasion. Prefer a dated ensemble description to treating the name as an arithmetic law.
- **Itsutsuginu-karaginu-mo** is a useful formal-ensemble label in modern costume references; do not assume the label and today's standardized arrangement reproduce all Heian usage.
- Distinguish the **hitoe**, an unlined robe, from multiple **uchiki**, layered robes; the layered set is not twelve copies of a garment called hitoe.
- Formal elements include **karaginu**, a short outer jacket, and **mo**, a trailing garment; hakama and other inner/outer robes belong to the ensemble. Order, number, materials, and terminology require date-specific verification.
- Men's **sokutai** is a full formal ensemble, not simply its outer **hō**. Court caps, belt, baton, trousers, underlying garments, and sometimes weapons participate in rank and office display (Takata n.d.).

| Surface or material | What it communicates | Useful design consequence | Evidence control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Sleeve, collar, hem edges | Layer relationships visible without exposing a whole person | A small visible band carries taste, season, or a mistake | Do not assume one universal rule of female seclusion |
| Thin silk and lining | Transmitted light and layered colour, not only opaque stripes | The same dye appears different over another layer | Modern printed swatches cannot reproduce the whole effect |
| Weave and motif | Permission, occasion, cost, office, inherited precedent | A legal colour can still be wrong in a restricted fabric | Rank rules are more than hue labels |
| Screens, curtains, fans, letter paper | Managed visibility and mediated encounter | Voice, handwriting, scent, and sleeves provide partial evidence of identity | Access depends on relationship and setting, not an absolute ban on speech |
| Silk production, dyeing, dressing assistance | Resources and skilled labour behind elite display | Garment prestige creates dependencies on workers and supply routes | Do not infer ordinary people's wardrobes from court luxury |

- A woman's formal layered ensemble, a man's sokutai, a monk's robes, and a worker's clothing are **different systems**; the Costume Museum catalogue explicitly separates such social and ceremonial contexts.

## 8. Layering calendar — season names require a textile operation

- Separate **omote/ura** (face/lining pairing), **successive robe layers**, and **warp/weft colour effects**. The museum identifies all three; an illustration of one is not automatically a recipe for another.
- _Kasane no irome_ is a convenient retrospective umbrella; Shōgakukan's entry notes the phrase's late-Edo usage and variation by garment, season, and house tradition. The practices are older than that label.
- Calendar scaffold: spring = lunisolar months 1–3, summer = 4–6, autumn = 7–9, winter = 10–12. These are **not Gregorian month numbers**, and not hard legal wear-ranges for every named combination.

| Seasonal position | Documented reference or open slot | Coupling opportunity | Confidence/limit |
|---|---|---|---|
| Early spring | _Kōbai no nioi_: red-plum gradation, red through progressively lighter pinks | Verse about opening buds and a graded visible edge reinforce timing | Shōji's modern demonstration (Cabinet Office 2020); not a universal ancient dye formula |
| Spring, floral progression | Plum, cherry, wisteria, yamabuki have distinct associations | Matching a botanical name is insufficient: choose which part or growth stage supplies the colours | Same name can identify different layer/lining arrangements |
| Summer, fourth month | _Hanatachibana_: _Masasuke shōzoku shō_ quotation includes yellow gradations, white, and green gradations | Leaves, blossom, and fruit can all be represented in one arrangement | Mediated through Hachijō/Costume Museum; white/green hitoe alternatives are preserved, not flattened into one rule |
| Summer, fifth month | _Shōbu_: quotation combines green gradations, white, and red-plum gradations with a white raw-silk hitoe | Festival plant terminology and garment naming share a referent | Hachijō argues for leaf/root colours, not purple iris flowers; label that identification interpretive |
| Autumn | _Beni-momiji_: quotation combines red, yamabuki/yellow, and green gradations | Transition between colours, not all-red saturation, can carry the seasonal meaning | Mediated late-Heian prescription; hitoe reading/variant needs original-edition checking `(to-verify)` |
| Winter and seasonal edges | Reserve a dated cold-season recipe before filling the wardrobe chart | Contrast calendar season, actual weather, and deliberate anticipation | Exact winter palette/order not established here `(to-verify)`; do not invent a “snow” formula |

- A white face over a red lining can yield a cherry-like pale tone through thin silk; it is not the same operation as putting a white robe over a red robe (Cabinet Office 2020; Costume Museum n.d.).
- **Recipe record:** source/date → garment → face/lining or ordered layers → hitoe → weave/translucency → season/occasion → wearer's permission → variants. Record the source's order before translating it into an illustration.
- A named motif can migrate between garment, paper, and verse without requiring identical pigments or numbers of layers.
- **Do not overgeneralize from modern practice:** the museum's “五衣と単” documents Edo, Taishō, and later arrangements; modern imperial dress is not an untouched eleventh-century survival.

## 9. Rank colours — requested “shikishō” needs a terminology correction

- **Secure terms:** **ishiki** (位色) and **tōjiki** (当色) for rank-corresponding colour; **ihō** (位袍) for the rank robe. **Shikishō** (式正) means proper/formal ceremony, not the rank-colour ladder (Shōgakukan n.d.). “Shikishō = rank colours” remains `(to-verify)`; do not silently make it a historical synonym.
- The table contrasts an inherited Yōrō clothing-code scheme with later practice summarized by Takata. **It describes selected male official dress, not a female layering chart, every court occasion, or a four-century frozen law.**

| Wearer/rank | Earlier code baseline | Middle-Heian tendency, especially tenth–eleventh centuries | Qualification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Emperor | White in the inherited scheme | Kōrozen, a distinctive yellow-brown hō, added for specified public court occasions from 820 | White remains important for rites; emperor is not “rank one” |
| Crown prince | Ōni/ōdan, 黄丹, yellow-red | Distinctive reserved yellow-red dress continues | Dye name and privilege, not any modern orange swatch |
| First rank | Deep purple | Black/dark rank robe | Dark purple's relation to black changes historically |
| Second–third ranks | Light purple | Black/dark rank robe | Do not retain the early light/deep distinction indefinitely |
| Fourth rank | Deep scarlet | Black | Especially clear warning against copying the Nara ladder into an eleventh-century scene |
| Fifth rank | Light scarlet | Deep scarlet | Some offices preserve lighter distinctions; office and permission also matter |
| Sixth–seventh ranks | Deep/light green | Green for lower ranks | Takata dates a further shift toward hanada/blue in the twelfth century |
| Eighth/initial ranks | Deep/light hanada, a blue family | Later lower-rank scheme is simplified | A code's nominal ranks need not all be populated in actual court society |

- **Kinjiki/kinshoku** (禁色) concerns restricted colours and, in relevant usages, fabrics/patterns; exceptional permission is part of the system, not evidence that the restrictions never existed.
- **Concrete coupling:** Hachijō's “山吹②” quotes late-Heian legal compilation on gardenia colours too similar to the heir's yellow-red, and attempts to rename them yamabuki or yellow fallen-leaf colours. Names can become loopholes; enforcement is not just visual recognition.
- That page also quotes a 1005 dispute over a sixth-rank chamberlain's conspicuous under-robe; privilege, rank, weave, and ceremonial context did not automatically agree. These are mediated quotations; inspect the originals before exact legal reconstruction.
- **Never derive this ladder from gogyō alone:** purple/black rank history, a plum layer, and north/water/black belong to different codes. One pigment can be prestigious here and cosmological elsewhere without contradiction.

## 10. Naming practices — identity depends on audience and relation

| Naming resource | Attested reference pattern | Fictional-system question | Anti-error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Clan/lineage plus personal name | Fujiwara no Michinaga, Ki no Tsurayuki | Which affiliation is named, and who may omit it? | _No_ is not an invariant modern hereditary surname particle |
| Office-derived designation | Shikibu, Shōnagon in court women's literary designations | Can a household member's office supply another person's public label? | Such titles do not prove the woman herself held that office |
| Sobriquet and literary association | Murasaki Shikibu is not a securely recovered birth-name pair | Who controls an enduring nickname? | Exact origin/first use of a sobriquet requires its own evidence |
| Residence, relationship, honorific | Court identification through location or relation rather than a naked personal name | Who is identifiable only inside a patronage network? | Do not translate every location-title into a modern surname |
| Written name versus reading | Shōshi/Akiko for 彰子; Teishi/Sadako for 定子 | Can one written name support learned and vernacular readings? | A conventional scholarly reading does not establish the bearer's spoken everyday name |
| Posthumous/regnal/era labels | Later historical labels and nengō dating | Who names a reign, and when? | A nengō need not equal a whole reign; posthumous labels are unavailable in life |

- Reference controls: introductions to Bowring (1996) and McKinney (2006), anthology headnotes, and Shively/McCullough (1999); exact Heian pronunciation of particular names is often `(to-verify)`.
- **Design inference:** store written form, possible readings, kin group, office, residence, intimate name, public title, and who uses each. A name's disclosure can be socially meaningful without inventing a universal magical “true-name taboo.”

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take

| Reusable mechanism | Couple these systems | Minimal design test |
|---|---|---|
| Domain-distributed digraphia | Writing, education, gendered expectations, office | Can the same person write an official record and an intimate poem differently? |
| Allusive semantic networks | Lexicon, seasonal ecology, garment/paper names | Does one existing word support the intended pun or association without an invented etymology? |
| Three independent colour codes | Cosmology, rank, seasonal taste | Can a combination be seasonally apt but institutionally forbidden? |
| Materially mediated colour | Textile technology, dye supply, class | Does translucency or lining change what a viewer actually sees? |
| Ritualized time and direction | Calendar, specialist authority, travel, correspondence | Can an appointment fail for a rule more specific than generic “bad luck”? |
| Indexical naming | Kinship, place, office, narrative viewpoint | Do different speakers identify the same person differently and recoverably? |
| Asynchronous change | Era model, language contact, costume, patronage | Do imported prestige forms persist after local innovations become fashionable? |

- **End-to-end method:** select a dated social event → assign writer/reader and script → establish calendar/route constraints → choose permitted ensemble → choose seasonal textile operation → compose a semantically connected message → test the recipient's likely reading.
- Pass lore-shokunin a **proposal**, not canon: mechanism, source witness, date/social scope, alternatives, contradictions, confidence, and questions requiring a decision. Keep historical reference facts separate from fictional transformations.

## 12. Provenance cautions and honest gaps

- **Court-source bias:** chronicles, diaries, and elite literature privilege patrons and literate households; fiction, prescriptions, and actual practice are different evidence types. Detailed court coverage does not license claims about every inhabitant of Heian Japan.
- **Scarcity:** early-Heian garments and direct visual witnesses are sparse; later paintings, copies, manuals, and reconstructions must carry their own dates. This entry does not supply an archaeological inventory or a complete provincial dress history.
- **Mediated costume evidence:** Hachijō's accessible posts distinguish earlier quotations from his interpretation, but do not replace a critical edition. Exact colour orders, textual variants, and rule enforcement remain narrow-scope checks, not universalized certainty.
- **Thin areas here:** exact winter layering recipes, precise day-by-day directional calculations, individual spoken name readings, and the local chronology of particular kami/Buddha equations remain `(to-verify)`.
- **Colonial/national framing:** “pure native kana culture replacing foreign Chinese culture” is a retrospective simplification. LaMarre (2000) critiques national-purity readings; Hayashi/Hayek (2013) trace modern categories that distorted Onmyōdō scholarship.
- **Reject pop universals:** no timeless twelve-layer uniform, one-word-one-colour symbolism, male/Chinese versus female/Japanese biological split, or anime-style Seimei biography as primary historical evidence. Modern costume pedagogy and heritage promotion are valuable but independently situated sources.
- **Translation calibration:** _phase_ is not a chemical element; _ao_ is not a fixed blue swatch; “kimono” can be a modern explanatory umbrella; a romanized modern reading is not reconstructed Heian pronunciation. Preserve the Japanese label and the source's domain before choosing an English gloss.
