## 1. Scope and calibration

- **Sacred register:** socially recognized ways of speaking, writing, voicing, or withholding words in relation to sacred authority; not necessarily a separate language.
- **Liturgical language:** a language or variety used in authorized worship; it can also support scholarship, administration, literature, conversation, or new composition.
- **Prayer language:** specify the event, not merely the religion: communal recitation, private petition, hymn, divination, sermon, and instruction can have different language rules.
- **Priestly cant:** use this analytical label only for a demonstrably bounded specialist repertoire; distinguish technical vocabulary, restricted ritual knowledge, esoteric interpretation, and an actual distinct code.
- **Fossilization** below means selective conservation relative to other varieties, not a claim that a language stops changing or its users stop thinking.
- Calibration: **documented** = a bounded source-supported observation; **interpretation** = an author's explanation; **design heuristic** = an explicit fictional construction tool; `(to-verify)` = unresolved detail, not permission to invent.
- These are bounded historical and ethnographic cases, not four internally uniform “civilizations.” “Mythic core” describes sacred narrative organization, not a judgment that practitioners' beliefs are false.

## 2. Named frameworks: what they license and what they do not

| Framework | Use in this coupling | Calibration / limit |
|---|---|---|
| Ferguson's diglossia (1959) | Map a learned H variety and ordinary L varieties by domain, acquisition, prestige, and codification | Foundational ideal type; H/L are social labels, not intelligence rankings. The original definition is narrower than all multilingual religion |
| Haeri's ownership versus custodianship (2003) | Ask whether speakers regard themselves as free to innovate or as caretakers of divinely owned words | Interpretation grounded in Egypt; do not assign the same attitude to every Muslim or Arabic speaker. Burt (2003) disputes some linguistic analyses |
| Embodied transmission in Larios (2017) | Treat memory, training, teacher relations, and bodily discipline as preservation infrastructure | Contemporary Maharashtrian schools, not direct observation of ancient Vedic society or all Hindu education |
| Recensional comparison in Meyendorff (1991) | Compare actual editions and rubrics with reformers' claims of restoring antiquity | His historical argument distinguishes claimed ancient authority from contemporary models; not a universal explanation of schism |

- Working synthesis: `authority claim ↔ teaching institution ↔ performance norm ↔ public recognition`; scripts, garments, and calendars can make each link visible.
- Keep directionality open: ritual can conserve language; an expanding literary standard can reshape worship; state schooling can redistribute sacred expertise; changed patronage can sustain a new recension.

## 3. Typology: sacredness, age, and closure are independent

| Configuration | Linguistic mechanism | Access pattern to investigate | Fictional consequence to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Archaic register within a living language | Retained pronouns, formulae, parallelism, special address | Broad comprehension; performance privileges may remain narrow | Anyone can recognize a blessing without being allowed to officiate |
| Learned liturgical language | School-acquired grammar and a canonical corpus | Unequal proficiency; recitation may be much wider than fluent composition | An excellent reciter may require a translator for new prose |
| Imported prayer language | A prestigious corpus learned across unrelated vernaculars | Access need not depend on ethnic ancestry | Ritual affiliation crosses political and language-family borders |
| Restricted specialist vocabulary | Replacement terms, euphemisms, technical senses, ritual names | Initiation or apprenticeship gates particular meanings | A public sentence contains an insider operational instruction |
| Esoteric interpretation of public text | Ordinary or canonical words given restricted readings | The text is available; the commentary or procedure is not | Stealing a book does not automatically confer competence |
| Authorized vernacular worship | Translation, paraphrase, or new composition under defined authority | Comprehension expands; approval can remain centralized | The reform opens participation without abolishing offices |
| Revival or deliberate re-archaization | Reconstructed pronunciation, revived forms, selected older editions | Newly taught norms may be presented as immemorial | Two parties can both claim to preserve the original |

- This table is a design taxonomy, not a ranking of religions. A community can combine several rows in different rites.
- Do not equate **not native**, **not understood**, **not public**, **not changeable**, and **not performable by laypeople**; record each separately.

## 4. Liturgical fossilization: freeze the right layer

| Layer | A plausible conserved feature | What may still move | Evidence needed in a real analogue |
|---|---|---|---|
| Text / recension | Accepted word sequence, hymn order, inherited morphology | New hymns, commentary, selection, editorial correction | Dated witnesses and named editions, not a claim of “unchanged since origin” |
| Sound | Prescribed accent or recitation pattern | Regional realization, teaching norms, reconstruction | Recordings or specialist descriptions by school and date |
| Meaning | A canonical gloss or theological interpretation | Everyday meaning, competing exegesis, translation | Contemporary commentary alongside lexicography |
| Script | Revered letter forms or spelling | Handwriting, layout, punctuation, print technology | Manuscript and printed exemplars; a script is not a language family |
| Performance | Who speaks which passage, sequence, bodily orientation | Tempo, amplification, audience roles | Rubrics compared with observed practice |
| Material sign | An office garment or authorized book format | Fiber, dye, cut, manufacture, repairs | Dated objects and local dress evidence |

- Heuristic: preserve an old formula while allowing ordinary vocabulary to change; then decide whether readers pronounce the formula historically, locally, or through a later prestige standard.
- Toy derivation, **not canon**: an older plural prayer ending survives in a memorized refrain after ordinary speech replaces that ending; teachers subsequently explain the retained ending as a sign of continuity.
- Distinguish inheritance from learned borrowing: a word reintroduced from a sacred corpus need not display the same sound changes as its everyday cognate. Couple to `linguistics/historical-linguistics.md`.
- Textual stability requires work: teachers, copying, correction, rehearsal, funding, and replacement of damaged objects. “Ancient” is not an adequate maintenance model.
- Sound preservation does not imply semantic emptiness; participants can attach efficacy, devotion, affiliation, aesthetic value, and varying degrees of lexical understanding to the same performance.

## 5. Prayer languages: model events and permissions

| Event | Language-policy question | Symbol / material coupling |
|---|---|---|
| Canonical recitation | Is the wording itself constitutive of the act, or may an authorized translation substitute? | Book treatment, memorization marks, posture, recitation space |
| Sermon or explanation | Must instruction use the ritual code, a vernacular, or a mixture? | A different speaking position or garment can mark the switch |
| Private petition | Are free words legitimate, supplementary, or disfavored in this setting? | Domestic versus institutional objects; no automatic public uniform |
| Hymn / communal response | Which portions are learned by everyone, and may melody carry unfamiliar words? | Calendar, procession, choir roles, festival garments |
| Naming / initiation | Does a name change, gain a ritual form, or acquire restrictions on use? | Investiture, new insignia, a register entry, a sponsor's role |
| Translation / teaching | Is a translation worship, commentary, study aid, or some combination? | Parallel-page books and labeled editions can embody unequal status |

- Never derive an entire community's private prayer practice from its formal liturgy. Conversely, vernacular petition does not prove that every canonical formula is freely substitutable.
- Track **recitation accuracy**, **reading ability**, **comprehension**, **composition**, and **authorization** as different competences; a single “knows sacred language” flag loses the useful distinctions.
- Keep comprehension graded and individual: a fluent scholar, memorizing child, skilled cantor, and recent convert need not occupy the same linguistic position.

## 6. Priestly cants as closed registers, not automatic secret languages

- Design heuristic: make an access ledger with separate rights to **hear → repeat → understand → perform → teach → revise**. These are independent permissions, not a mandatory rank ladder.
- Define the gate: lineage, initiation, apprenticeship, ordination, literacy, patronage, examination, restricted setting, or a combination. Do not silently make ancestry and linguistic competence identical.
- Define the code's extent: a few names, a technical lexicon, special discourse patterns, restricted texts, or a fully productive language; the last requires a much larger learning and transmission ecology.
- Define the boundary mechanism: social sanctions, teacher control, restricted manuscripts, professional dependency, or ritual prohibitions. Mere difficulty is not proof of deliberate secrecy.
- Define leakage: public refrain, overheard terminology, circulating manuscripts, expelled specialists, translations, commercial recordings. Closure requires maintenance, not omnipotence.
- Define outsiders' knowledge carefully: knowing the lexical meaning of an invocation may not confer ritual authority; being authorized may not imply complete grammatical analysis.
- Keep a public face: officiants can explain a rite in ordinary speech while reserving procedural knowledge. A contrast between accessibility and specialist responsibility need not be cynical deception.
- Separate professional jargon from exclusionary ideology: technical precision can be useful; who gets to acquire it is a different question.
- Historical caution: Vedic school access in Larios (2017) is evidence about particular institutions; it does not establish that Sanskrit as a whole is a secret priestly cant.
- Research gap: this entry does not establish a particular historical substitution-cant inventory. Any named “secret priest language,” its vocabulary, and its initiation restrictions remain `(to-verify)` until supported by tradition-specific evidence.

## 7. Bounded comparative cases: language, sacred narrative, symbol, era, dress, names

### A. Vedic Sanskrit and contemporary transmission in Maharashtra

| Dimension | Usable reference knowledge |
|---|---|
| Language family / writing | Sanskrit is Indo-European, Indo-Iranian, Indo-Aryan; Vedic is earlier than Classical Sanskrit. Oral transmission is central; no modern script should be projected back as the original vehicle of the hymns (Jamison & Brereton 2014) |
| Mythic / pantheon core | The Rigveda contains hymns to deities including Agni, Indra, and Soma; divine address, praise, offering, and poetic performance are coupled. Do not replace Vedic relationships with a timeless modern Hindu pantheon (Jamison & Brereton 2014) |
| Symbolic system | Accent, sequence, and recitation technique make sound a cultivated sign of continuity; the fire/offerings setting is not equivalent to every later temple ritual (Jamison & Brereton 2014; UNESCO 2008) |
| Era model | Separate ancient composition, later textual/ritual transmission, and Larios's contemporary schools. A modern teaching practice is evidence of modern transmission, not proof that every ancient institution was identical |
| Costume / material signature | Couple recitation to trained bodies, teaching space, books, and ritual implements; exact school dress, sacred-thread rules, and their chronology are `(to-verify)`. Do not make one modern Brahmin ensemble an ancient pan-Indic uniform |
| Naming practices | Distinguish deity names/epithets, attributed seers, patrons, and living students' personal names. Hymnic attribution is not a census or a complete personal-name grammar; local student naming practice is `(to-verify)` |

- **What a worldbuilder can take:** unusually stable sound can be reproduced through intensive embodied teaching while ordinary languages, scripts, institutions, and clothing change.
- **Provenance caution:** Larios (2017) concerns selected Maharashtrian schools and their Brahmin male trainees. UNESCO's accessible summary incorrectly says Vedic derives from Classical Sanskrit; use it for described preservation techniques, not historical linguistic chronology or undated survival counts.

### B. Latin-rite Catholic worship and the 1963 reform text

| Dimension | Usable reference knowledge |
|---|---|
| Language family / writing | Latin is Indo-European, Italic; Latin-language worship and Latin script are not the same variable. A vernacular can use Latin script without becoming Latin |
| Mythic / theological core | Incarnation, Christ's death and resurrection, Eucharist, and salvation organize the liturgical account; saints and Mary are not a competing pantheon in Catholic doctrine (Second Vatican Council 1963, §§5–8, 47, 102–104) |
| Symbolic system | Word, bread/wine, gestures, sacred time, and ministerial roles work together; sacred language is only one component of the semiotic system (1963, §§7, 24, 30, 47) |
| Era model | Do not compress Latin's earlier vernacular history, later learned uses, and twentieth-century reform into one event. In 1963, §§36 and 54 both preserve Latin and allow an expanded vernacular role under authority; “the council banned Latin” is false |
| Costume / material signature | Modern Roman norms identify alb, stole, and chasuble with differentiated functions; vestment colors articulate the liturgical year. This is documented modern prescription, not evidence of identical medieval or early Christian dress (GIRM §§335–346) |
| Naming practices | Separate personal/baptismal names, saints' calendar names, and Latin written or liturgical forms. A dated regional baptismal-name corpus is needed before asserting name frequencies or mandatory saint-name rules `(to-verify)` |

- **What a worldbuilder can take:** an institution can authorize translation while retaining a prestigious source language, clerical roles, a ritual calendar, and visibly differentiated garments.
- **Provenance caution:** conciliar and missal texts prescribe practice; they do not measure every parish's behavior. Latin versus vernacular cannot stand in for every doctrinal, political, or jurisdictional difference.

### C. Qur'anic Arabic, Egyptian Arabic, and modern institutions

| Dimension | Usable reference knowledge |
|---|---|
| Language family / writing | Arabic is Afroasiatic, Semitic; Qur'anic/Classical Arabic, modern formal usage, and Egyptian vernaculars must not be collapsed into one unchanging spoken code. Arabic script also serves languages outside the Arabic family (Ferguson 1959; Haeri 2003) |
| Mythic / theological core | Islamic monotheism and Qur'anic revelation ground the sacred-language issue; prophetic exemplars are not a pantheon. Haeri investigates the social consequences of the text-language's authority, not the truth of revelation |
| Symbolic system | Recitation, schooling, textual correction, and the prestige of authorized wording distribute authority; sacred and state-literary domains can overlap without being identical (Haeri 2003, especially chapters 2–4; Burt 2003) |
| Era model | Separate the early revelation context from later grammatical institutions and Haeri's modern Egyptian setting. Modern newspapers, schools, and copy editors are active mediators, not mere fossils of seventh-century speech |
| Costume / material signature | Use books, classrooms, and print production as documented material anchors. An exact Cairene religious-professional or prayer ensemble is `(to-verify)`; no pan-Islamic robe should be treated as a universal certificate of Arabic competence |
| Naming practices | Arabic naming can combine personal names, ancestry links, honorifics, and affiliation names; their local deployment varies. Schimmel is a comparative reference, not a current Egyptian frequency table; edition details and any imported name-form examples are `(to-verify)` |

- **What a worldbuilder can take:** a sacred code can also be a contested language of citizenship, publication, education, and prestige; printers and schoolteachers can matter as much as ritual specialists.
- **Provenance caution:** Haeri's “custodianship” is an analysis of social relationships, not a universal Muslim psychology. Burt (2003) praises the ethnography but challenges several transliterations, translations, and syntactic analyses; do not copy those examples unchecked. Sacred Arabic is not thereby a closed priestly cant.

### D. Muscovite liturgical reform and the Old Believer context

| Dimension | Usable reference knowledge |
|---|---|
| Language family / writing | Church Slavonic belongs to Indo-European, Slavic, through the South Slavic Old Church Slavonic tradition; its Muscovite recension used Cyrillic. It is neither simply old everyday Russian nor a language invented by the reformers |
| Mythic / theological core | Christian salvation history and Orthodox liturgy provide the shared sacred frame. Meyendorff (1991) also examines the political-religious project of Moscow as “Third Rome”; do not reduce all dissent to that one explanation |
| Symbolic system | Service-book wording and rubrics authorize bodily action; rival editions can become evidence of fidelity. Compare books and actions together, rather than treating ritual as detachable pronunciation trivia |
| Era model | Focus on mid-seventeenth-century reform. Meyendorff argues that claimed ancient correction actually relied on contemporary Greek and South Slavic editions; restoration rhetoric and source history need separate timelines |
| Costume / material signature | Printed service books and ministerial vesture situate the register materially; exact pre-/post-reform local garment differences are `(to-verify)`. Do not derive a complete sect costume from a textual dispute |
| Naming practices | Keep clerical or monastic names, secular identities, and polemical group labels distinct. Edition-specific divine-name spellings and personal-name distributions require direct pre-/post-reform witnesses `(to-verify)` |

- **What a worldbuilder can take:** a revision within a shared liturgical tradition can become a boundary of allegiance; neither a new language nor a new pantheon is required.
- **Provenance caution:** Meyendorff's interpretation should be checked against the actual editions and dissenting testimony. This entry's coverage of Old Believer subgroups, costume, and local naming is thin; do not invent a single uniform community or a one-cause schism.

## 8. Schism linguistics: build a causal chain, not an accent stereotype

- Design chain: **contested authority → disputed textual/performance change → institutional enforcement or refusal → public boundary marker → divergent transmission**. A linguistic difference can become diagnostic without being the original cause.

| Dispute axis | Design question | Cross-system consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Recension | Which manuscript or printed edition counts as authoritative, and why? | Rival provenance stories, printers, book ownership, and donation networks |
| Translation | Does the disputed wording change doctrine, participation, jurisdiction, or perceived continuity? | Teacher demand, new publishing markets, competing naming glosses |
| Pronunciation / orthography | Is a difference a sound change, spelling convention, correction, or theological sign? | A repeated small feature becomes an allegiance test; do not invent its historical attestation |
| Rubric / speaking role | Who may utter a passage, at what volume, in what sequence? | Gesture, access to space, vesture, gender/office expectations, ritual training |
| Calendar / commemoration | Which events and names enter the authorized yearly cycle? | Festival clothes, naming pools, memorial books, market rhythms |

- Model both sides' custodial claims. “Traditional” can mean an inherited local recension, a newly recovered witness, a recently imposed standard, or a remembered practice.
- Counterexample to linguistic determinism: the 1963 Catholic text permits vernacular expansion within the institution's own approval structure; translation is not definitionally schism.

## 9. Era staging and an executable fictional-culture worksheet

| Conditional stage, not universal sequence | Language / institution move | Myth, symbol, and costume move |
|---|---|---|
| Founding or retrospective founding narrative | Establish an exemplar: revelation, ancestor speech, poet, council, or teacher lineage | Decide which origin claim legitimizes words and objects; distinguish believed origin from documented date |
| Reproduction | Endow teachers; standardize selected texts or performance rules | Books, ritual tools, initiation signs, and calendar repetitions teach recognition |
| Expanding constituency | Add translations, glosses, multilingual instruction, or traveling specialists | New communities can retain an emblem while changing local materials and clothing |
| Institutional competition | Rival schools, states, patrons, or reformers compete over custodianship | Recensions, insignia, garments, and commemorative names can mark allegiance |
| Reconfiguration | Choose coexistence, reform, revival, fragmentation, or loss of training | Re-date symbols and garments separately; an old-looking ensemble may be newly standardized |

1. Select one rite and one dated institutional setting; list its vernaculars, ritual variety, scripts, and audiences.
2. State the authority claim and the claimed efficacy: correct wording, correct intention, authorized office, comprehension, participation, or a specified combination. These are design choices, not assertions about real metaphysics.
3. Fill the access ledger for a lay participant, novice, officiant, teacher, and editor; record exceptions and who funds training.
4. Choose a conserved linguistic feature and a changing one; derive both through the language's sound/morphological history rather than adding random archaisms.
5. Couple one visible sign, one calendar use, one object, and one naming rule to the register; identify which connection is historical, which is doctrine, and which is merely an outsider's assumption.
6. Stress-test a bilingual child, a convert, a migrant specialist, a copied book, a poor community unable to replace garments, and a contested translation. Record the institution's response without assuming agreement.
7. Stage a reform and its alternative outcome; show the causal path between language, patronage, mythic interpretation, symbol, era, and dress. Do not require all systems to change together.
8. Hand lore-shokunin an **option packet**, not a canon entry: proposed mechanism; permitted variants; chronology dependencies; authority/access ledger; naming and material consequences; sources; uncertainties; decisions reserved to the world-bible owner.

## 10. Provenance controls and checkable editions

- Reject universal progress ladders from “primitive magical speech” to “rational religion”; the cases above show coexistence of learned, ordinary, oral, written, state, and devotional uses rather than a mandatory evolutionary order.
- Contested question: how sound, semantic understanding, emotion, and authority contribute to ritual efficacy. Do not infer “meaningless” from unfamiliar wording or assert that every community values exact sound over comprehension.
- Colonial/outsider-source control: terms such as “jargon,” “corrupt,” “pagan,” or “superstition” can encode an observer's hierarchy. Attribute them, check local categories, and distinguish a missionary's judgment from a linguistic description.
- Chronicle limits: normative liturgies and elite textual witnesses underrepresent domestic prayer, women, learners, excluded groups, and unrecorded variation. Missing documentation does not establish secrecy or absence.
- Verification scope: primary conciliar and GIRM passages, Ferguson's accessible reprint, Burt's review, and linked publisher/catalogue summaries were checked; full scholarly books were not read end-to-end for this entry. Unchecked fine-grained applications remain marked above.
