# Dating Contact Histories: Loanword Strata, Imported Things, and Foreign Names
- topic: Infer relative sequences and bounded dates for fictional contact, borrowing, adoption, and deposition by coupling linguistic, material, and naming evidence without treating them as one clock.
- sources: Campbell (2013); Haspelmath & Tadmor (eds., 2009); Harris (1989, 2nd ed.); Schiffer (1987); Durkin (2014); Spear (2000); Mugane (2015); Brielle et al. (2023); Fujikawa (2017); Gutiérrez (1993). Editions, links, and verification limits below.

## 2. Name the event before trying to date it

| Clock | Event being dated | What is not automatically dated |
|---|---|---|
| Contact | A particular population's encounter with a carrier | First encounter anywhere; migration; political submission |
| Lexical | A word or sense enters a specified speech community | Arrival of its referent; first surviving written token |
| Material | Manufacture, import, imitation, use, repair, or deposition | All of these at once; identity of every user |
| Onomastic | Adoption of a name form, naming rule, or title | Bearer's ancestry, birth, or settlement date |
| Documentary | Composition, copying, revision, or inscription | Story's setting; original wording; age of reused support |
| Ritual | Adoption/reinterpretation of a rite or emblem | Beginning of the religion; unchanged meaning thereafter |

- With forward-running dates, **terminus post quem (TPQ)** means an event cannot be earlier than a bound; **terminus ante quem (TAQ)** means it cannot be later than a bound (Harris 1989).
- A coin issued in year 240 supplies a TPQ for an undisturbed deposit containing it, not a TAQ of 240. Circulation and heirloom use can last much longer.
- A floor independently known to seal a deposit in year 310 supplies its TAQ, provided later intrusion is excluded. The newest secure constituent sets the strongest TPQ, not the oldest one.
- A word securely used in an original document dated 280 existed **by** 280: an upper bound on its entry into that register, not proof it entered in 280.
- If the document survives only in a later copy, distinguish the dated witness from the proposed original. A modernized loanword may date the copyist's vocabulary rather than the narrated event.
- A myth's foreign king marrying a local ancestor is an origin claim; it becomes a chronological anchor only through independently assessable witnesses and synchronisms.

## 3. Build loanword strata, not a list of exotic-looking words

Framework: comparative reconstruction plus contact history (Campbell 2013; Haspelmath & Tadmor 2009; Durkin 2014).
1. Record recipient dialect/register, attested form, reconstructed pronunciation, sense, dated witness, and whether it is quotation, code-switching, or established use.
2. Separate **ultimate etymon** from **immediate donor** and **carrier**. A Persian-derived word carried by Arabic-speaking traders does not establish a Persian-speaking colony.
3. Compare sets of recurring correspondences; resemblance alone cannot distinguish inheritance, chance, borrowing, or a shared earlier loan.
4. Order changes using native words and independently dated evidence before using them to order loans. Otherwise the loan chronology merely repeats its own assumptions.
5. Ask whether a loan underwent a recipient change, reflects a dated donor innovation, or bypassed both through a learned/liturgical channel.
6. Partition technical, domestic, courtly, sacred, and naming registers. They can borrow from the same donor at different times and retain different pronunciations.

| Diagnostic | Conditional inference | Required alternative check |
|---|---|---|
| Loan undergoes a recipient sound change | Entry before that change in this community | Same output through adaptation, analogy, or a neighboring dialect |
| Loan preserves the unshifted input | Entry after the change ceased applying | Learned reborrowing, quotation, archaizing spelling, or dialect immunity |
| Recipient form reflects a donor innovation | Entry after that innovation in the donor variety | Parallel recipient change; mistaken immediate donor |
| Two forms of one etymon differ systematically | Multiple channels or borrowing episodes | Native derivation, spelling convention, or semantic split |
| Loanwords cluster in a craft domain | Contact concentrated in that practice/register | Dictionary selection bias; imported prestige terminology for local goods |
| Native term replaces a foreign label | Purism, translation, or changed prestige is possible | It does not establish withdrawal of the object or people |

“More integrated” does not mean “older” without a demonstrated mechanism: adaptation can occur immediately, while a long-established learned form can remain foreign-looking.
A borrowed *cloth* word can later name a local weave or a different garment. Date the **sense–referent pairing**, not merely the word's oldest ancestor.
**Discredited clock assumption:** classical constant-rate glottochronology does not supply a general absolute date; do not repurpose it as a contact clock (Campbell 2013).
**Unsupported shortcut:** donor percentages do not translate into borrowing dates or migrant population shares; no fixed loanword-per-century rate is licensed.
**Conditional method:** relative loan strata remain useful when supported by recurring sound correspondences, documented senses, and independent anchors.

## 4. Build material biographies and provenance chains

Use Harris's stratigraphic sequence for deposits and Schiffer's formation-process checks for movement between them. A linguistic “stratum” is an analytical grouping, not a physically sealed layer.

| Material observation | Chronological value | Coupling trap |
|---|---|---|
| Securely sourced imported vessel or bead | Establishes movement of an object/material before deposition | Manufacturing origin is not necessarily the trader's language |
| Local kiln waste or workshop offcuts | Establishes local production at that horizon | Local production may retain a foreign trade name |
| Cloth fiber, weave, dye, and cut | Different components can have different histories | Imported yarn + local weave + foreign cut is not one import |
| Foreign clasp on an older robe | Dates clasp manufacture/attachment, not necessarily weaving | Costume can be assembled across generations |
| Maker mark or inscribed personal name | Can link object production to a person/institution | Mark may be a brand, later addition, or imitation |
| Sacred image or foreign crest | Dates a visible form in that context | Symbol transfer does not prove theological conversion |
| Old artifact in a later sealed assemblage | Residual survival or deliberate curation | Never force its early date onto the whole assemblage |

Record manufacture → shipment/re-shipment → acquisition → alteration → use → discard/ritual retirement → excavation; attach dates only where evidence exists.
For costume, preserve separate fields for material origin, maker's technique, silhouette, fastening, wearer's label, and ritual interpretation.
Date short-lived associated samples where appropriate, with calibration and association checks; marine reservoir effects and old wood can offset apparently precise dates. Brielle et al. (2023) explicitly flag marine-diet effects in coastal chronology.
An absent perishable garment is weak negative evidence. A claimed disappearance needs recovery conditions and a relevant, sufficiently sampled corpus—not just an empty display case.

## 5. Read foreign names through institutions, not ancestry

The local `naming-systems.md` supplies slot inventories; this file adds chronological tests. Keep endonyms, exonyms, personal names, titles, place names, and divine epithets distinct.

| Pattern | Candidate process | What would discriminate it |
|---|---|---|
| Foreign given name + local lineage name | Conversion, patronage, fashion, intermarriage | Baptism/grant record; family linkage; naming cohort |
| Imported title attached to local names | Borrowed office or prestige style | Charter creating office; change in institutional duties |
| Foreign names mainly in port records | Trading diaspora or selective registration | Domestic records, residence evidence, writing-language comparison |
| Old name pronounced through a newer sound system | Continued transmission of an older name | Earliest spellings and known scribal substitutions |
| Foreign-looking place-name layer | Settlement, renaming, transferred name, or exonym | Dated local forms, settlement sequence, language of record |
| Dynasty claims foreign sacred descent | Legitimacy narrative, possibly with historical ancestry | Independent genealogical, archaeological, and contextual evidence |

- A name may be inherited unchanged, translated, restored, or newly fashionable. An archaic name does **not** independently date a family's settlement.
- One person can appear under a local name, baptismal name, office, and foreign transcription. Resolve co-reference before counting “incoming foreigners.”
- A theonym in a new alphabet dates that written representation. It need not date the deity's arrival; an old local deity may receive a foreign equation or epithet.
- Separate three possible transfers: **shape** of emblem, **name** of emblem, and **ritual explanation**. A later priestly gloss can make an old imported motif appear indigenous or primordial.

## 6. Triangulation workflow and rival explanations

1. Define locality, social group, date system, and target event: e.g., “adoption of a named court garment,” not “when this civilization began.”
2. Make a ledger: `ID | observed datum | source/witness | dated event | bound/interval | assumptions | dependency group | alternative explanation | status`.
3. Build language, object, and name sequences independently before comparing them; mark any borrowed date or shared text as a dependency.
4. Draw an event graph: borrowing precedes attestation; manufacture precedes deposition; an authenticated personal grant precedes use of that granted name. Do not add edges just because forms look similar.
5. Compare route hypotheses: direct contact, intermediary trade, artisan migration, conversion, elite imitation, inherited survival, or later revival.
6. Intersect bounds only for evidence demonstrated to concern **the same event**. Maintain separate intervals for first trade, name adoption, and costume popularization.
7. Test apparent contradictions through later copying, semantic drift, heirlooms, intrusive objects, dialect mixture, and false identification before inventing an undocumented invasion.
8. Remove the strongest anchor and widen every uncertain one: report what ordering survives and which absolute date collapses.
9. Hand off candidate histories, counterevidence, and unresolved checks. lore-shokunin selects/records canon; this method does not.

| Candidate contact mechanism | Expected coupled traces, not mandatory laws | Useful counter-test |
|---|---|---|
| Trade without political takeover | Goods/measure names, selective imports, port naming networks | Continuity of local administration and domestic vocabulary |
| Conversion network | Ritual loans, devotional objects, additional sacred names | Coexistence of local clothing and earlier rites |
| Artisan movement | Production vocabulary and operational techniques, not just finished goods | Workshop evidence and apprenticeship continuity |
| Court imitation | Prestige labels, imported regalia, foreign-style names at elite sites | Distribution outside courts; local manufacture under imported labels |
| Revival or antiquarian restoration | Old-looking forms alongside contemporary production/copying | Modern materials, explicit restoration records, chronological inconsistencies |

Triangulation is not a license to multiply confidence scores. A chronicle naming a ruler, an artifact dated by that ruler-list, and a loan dated by that artifact may be **one circular argument**, not three independent confirmations.

## 7. Comparative setting A — Swahili coast, differentiated towns and periods

| Coupled system | Reference profile and dating lesson |
|---|---|
| Language family + writing | Kiswahili is Bantu, within Sabaki; Arabic loans do not turn it into an Arabic descendant. Distinguish Arabic inscriptions from Swahili written in Arabic-derived Ajami (Spear 2000; Mugane 2015). |
| Myth/religious core | Islamic monotheism, not a pantheon; customary (mila) practice and spirit-possession complexes are documented alongside dini Islam (Spear 2000, reviewing Middleton and Giles). A specific town's pre-Islamic narrative corpus is not established by the sources inspected for this entry. |
| Symbolic system | Arabic writing, mosque/tomb forms, and prestige objects participate in status and religious expression; do not assign every imported ornament an Islamic meaning. |
| Era model | Late-first-millennium coastal settlements and exchange; expanding medieval urban/Islamic institutions; differentiated Portuguese and later Omani-era interventions. Site phases must not become a single coast-wide conversion date. |
| Costume/material signature | Local weaving, beadworking, and iron production coexist with imported glazed pottery and other prestige goods (Spear 2000). Medieval garment cuts and dye-to-rank assignments are not documented in the sources inspected for this entry; later kanga evidence is not a medieval uniform. |
| Naming practices | Arabic personal-name forms, *bin* patronymics, origin labels, and Shirazi descent narratives can signal affiliation/status. Their presence is not a census of newly arrived Arabs or Persians. |

Loanword bridge: Arabic vocabulary in law, trade, and religion must be stratified by dialect and period; treating the present lexicon as a snapshot of medieval borrowing is unsafe (Spear 2000; Mugane 2015).
Material bridge: Spear's Shanga discussion combines local continuity, imported ceramics, mosque rebuilding, and changing production; he notes differences in dating and periodization between Horton's own reports, so the Shanga site report (Horton 1996) should be checked before reusing phase dates as fine anchors.
Naming bridge: a Shirazi origin claim is evidence about remembered/claimed descent; neither dismiss it as inherently fictional nor accept its voyage and dynastic dates without corroboration.
**What a worldbuilder can take:** a locally rooted port society can acquire foreign ritual words, imports, and elite name styles on different schedules without population replacement.
**Provenance cautions:** colonial archaeology overweighted exotic goods and foreign founders. Spear (2000) is a critical historiographic baseline, not the final word on ancestry.
Brielle et al. (2023) find African–Asian ancestry mixtures and infer contact/mixture by about 1000 CE, complicating blanket dismissals of Persian connections; their mostly elite, geographically uneven samples and demographic models cannot date individual loans, prove every pedigree, or equate ancestry with language.
Early chronicle testimony is sparse and often retrospective; the Kilwa Chronicle's sixteenth-century written form is not a contemporary witness to all earlier events (Brielle et al. 2023). Local ritual traditions and non-elite dress are not established by the sources inspected for this entry.

## 8. Comparative setting B — Japanese Nanban/Kirishitan contact

| Coupled system | Reference profile and dating lesson |
|---|---|
| Language family + writing | Japanese belongs to Japonic, not to the donor's family. Kanji/kana writing coexisted with mission printing in Latin script and Japanese characters (Fujikawa 2017). |
| Myth/religious core | Buddhist and kami traditions were already present; Catholic teaching introduced another institutional narrative system. “Shinto versus Christianity” must not imply a timeless, internally uniform pair. |
| Symbolic system | Crosses, rosaries, Marian images, and IHS emblems enter local media and production. A copied sacred image can be locally made while carrying a transregional devotional form. |
| Era model | Sixteenth-century trade contact; Xavier's mission from 1549; late-sixteenth-/early-seventeenth-century mission institutions; suppression and underground transmission. Political policy and cultural survival have different endpoints. |
| Costume/material signature | Nanban paintings represent European outfits alongside Japanese dress; imported images and Chinese silk moved through Portuguese/Jesuit networks. A painted costume is not proof of an excavated garment or widespread local wear. |
| Naming practices | Japanese names coexist with Christian ones: Hosokawa Gracia and Naitō Julia are examples discussed by Fujikawa. A baptismal name records religious/social affiliation, not Portuguese birth. |

Loanword bridge: *kirishitan* derives from Portuguese *cristão* (Fujikawa 2017; etymon also given in the 世界大百科事典 entry 「キリシタン」, checked 2026-10-17). Its etymon identifies a contact channel; its exact first local attestation and community-specific adoption date are not established by the sources inspected for this entry.
Material bridge: silk acquired through Macao is not therefore Portuguese-grown silk. Split producer, financier, shipper, and linguistic intermediary (Fujikawa 2017).
Naming/symbol bridge: conversion can add a Christian name and devotional objects while leaving local kinship and dress conventions intact; investigate individuals rather than generating a mandatory “convert costume.”
Survival test: Fujikawa describes underground prayer traditions and copied Christian texts after suppression. A prohibition cannot serve as a hard TAQ for every loan, sacred name, or object.
**What a worldbuilder can take:** an imported name–ritual–object bundle can travel through selective institutions, then outlive the institution publicly licensed to transmit it.
**Provenance cautions:** Jesuit published letters could be edited for reputation; compare manuscripts, hostile texts, Japanese sources, and excavated contexts rather than treating all accounts as neutral (Fujikawa 2017).
Gutiérrez (1993) supplies useful image descriptions but uses dated East/West hierarchies and missionary-centered judgments; the article is Jesuit-authored and its closing note says the journal's staff added the captions, so its attributions, costume identifications, and caption dates are not tied to catalogue records (article inspected 2026-10-17). This entry does not reconstruct regional kami myth cycles from mission literature.

## 9. Era staging for a fictional culture

The following is a designer's comparison grid, not a universal progress ladder. Phases can overlap, reverse, or differ between port, capital, shrine, and countryside.

| Candidate phase | Language/name layer | Material/costume layer | Myth/symbol layer |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intermediary encounter | Trade jargon; distant exonyms | Rare re-exported objects | Exotic motif with little doctrinal knowledge |
| Repeat exchange | Commodity/measure loans; port bynames | Repairs and local fittings for imports | Prestige story attached to object origin |
| Specialist establishment | Craft vocabulary; professional titles | Workshops and apprentices; mixed techniques | Patron cult or production taboo translated |
| Institutional appropriation | Court/ritual register; bestowed names | Regalia, dress permissions, local replicas | Emblem receives official genealogy |
| Domestic diffusion | Ordinary senses, native derivatives, popular names | Local substitutes; cheaper silhouettes | Imported rite gains local explanations |
| Rupture and survival | Purism, concealment, inherited name pools | Heirlooms, confiscations, hidden objects | Polemic, secret practice, reinterpreted symbols |
| Revival | Learned reborrowings, restored spellings | Reproductions assembled from old models | Claim of unbroken antiquity; authenticity disputes |

Stage households and institutions separately: a court can use foreign titles while market speech retains native garment words. Unevenness is evidence to explain, not an error to erase.
Keep competing era labels: a dynasty's “Restoration” may coincide with an archaeologist's new production horizon and a linguist's period of intensified borrowing.

## 10. Worked dating exercise — explicitly invented, not canon

**Toy assumptions:** all years share a forward-running fictional calendar; source objects are authentic and contexts undisturbed. A recipient /p/ → /f/ change finished in year 120 and ceased being productive; subsequent loans can retain /p/. Donor /p/ persisted, and comparative sets exclude immediate /p/ → /f/ adaptation and dialect mixing.

| Independent toy observation | Licensed inference |
|---|---|
| Cloth word *fala* corresponds to donor *pala* and shares the native change | Cloth-word borrowing precedes 120, conditional on the stated exclusions |
| Belt word *pata* retains donor /p/ in a later recipient register | Belt-word borrowing follows 120, conditional on that channel and sound history |
| An original inventory of year 152 uses *pata* for the relevant belt type | That word–sense pairing existed by 152; entry is bracketed after 120 and by 152 |
| A sourced clasp belongs to a donor production series made in 146–150 | Its manufacture is within that interval; its eventual deposition cannot precede 146 |
| An original funerary tag identifies a local person by a foreign sacred name individually granted in 148 | Tag use/deposition cannot predate the grant; the name does not imply foreign ancestry |
| The burial containing tag, clasp, and cloth is independently sealed by construction in 155 | Burial deposition is no later than 155; intrusion and later tag addition have been excluded |

- Burial package: TPQ `max(146, 148) = 148`; TAQ `155`; hence **148–155**, not “146–150” and not automatically “152.”
- Belt-word entry: **after 120, by 152**. This is a different event and interval; do not intersect it with burial deposition to manufacture a false date.
- The old cloth label can be used for newly woven cloth in the burial. Its early borrowing date neither dates those fibers nor proves imports already existed before 120.
- Candidate H1: a recent direct mission supplied sacred names and imported belts. Candidate H2: names arrived through a mission, but clasps came through an unrelated merchant intermediary.
- Both fit the observations. Distinguish them with shipping accounts, sponsor links, workshop evidence, or object-specific documentary associations; do not choose H1 because it is narratively tidy.
- Sensitivity: remove the name grant and the burial bracket widens to 146–155; remove the secure sealing event and no finite TAQ follows from the remaining evidence.
- If the grant survives only in a late copy or the clasp is intrusive, downgrade the corresponding bound before recomputing. Never average incompatible dates to conceal a contradiction.

## 11. Handoff audit, what a worldbuilder can take, and provenance cautions

Hand off a **reference packet**, not a new world-bible assertion: target event; locality/register; phase graph; loan strata; object biographies; name-slot histories; source/witness ledger; rival routes; sensitivity results; unresolved checks.
- [ ] Every proposed date says which event it dates and whether it is a bound, estimate, or independently anchored interval.
- [ ] Every etymology separates immediate donor, ultimate source, possible intermediaries, and certainty; sound chronology rests on more than one convenient word.
- [ ] Every imported costume feature separates fiber/weave/dye/cut/fastening and manufacture/import/alteration/deposition.
- [ ] Names have dated witnesses, institutional context, and co-reference checks; neither names nor objects are silently equated with ethnicity.
- [ ] Mythic genealogy, devotional affiliation, emblem shape, and theological explanation are separate claims with separately dated evidence.
- [ ] Dependency groups prevent a text, its archaeological derivative, and its retelling from counting as independent confirmations.
- [ ] Chronological contradictions and archive/preservation biases remain visible; every unresolved particular carries **(to-verify)**.
- [ ] lore-shokunin receives alternatives and confidence limits; no canon or sibling files are written by this method.

**What a worldbuilder can take:** coherent historical texture comes from constrained mismatches—an old cloth word on a new weave, a foreign sacred name on a local lineage, an imported clasp on inherited regalia—not from synchronizing everything into one conquest year.
**Provenance cautions:** these settings illustrate mechanisms, not detachable civilization kits. Fine dating needs original-language and site-specific work; early coastal chronicles, ordinary people's dress, regional ritual histories, and the exact first attestations flagged above remain incomplete here. Keep that incompleteness in the handoff rather than filling it with invented scholarship.
