## 1. Separate the units before drawing an urban–rural map

| Unit/framework | What it explains | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Dialect contact and koineization — Siegel (1985) | Contact among varieties, mixing, leveling, and possible emergence of a shared variety | Every market producing a new language, or an inevitable timetable |
| Accommodation | A speaker adjusts during interaction; adjustments may be selective and temporary | A stabilized community grammar or loss of the home variety |
| Dialect leveling | Reduction of differences among varieties; measure particular features and domains | Complete uniformity, universal grammatical simplification, or cultural assimilation |
| Register and enregisterment — Agha (2007) | Activity/audience-specific repertoires; features become recognizable social types through circulation and commentary | One permanent way of speaking for every resident of a place |
| Social networks — Milroy (1987) | Density, multiplex ties, and local norms help explain maintenance and change | “Rural = closed”; an urban neighborhood can have very dense ties |
| Written standardization | Institutions select spellings, genres, and authorized forms | Everyone speaking alike, reading, or accepting the institution’s prestige |

- A **market koine** is a hypothesis about a durable shared variety, not a synonym for bargaining vocabulary. Repeated formulas alone can be an occupational register.
- A **lingua franca** is defined by use across groups; it can be a pre-existing language. A koine concerns formation through variety contact; neither term implies a pidgin or creole.
- Geography, wealth, ritual authority, occupation, age, gender, and mobility are separate coordinates. “Court,” “town,” and “village” must not become three rungs on one ladder.
- In particular, costume vocabulary can converge while accent remains local; pronunciation can level while ritual names remain restricted. Coupling requires a mechanism, not simultaneous change by decree.

## 2. Map encounters, not concentric circles of civilization

| Contact site | Likely interaction pressure: design hypothesis | Costume/name/myth coupling to test | Counterpressure |
|---|---|---|---|
| Periodic fair | Reusable greetings, prices, measures, and repair requests | Sellers translate local cloth names into sale categories; shrine-day names organize attendance | Visitors return to separate networks; little shared child acquisition |
| Permanent market quarter | Frequent transactions across origins and trades | Product grades, credit reputations, shop identifiers, festival merchandise | Residential or occupational segregation preserves repertoires |
| Court or administrative seat | Gatekeeping through address, document genres, ceremonial expertise | Authorized garment and office names distinguish eligibility from mere possession | The court’s repertoire may have little prestige in workshops or villages |
| Village council or ritual association | Local knowledge, kin relations, precedents, and office matter | A household’s garment entitlement and public naming privileges may be contested locally | Rural residents include dependents, landowners, travelers, and officeholders, not one class |
| Port, garrison, or construction camp | Diverse adult speakers need coordination | Loanwords for equipment, uniforms, supplies, or unfamiliar textiles | Adult work-site accommodation need not reach household speech |
| Apprenticeship and service migration | Long residence joins work, lodging, and peer ties | Migrants learn trade terms and situational address without necessarily adopting elite dress | Hiring chains from one origin can reproduce a regional enclave |
| Pilgrimage route | Recurrent travel connects distant settlements | Sacred epithets, tokens, distinctive garments, and reputational bynames circulate | Ritual language may be retained precisely because ordinary speech changes |
| Return migration | Mobile speakers re-enter local relations | A shop term can become fashionable, pretentious, or useful at home | Returners can accentuate local speech to reclaim belonging |

These are construction prompts, not predictions from settlement size. Require a named carrier, repeated encounter, audience, and sanction for each proposed diffusion route (Siegel 1985; Milroy 1987; Agha 2007).

## 3. Migration-driven leveling: model cohorts and features separately

- **Mixing:** migrants bring variants; initial variation is not itself a new stable dialect.
- **Leveling:** some differences lose ground in particular networks. Low frequency is not an automatic death sentence: salient identity forms can persist.
- **Reallocation:** surviving variants can acquire new functions, such as familiar/formal or older/younger usage, rather than retaining their source-region distribution.
- **Focusing:** speakers increasingly share expectations about locally normal forms; institutional codification may follow, precede, or remain separate from this process.
- These processes can overlap. Kerswill and Williams (2000) provide a modern British new-town study, not proof that every ancient market, colonial settlement, or refugee city follows the same sequence.

| Cohort/network | Record in the design sheet | Plausible contrast, not a universal result |
|---|---|---|
| Adult newcomers | Origins, work language, household language, length and intensity of contact | Workplace accommodation alongside persistent home features |
| Locally raised children | Caregivers, peers, school access, neighborhood boundaries | Peer-network norms differ from the parents’ mixture |
| Established urban residents | Their numbers, access to jobs, prestige, and contact with newcomers | A pre-existing variety absorbs innovations rather than being replaced |
| Circular migrants | Repeated routes, seasons, family ties, return visits | Features circulate in both directions without permanent relocation |
| Institutionally separated residents | Exclusion from housing, marriage, schooling, guilds, or worship | Durable multilingualism or dialect differentiation inside one city |

Keep a feature ledger: `feature → source varieties → acquisition setting → distribution by cohort → social evaluation → retained/leveled/reallocated → evidence or design premise`.
Do not assign a universal “one–two generations” clock, percentage threshold, or automatic simplification rule. A fictional timeline must supply its own demographic and interaction premises.

## 4. Court and village costume vocabularies: compare tasks, not sophistication

The following are **English gloss templates for design**, not reconstructed historical word lists. One speaker may command several columns; a garment can have several names without being several artifacts.

| Naming task | Court/ceremonial emphasis | Market/workshop emphasis | Village/household emphasis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Identify the object | Authorized ensemble, rank, occasion | Cut, size, fiber, finish, grade | Use, fit, ownership, inherited local category |
| Assess excellence | Correctness of presentation, provenance, fashionable distinction | Workmanship, dye quality, saleable condition | Durability, reparability, season, suitability for work or ceremony |
| Describe a color | An approved term or allusive name where locally established | Dye/process names and commercial shades | Local distinctions relevant to textiles and use; not inherently fewer terms |
| Count and exchange | Ensemble, gift, allowance, ceremonial entitlement | Piece, length, weight, bundle, credit unit | Household stock, inherited item, loan, repairable remnant |
| Identify a wearer | Office, title, entitlement, etiquette | Customer, maker, supplier, debtor | Kin relation, household, age, local office, event role |
| Handle an old word | Antiquarian or ceremonial retention | Resold, relabeled, or narrowed to a product | Maintained, repurposed, or replaced through local practice |

- Build an **artifact-first term card**: physical referent; local word/form; region; speaker/task; date range; semantic scope; acquisition route; evidence status. Do not infer class from etymology alone.
- Add a **biography**: new cloth → commissioned garment → gift/livery → resale → altered workwear → ceremonial heirloom, choosing only transitions the fictional economy permits.
- Distinguish object movement from word movement: a purchased robe can keep its maker’s label, acquire a local label, or be classified under an older term.
- Native/borrowed is not automatically humble/luxurious. A borrowed trade name may become ordinary while a native ceremonial term remains prestigious.
- Village dress can encode fine status differences; court terms can be broad outside specialist settings. Ooms (1996: 132–133) directly couples public surname privileges, dress restrictions, and address distinctions.
- A sumptuary prohibition is evidence of a rule and its classification, not a census of compliance. Specify jurisdiction, exceptions, enforcement, and the difference between purchase, possession, and public wearing.

## 5. Couple sacred language, visible symbols, and names without homogenizing them

| Layer | Question to answer | Register-ecology consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Mythic narrative | Who tells which origin, foundation, or saint/deity story, to whom? | A court genealogy and a village foundation story need not be competing versions of one text |
| Ritual authority | Who may utter a divine name, lead a procession, or name a garment? | Shared commercial vocabulary can coexist with restricted sacred vocabulary |
| Symbolic interpretation | Who recognizes a motif, and from what teaching or experience? | A merchant can know a design’s sale name without mastering its ritual or poetic interpretation |
| Material production | Who grows, spins, dyes, weaves, repairs, and sells? | Rural producers may command distinctions absent from elite consumer speech |
| Personal naming | Which name is used in a petition, household, shop, initiation, or funeral? | Migration changes audiences; it does not necessarily erase earlier names |
| Public display | Who can wear a mark, title, crest, color, or office ensemble? | Name privileges and costume privileges can reinforce one another without being identical |

- **Enregisterment test:** identify the joke, etiquette lesson, theatrical persona, correction, or other commentary that makes a feature recognizable as “courtly,” “provincial,” or “market” (Agha 2007). Characters’ evaluations are not the narrator’s linguistic facts.
- Symbol travel does not prove belief travel. A festival pattern sold across regions may be decorative for one purchaser and devotional for another; treat that as a hypothesis requiring audience evidence.
- No obligatory “old gods survive only in villages” rule. Urban cults can be innovative or conservative; rural ritual organizations can be institutionally literate and internally stratified (Ooms 1996, chapters 1 and 4).

## 6. Era staging: change the contact regime, not the civilization’s essence

| Staging condition | Language possibilities | Dress/myth/name possibilities | Evidence needed before treating the coupling as historical |
|---|---|---|---|
| Seasonal congregation | Formula-sharing without a community koine | Fair, pilgrimage, and harvest-event vocabularies circulate | Recurrence, attendance routes, intermediaries, local genres |
| Growing permanent town | Accommodation and unstable mixtures | More product labels and resale categories; prestige competition | Migration composition, residential patterns, labor recruitment |
| Established mixed peer networks | Some shared local norms; possible reallocation | New urban identity can coexist with inherited ritual affiliations | Cohort evidence, not just older writers complaining about youth |
| Court or bureaucratic consolidation | Selected written and ceremonial norms | Titles, garment entitlements, official name forms become auditable | Dated rules, actual petitions/accounts, exceptions and enforcement |
| Expanded transport and schooling | Wider face-to-face contact plus institutional standard pressure | Regional commercial terminology expands; uniform categories may spread | Who actually travels, studies, serves, or is excluded |
| Heritage revival or political rupture | Selected old forms are revalued or newly codified | “Traditional” garment labels and origin stories can be reorganized | Earlier attestations versus retrospective claims of antiquity |

These conditions are branches and overlays, not a mandatory progression. One polity can contain all of them; use several clocks: migration, schooling, garment production, religious reform, and state naming policy.
Print or broadcasting alone is not a sufficient explanation for conversational leveling; spell out acquisition and interaction routes rather than treating media as a magic homogenizer.

## 7. Bounded comparison: Tudor/Stuart England and London, approximately 1500–1700

| System | Reference anchor | What the urban–rural comparison must preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Language family + writing | English is Germanic within Indo-European; Latin-alphabet manuscript and print practices; Latin remains relevant in learned/institutional domains (Nevalainen and Raumolin-Brunberg 2003) | Do not treat English spelling as a transcript, or neighboring Welsh and Scots histories as mere rural English variants |
| Mythic/religious core | Christian sacred history and Trinitarian theology; saints and Marian devotion are crucial to Duffy’s pre-Reformation account (1992) | Saints are not a pantheon of independent gods; Reformation changes are dated institutional conflicts, not “urban reason replacing rural superstition” |
| Symbolic system | Apparel, household affiliation, and ceremonial presentation participate in status display (Vincent 2003) | The same garment category can appear in different ranks; details, setting, access, and observers matter |
| Era model | Political reign boundaries do not coincide neatly with religious, linguistic, and dress changes | Duffy’s 1400–1580 coverage cannot substantiate all seventeenth-century practice |
| Costume/material signature | Tailored and layered dress; doublets, hose/breeches, gowns, headwear; wool, linen, and luxury textiles in differentiated uses (Vincent 2003) | Elite clothing evidence is not a complete rural wardrobe survey; exact county-specific workwear terms are not supplied by the cited sources |
| Naming practices | Given names and hereditary surnames; local, occupational, nickname, and personal-name surname histories (Reaney and Wilson 1991) | A surname’s occupational origin does not prove its bearer’s current trade; forms of address are a separate system |
| Contact mechanism | London’s changing role and individuals’ language histories are studied through correspondence (Nevalainen and Raumolin-Brunberg 2003) | Metropolitan diffusion is feature-specific; neither “the court invented standard English” nor “London was one market koine” follows automatically |

- **What a worldbuilder can take:** separate court access, urban employment, parish affiliation, household service, and regional origin. Let a migrant acquire a trade vocabulary without making their ritual repertoire or dress entitlement identical to their employer’s.
- **Provenance cautions:** surviving letters privilege people with access to writing; drama’s rustic speech is stylization, not an audio archive. Vincent’s elite focus and Reaney/Wilson’s dictionary scope cannot supply unrecorded village conversations or naming frequencies.
- Duffy’s interpretation is an influential revision of older Reformation narratives, not a politically neutral consensus on every locality. Specific local continuities, motivations, and rates of change require additional evidence beyond the cited interpretation.

## 8. Bounded comparison: Tokugawa Japan, 1603–1868

| System | Reference anchor | What the urban–rural comparison must preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Language family + writing | Japanese belongs to Japonic; mixed kanji/kana writing and different literary/vernacular practices (Frellesvig 2010) | Chinese-derived vocabulary or script does not make Japanese genetically Chinese; literacy and text genre are additional variables |
| Centers and varieties | Edo, Kyoto, and Osaka are distinct reference centers, not one homogeneous “city” | Exact feature histories and an Edo market-koine reconstruction are outside the verified material here; do not back-project today’s Tokyo standard |
| Mythic/pantheon core | Buddhist memorial practice and local protective-deity associations coexist in Ooms’s cases; his Mino discussion names *ujigami* affiliations (1996, chapters 1 and 4, pp. 214–215) | This is a plural ritual ecology, not an exhaustive national pantheon; local foundation myths, deity narratives, and their diffusion are not covered by the cited sources |
| Symbolic system | Seasonal/poetic motifs, color, and decoration can communicate meaning; the V&A discusses cranes as longevity/good-fortune imagery | A museum’s explanatory synthesis does not prove every maker or wearer decoded every motif identically |
| Era model | Shogunal/domain governance with changing local class and status relations (Ooms 1996) | “Tokugawa” is not one unchanging costume or linguistic period; Slade’s treatment used here is chiefly late-Edo and later |
| Costume/material signature | Robe-and-sash clothing; pattern, textile quality, accessories, and occasion differentiate ensembles (Slade 2009; V&A n.d.) | The V&A’s garment FE.101-1982 is dated 1800–1850: do not use it to reconstruct all seventeenth-century village clothing |
| Naming practices | Restrictions concern public surname use, not simply whether peasants possessed surnames; privileges and exemptions matter (Ooms 1996: 132–133) | “Only samurai had surnames” erases the public/private and status distinctions documented in the source |
| Village institutions | Local class conflicts and regulations shape visible distinctions; Mino examples connect elite groups, protective deities, marriage boundaries, and display (Ooms 1996: 214–215) | A village contains competing statuses and interests; its ceremonial vocabulary is not a simplified urban vocabulary |

- **Dated lexical anchor:** Tanoguchi’s 1681 village rules restricted *haori*, *hakama*, *ashida*, and umbrellas for clients promoted to titled-peasant status. A separate 1844 case reported in the same section restricted *kamishimo* while allowing main-mourner and groom exceptions (Ooms 1996: 177–178). These are terms in translated legal evidence, not counts of actual wearers or proof of unchanged garment forms.
- **What a worldbuilder can take:** connect permission to sign a public name, use an address form, display an ensemble, and participate in a ritual office; then model disagreements and exceptions, not a perfectly synchronized caste chart.
- **Provenance cautions:** Ooms’s case concentration, especially in Shinano, is explicit; Mino comparisons are not every village. His conquest/colonial-regime analogy is an interpretive argument, not a replacement for period-specific evidence.
- Chronicle coverage of ordinary multilingual or multidialectal encounters is uneven; petitions are mediated written acts, not verbatim phonetic records. Women’s everyday garment vocabulary and precise regional pronunciations are thinly covered here.

## 9. Apply the method to a fictional culture: option packet, not canon

1. Draw a **mobility graph**: settlements, markets, service destinations, pilgrimage sites, transport costs, seasons, and return routes; record coercion or exclusion rather than assuming voluntary movement.
2. Draw a separate **authority graph**: court, temple, guild, village council, landlord, school, and household. Prestige must have an audience and a consequence.
3. Select contact cohorts and features from section 3; distinguish pronunciation, grammar, vocabulary, address, spelling, and document genre.
4. Choose a garment’s real construction and material constraints before creating its register variants; record whether labels denote the same object, different grades, or different ensembles.
5. Specify mythic authority and symbol interpretation independently: storyteller, officiant, designer, seller, and purchaser may have different knowledge.
6. Give mobile people audience-dependent name forms where appropriate; separate lawful public identification from intimate address, nicknames, trade identifiers, and religious names.
7. Stage changes by contact conditions, not a fixed generation clock; allow an unchanged ritual term, an innovative village form, and a conservative urban enclave.
8. Produce alternative outcomes and their premises; send them to lore-shokunin with evidence labels and unresolved questions. Do not choose a world-bible truth in this entry.

| Hypothetical option | Language outcome to propose | Costume/symbol outcome to propose | Premise that would defeat it |
|---|---|---|---|
| Repeated fair, little permanent residence | Shared sale formulas; home dialects maintained | Common commercial cloth label alongside local ceremonial names | Extensive mixed peer residence would require a different acquisition model |
| Long-resident mixed neighborhood | Candidate shared local variants, some socially reallocated | A new neighborhood style using differently understood inherited motifs | Persistent segregation or strong older urban norms could change the outcome |
| Court monopoly with strong village institutions | Document/ceremony register distinct from several spoken varieties | Official ensemble names coexist with technically rich household and workshop terms | No mechanism for teaching or enforcing the supposed monopoly |

These are conditional methods, not an invented civilization, approved chronology, lexicon, pantheon, or costume canon.

## 10. Stress tests and calibration failures

| Failure signature | Calibration status | Repair |
|---|---|---|
| Every urban resident sounds courtly; every rural resident sounds rustic | Unsupported settlement essentialism | Cross-tabulate locality, occupation, status, cohort, and situation |
| A village is automatically an archaic linguistic and religious refuge | Unsupported universal; countered by locally changing institutions in Ooms’s cases | Give every location maintenance and innovation mechanisms |
| A market must create a pidgin, then a creole, then a standard | False mandatory life cycle | Diagnose the actual varieties, multilingual practices, acquisition, and domains |
| All migrants lose all source features at the same speed | Unsupported deterministic leveling | Track features and networks; allow retention and reallocation |
| A color word supplies a universal sacred meaning | Invalid inference | Separate lexical contrast, dye/material, motif interpretation, and audience |
| A court term is inherently more precise than a village term | Deficit framing, not a linguistic finding | Compare tasks and referents; include producers’ technical distinctions |
| A chronicle’s pejorative group or costume label becomes a neutral category | Source-bias risk, especially in colonial or elite outsider records | Attribute the label, seek self-designations, and record whose voice is missing |
| A sumptuary rule proves universal compliance | Norm/practice confusion | Look for exceptions, disputes, accounts, objects, and enforcement records |
| One modern new-town study supplies a universal timetable | Unlicensed analogy | Transfer mechanisms conditionally; obtain demographic and interaction evidence |

Scene checks: can the same traveler bargain, commission a repair, attend a village ceremony, and petition an official using different repertoires without changing ancestry? Can a locally powerful household police names and display without being urban? Can a migrant’s child sound local while retaining a different ritual affiliation?
For every successful scene, preserve the mechanism and its evidence status; for every gap, return a question or alternative to lore-shokunin, not an invented historical fact.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take — and provenance cautions

- Take the **relational machinery**: mobility routes, network boundaries, acquisition settings, competing institutions, object biographies, and audiences that reward or punish variants.
- Couple language, myth, symbol, era, and costume through named practices: selling cloth, signing a petition, narrating a foundation, displaying an entitlement, or returning home. Similarity without a carrier is not an explanation.
- Keep fictional proposals explicitly conditional. Real-world evidence establishes possibilities and constraints; it does not authorize a transplanted civilization or decide canon for lore-shokunin.
- Verification scope is uneven: bibliographic records identify the linguistic works; the author page explains the correspondence evidence; Ooms’s specified passages and the V&A essay support the bounded examples. This is not a cover-to-cover or exhaustive regional source audit.
- No historical village word list, measured leveling rate, or exact Edo pronunciation is supplied. Those require dated, localized attestations, not reconstruction from costume pictures or modern dialect stereotypes.
- This comparison does not establish African, South Asian, Indigenous American, or other urban–rural histories. Those traditions need their own language, writing, ritual, material, naming, and source histories; the present coverage is too thin to generalize to them.
- Use `(to-verify)` items as research tasks, not as validated evidence. Distinguish source observation, scholarly interpretation, and this entry’s design hypotheses in every handoff.
