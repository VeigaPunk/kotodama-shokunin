# Trade-Port Contact Workshop — Shared Speech, Mixed Things, Unequal Histories

- topic: designing a possible trade-port trajectory from intermittent contact through stabilized contact speech to an expanded community vernacular, coupled to myth, symbols, era, naming, and material culture without treating creolization as inevitable
- sources: Salikoko S. Checkable links and limits appear in §12.

## 1. Calibration: a workshop branch, not a universal life cycle

- **REFERENCE** denotes scholarship or an attested comparison; **DESIGN** denotes the invented port exercise; **UNKNOWN** denotes missing evidence, marked `(to-verify)` when a historical claim is contemplated. These are provenance labels, not canon permissions.
- **REFERENCE:** contact intensity is not a creole-production dial. Access to particular speakers, network structure, mobility, coercion, and transmission matter; high multilingualism can maintain several languages rather than generate a new one (Mufwene 2001).
- The older sequence “jargon → pidgin → creole → post-creole” is a historical model, not a required trajectory or settled definition. A prior reduced pidgin is not demonstrated for every creole; expansion can occur among adult second-language users. See Mufwene (2001) and the author-hosted overview, §2–3.
- Use `language-contact.md` for its contact repertoire, but do **not** import its §7.1 nativized-pidgin definition as a universal premise. Here, nativization is a transmission development to document, not a magic grammatical switch.
- “Mixed” below means multiple historical inputs unless explicitly called a **mixed language** in the technical sense. A creole is not automatically a technical mixed language; a borrowed garment does not prove either linguistic outcome.
- **Contested:** creole genesis, genealogical classification, and proposals for a distinctive creole structural prototype remain debated. “Broken language,” racial inheritance of grammar, and a universal baby-talk origin are not acceptable explanatory defaults. Nor are all theories of universals thereby disproved (Mufwene 2001).
- **Design boundary:** the donors, grammatical forms, rituals, and outfits below are disposable teaching variables, not a proposed civilization or world-bible entry. No real tradition's deity, sacred name, or costume is being reconstructed.

## 2. Outcome menu: choose the social history before the label

| Outcome | What must be specified | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| Ad hoc contact jargon | recurring tasks but unstable conventions; repair by gesture, translation, repetition | a community grammar or obligatory next stage |
| Stabilized pidgin | shared norms for intergroup use, conventionally acquired as an additional language | permanent lexical poverty or inability to expand |
| Expanded pidgin | use spreads into more domains among fluent additional-language users | that children alone supplied its grammar |
| Creole/community vernacular | specific formation history and community transmission, often including first-language acquisition | one universal structure, ancestry mixture, or mandatory pidgin precursor |
| Borrowing or language shift | an existing language persists with additions, or speakers adopt another language | a new creole simply because contact was intense |
| Koineization/code-switching | dialect leveling in a contact population, or multilingual alternation with its own norms | that alternation is random, deficient, or already a distinct mixed language |
| Technical mixed language | a conventionalized system with substantial contributions from different languages; establish the particular distribution | a fifty-fifty word lottery, or merely a market lingua franca |

- **REFERENCE:** the menu distinguishes processes that can coexist; it is not a set of watertight demographic bins. A language called “Pidgin” may also have native speakers; local names and scholarly categories need separate fields (Mufwene 2001; Michaelis et al. 2013).
- **Attested contrast, not civilization profiles:** Tok Pisin's trade, labor, and later community uses demonstrate changing domains; Michif demonstrates a different, conventionalized contact outcome. Neither licenses an invented pantheon or national outfit.

## 3. Build the contact-ecology ledger

Adapt Mufwene's **ecology/feature competition and selection** approach: list the variants speakers actually encounter and the networks through which variants become recurrent. The ledger below is workshop tooling, not a predictive formula.

| Variable | Questions to answer | Consequence to test, not presume |
|---|---|---|
| Frequency and duration | seasonal visits, daily crews, permanent residence; who returns? | repeated interaction can stabilize conventions; turnover can keep them variable |
| Network topology | brokerage monopoly, mixed work teams, segregated wards, overlapping peer groups? | a few central bilinguals may select forms without population-wide bilingualism |
| Linguistic access | whose dialects are heard at home, on ships, at court, in workshops? | the effective lexifier need not be the largest population's language |
| Power and safety | free movement, debt bondage, enslavement, exclusion, punishment for speech? | constrained access and disrupted transmission differ from voluntary trade |
| Care and peer transmission | who cares for children, in what language; what do peers use? | mixed parentage alone predicts neither a shared language nor heritage-language loss |
| Domains and authority | language of wages, disputes, worship, jokes, mourning, schooling? | prestige is domain-specific; a cargo language may lack ritual authority |
| Mobility and newcomers | return workers, refugees, seasonal spouses, new recruitment routes? | a portable lingua franca may spread beyond the original port |
| Material and media access | looms, tailors, dye supply, seals, writing tools, interpreters? | words, objects, scripts, and ritual authority may travel through different networks |

- Record separate contact cards for A–B, A–C, and B–C, then for each workplace/household network; a port-wide “high contact” score conceals the mechanism.
- No numerical threshold or fixed number of generations follows from these sources. State the enabling event and its observable consequence rather than assigning a universal creolization date.

## 4. DESIGN input: a cool, wet estuary port

Assume sail-and-river cargo, seasonal storms, narrow handlooms, needle-sewn panels, and costly imported sheet metal. No factories, mass schooling, or cheap print exist unless a later branch introduces their infrastructure. These are linked constraints, not a global “civilization level.”

| Donor variable | Language family and structure | Writing and language access | Costume/material signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: resident cargo brokers | invented family A, unrelated to B/C by stipulation; SVO, relatively analytic | alphabet on fiscal tablets and tags; literacy concentrated in broker offices | sewn bast-fiber tunics; panel seams and cargo seals |
| B: estuary households | invented family B; SOV with suffixes; internally varied speech | no dedicated script in this exercise; oral genealogies and specialist interpreters | locally woven wraps, wooden toggles; particular unbleached sashes restricted to mourning |
| C: mobile clothworkers | invented family C; VSO and agreement morphology; includes /f/ and clusters | consonantal script on account labels, optional vowel signs for ambiguity | wool shoulder wraps and sheet-metal clasps; imported wool and metal remain costly |

| Donor variable | Mythic/pantheon core: a design option | Symbolic distinctions | Era model and naming practices |
|---|---|---|---|
| A | oath-witness cult alongside household rites; no claim that everyone worships one god | knotted cord marks a witnessed debt, not universal “truth” | charter/recharter periods; personal name plus office byname in accounts |
| B | obligations to particular dead kin and river-threshold custodians; no unified pantheon required | an unbleached mourning sash differs from ordinary pale cloth | remembered flood/relocation periods; household links and restricted ancestor names |
| C | route guardians and return narratives; guild rites do not exhaust belief | clasp design marks an apprenticeship affiliation, not biological ethnicity | journeys and workshop successions; personal name plus changing workshop affiliation |

- These traits are independent choices: SOV does not cause ancestor rites, an alphabet does not cause bureaucracy, and wool does not imply a particular pantheon. Include dissenters, class differences, and specialists within each donor.
- Decide who already shares a second language. If everybody can use it for every relevant task, explain why a new contact norm would be adopted instead of assuming necessity.

## 5. Era staging: events, not compulsory evolutionary rungs

| Stage | Enabling event in this branch | Speech/writing observable | Material/ritual observable | Alternative branch |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| E0: before regular contact | separated networks with occasional brokerage | donor repertoires and exonyms already exist | objects cross boundaries without local reproduction | interpreters remain sufficient indefinitely |
| E1: returning crews | repeated seasonal A–B–C transactions | formulas, competing pronunciations, visible repair | imported wraps worn alongside local clothing | one donor language is learned instead |
| E2: resident work wards | shared stores, wage routines, cross-group apprenticeship | stable trade norms; adults transmit them to newcomers | local recutting of foreign cloth; copied fasteners with changed uses | trade code remains narrow while homes retain separate languages |
| E3: expanded neighborhood use | caregiving and peer networks adopt the contact variety | narratives, disputes, kin terms, hypothetical talk; some children acquire it first | locally taught dress practices and new civic rites, with continuing separate rites | expanded second-language lingua franca without widespread nativization |
| E4: competing institutions | council, temple, school, or printer acquires resources | optional orthographic reform and status struggle | uniforms, festival rules, or heritage revivals become contested | oral stability without standard spelling; shift, continuity, or further divergence |

- Walk E1→E3 below because that is the chosen exercise, not because the other branches are failures. E4 is optional. Do not silently replace generational evidence with a neat calendar.
- Preserve asynchronous eras: the shared work cape may precede the community vernacular; script adoption may lag both; mourning rules may resist change throughout.

## 6. Walkthrough: from contact intensity to stabilized use

1. **E1 transaction:** an A broker, B boat crew, and C clothworker exchange wraps and cargo space. List essential acts: identify goods, count, reject quality, promise delivery, specify a recipient, repair misunderstanding. Establish which participants interpret; do not begin with a random multilingual dictionary.
2. **Track variants:** keep an attestation-style worksheet: `{speaker network, donor variety, form, meaning, task, era, competing form}`. For fiction these are designed observations, not historical evidence. Let different crews initially use different counting or rejection formulas.
3. **Choose the lexical center:** in this branch recurring broker routines favor many A forms, while B navigation/care vocabulary and C textile vocabulary remain useful. This licenses A as the principal lexifier, not A ownership of all grammar, prestige, or identity.
4. **Repair specific sound sequences:** a C wrap term `/frak/` is adapted in the selected contact norm as `/paraka/`: /f/→/p/, /a/ separates the cluster and follows final /k/. Record that order and those environments; do not announce universal “pidgin simplification.”
5. **E2 stabilization event:** resident mixed work teams repeatedly train newcomers with the same recipient marking and wrap term. Demonstrate comprehension across teams without the original broker. Common conventions plus permitted variation count as stabilization; a council decree alone does not.
6. **Keep unresolved variation:** C bilinguals may retain /f/ in donor-language conversation, while the contact form has /p/. B speakers may retain SOV in home-language clauses. These are repertoires, not errors to erase from the whole population.
7. **Expand adult uses first where appropriate:** shared repairs, meals, wage disputes, and humor require more than cargo formulas. Add constructions through documented usage choices; adults can innovate and stabilize them. Do not hold expressive thought hostage until children arrive.
8. **E3 transmission event:** specify overlapping homes, caregivers, and peer groups that make the contact variety a daily vernacular. Some children acquire it first; others acquire B or C first and the contact variety later. Record multilingual continuities, not automatic heritage loss.
9. **Prove domain expansion:** give the variety ways to discuss an absent person, a disputed oath, a conditional promise, and a narrated past. No single marker is a certificate of “becoming creole”; the transmission history and expanded usage are the evidence package.
10. **Reclassify cautiously:** describe the result as a stabilized, expanded contact vernacular; under the chosen transmission history a creole analysis is available. The toy's multiple inputs do not make it a Michif-type mixed language.

## 7. DESIGN language ledger: make the branch reproducible

This is a small demonstration, not a complete conlang or a structural template for real creoles. Each source label A/B/C refers only to the invented donor variables.

| Feature | Selected norm | Designed route and constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Core phonology | /a e i o u/; /p t k m n s l r w/; syllables (C)V(n); penultimate stress | recurring contact speech selects this inventory; not the mathematical intersection of donor inventories |
| Lexical adaptation | C `/frak/` → contact `/paraka/`, initially 'shoulder wrap' | apply the E1 repairs before later semantic change; prestigious donor pronunciations can coexist |
| Clause order | subject–verb–object | select an A-compatible recurrent pattern; B/C speakers' other languages remain different |
| Noun phrase | noun–numeral; possessum `ma` possessor | A `kali` 'two' and `ma` 'of'; preserve this ordering in samples |
| Core roles | A `na` 1SG, `ta` 2SG, `lu` 3SG; `pati` 'give'; `ti` GOAL, `ka` LOC | `lu` is singular and ungendered; the same phonological form does not license arbitrary additional functions |
| Negation/question | A `se` NEG before the predicate sequence; clause-final `ke` polar question | stable choices for this toy; establish actual scope before adding modals |
| Later time reference | A `pala` 'earlier' → `pa` ANT; A `nima` 'tomorrow' → `ni` FUT | explicit lexical reduction/reanalysis options during expansion; do not invent an exact historical rate |
| Later social reference | B `mu` inclusive 'we'; exclusive plural left to a later design pass | require repeated use across networks before calling it a community norm; morphology need not come only from A |
| Embedding | A `koma` 'say', reduced `ko` introducing a reported clause | a designed reanalysis route, not proof of substrate transfer or a universal creole complementizer |
| Protected terms | B `senu` denotes the restricted mourning sash, not all pale clothing | borrowing the label grants neither ritual access nor knowledge of restricted names |

- E2 clause schema: `S (se) V O (ti Recipient) (ka Location) (ke)`. E3 toy extension: `S (se) (pa/ni) V O ...`; ANT = anterior to reference time, FUT = future. `pa` and `ni` are alternatives in this limited exercise, not a universal prohibition on stacking markers.
- Later clausal reporting uses `S koma ko [finite clause]`. Retain the subject inside the reported clause; decide quotation versus report conventions before adding deictic shifts. Full relative/conditional constructions remain a required design pass, not secretly completed here.
- In the grammaticalization worksheet record lexical, ambiguous, and grammatical uses separately. A resemblance plus a plausible story is a design derivation, not an attested etymology (Mufwene 2001; APiCS surveys show language-specific trajectories).

## 8. DESIGN utterance checks: forms must obey the ledger

| Era and task | Contact form | Segmentation/gloss | Reading |
|---|---|---|---|
| E2 transfer | `ta pati paraka kali ti na` | 2SG give wrap two GOAL 1SG | You give me two wraps. |
| E2 rejection | `na se pati paraka ti ta` | 1SG NEG give wrap GOAL 2SG | I do not give you a wrap. |
| E2 clarification | `ta pati paraka ti na ke` | 2SG give wrap GOAL 1SG Q | Do you give me a wrap? |
| E3 promise | `lu ni pati paraka ti na` | 3SG FUT give wrap GOAL 1SG | That person will give me a wrap. |
| E3 prior event | `lu pa pati senu ti na` | 3SG ANT give mourning.sash GOAL 1SG | That person gave me a mourning sash before the reference time. |
| E3 reported promise | `lu koma ko ta ni pati paraka ti na` | 3SG say COMP 2SG FUT give wrap GOAL 1SG | That person says that you will give me a wrap. |

- The sash sentence is grammatically well formed but may describe a ritual violation: **grammaticality ≠ social permission**. Produce separate elder, outsider, and workshop readings before presenting it as ordinary speech.
- These are constructed examples, not quotations from Tok Pisin, Michif, or any historical creole. Do not represent contact speakers by misspelling English; show their language through consistent forms and translation.
- Negative controls: `paraka` obeys the selected syllable pattern; donor `/frak/` does not unless explicitly marked as switching. `lu` cannot mean 'they' plural without an additional change entry. A polite register is not created merely by adding a foreign word.

## 9. Material culture: follow routes, makers, and changed value

Use Thomas's **entanglement** perspective: exchanged things acquire new uses and values within unequal relationships. This does not imply formerly pure cultures or symmetrical exchange (Thomas 1991). The ledger is a design adaptation, not an ethnographic finding.

| Artifact route | E1 coexistence | E2 local transformation | E3 stabilization evidence |
|---|---|---|---|
| C wrap → shared work cape | imported wool wrap; C term adapted as `paraka` | A-style seams join locally available bast panels; B-style wooden toggles replace costly metal | cross-group apprentices learn the same cut; the term survives despite changed fiber and shape |
| C metal clasp | scarce import worn by some prosperous clients | local substitutes and copied designs; donor makers may contest affiliation marks | distinguish material, construction, right to a mark, and generic fastener name |
| B mourning sash `senu` | outsiders can identify it without permission to wear it | repair services cross groups while ritual custody stays restricted | transmission within particular households persists despite shared street speech |
| A debt cord/seal | brokerage instrument, not a neutral shared emblem | crews adopt a modified marker for completed work | objects and testimony show the new use; shrine use remains a separate question |

- For every redesign record `{fiber origin, loom width, cut, seam, fastener, labor, price/access, wearer/occasion, name, era, maker authority}`. “Hybrid costume” without this production trail is merely a collage.
- Separate technological transfer, imported object, local imitation, and symbolic appropriation. One garment can exhibit all four, but each needs its own route; a loanword alone cannot distinguish them.
- Date lexical and material change separately: `paraka` can broaden from imported wrap to local work cape; an old temple inventory can retain the earlier meaning. Object dates do not automatically date the word's first borrowing.
- Mixed clothing is neither a new ethnicity nor evidence of a creole language. Shared speech can coexist with sharply differentiated wedding/funeral dress, and common dress with continuing linguistic division.

## 10. Myth, symbol, naming, and writing: permit selective convergence

Stewart & Shaw (1994) make the politics of religious synthesis a necessary check: who calls a practice “mixed,” who rejects that label, and who can enforce purity claims? Use **syncretism/anti-syncretism** as questions, not a universal recipe.

| System | DESIGN port move | Boundary or disagreement to preserve |
|---|---|---|
| Myth and cult | a civic safe-return rite borrows an A witness formula and a C route narrative | B households may attend without equating their dead kin with either cult; coexistence is not merged theology |
| Symbols | a modified debt cord marks completion of a communal quay repair | cargo proof, civic obligation, and sacred oath remain distinct readings until an institution connects them |
| Costume and symbol | the work cape uses shared construction but avoids a restricted sash configuration | prohibition concerns material/form/occasion, not a universal rule that pale cloth means death |
| Personal names | office/workshop bynames enter port records while home names remain in use | two entries can refer to one person; hereditary surnames require a separate legal/transmission decision |
| Place/divine names | rival exonyms coexist; a translated cult epithet can circulate independently of a protected name | translated epithet ≠ identical deity, and shared name ≠ shared doctrine |
| Writing | A-office clerks initially spell contact speech with their own alphabet; C labels remain in another script | stable speech does not require standardized spelling; later literacy needs teachers, media, access, and political consent |

- Keep an era- and audience-indexed symbol ledger: `{sign, interpreter, material context, claimed meaning, opposing reading, authority, date}`. A useful design grid must not erase multivocality or force every ward into one closed alignment matrix.
- For names keep `{person/place, endonym, exonym, record form, pronunciation, permitted speaker, era}`. Preserve sound adaptation and special-register exceptions; do not treat any donor's names as freely interchangeable decorative syllables.
- If a council declares the work cape “ancient port dress,” record that as an institutional claim and compare it with the staged construction history. Shared identity can be retrospective without making the clothes or language fraudulent.

## 11. Workshop audit and lore-shokunin handoff

- **Causal test:** each selected feature has a donor variant or innovation, an actual network of transmission, and an era. Neither donor population percentage nor garment resemblance stands in for the network.
- **Language test:** check sound repair, pronoun reference, word order, negation, recipient versus possessor, and time reference against the samples; add narrative and conditional constructions before claiming a complete usable language.
- **Social test:** specify who uses the vernacular at home and at work, who does not, who gains or loses interpretive power, and which older languages continue. Do not describe coercion as cheerful cultural fusion.
- **Material test:** a maker can explain the shared cape's construction and supply; a trader can explain its price; a household can identify occasions on which not to wear it. Outsiders need not agree on its symbolism.
- **Era test:** no printed orthographic standard without printing or a specified manuscript bureaucracy; no mass uniform without supply; no renamed garment silently retaining all its earlier meanings.
- **Counterfactual test:** remove permanent residence, shared apprenticeship, or peer transmission in turn. Record which language/material developments still happen; this catches a falsely automatic coupling.
- **Handoff packet:** contact cards, domain/transmission map, feature/variant ledger, glossed samples, artifact routes, symbol disagreements, name/script concordance, era dependencies, alternatives, and an open `(to-verify)` list. Label every fictional item DESIGN.
- The packet describes options, not an approved city, language name, ethnicity, event, deity, or costume law. Lore-shokunin chooses and records canon; kotodama supplies generative method and reference limits.

## 12. What a worldbuilder can take — and provenance cautions

- **Take:** recurrent networks instead of word percentages; separate timelines for language, objects, belief, and legal identity; innovations alongside donor survivals; productive grammar instead of “broken speech”; material biographies instead of a blended national costume.
- **Mufwene (2001):** the standard book above is also listed in the bibliography of his [author-hosted “Pidgin and Creole Languages” overview](https://mufwene.uchicago.edu/pidginCreoleLanguage.html), especially §2–3. The webpage labels its encyclopedia publication 2002; do not silently use that label as the book's date. Its ecological account is a scholarly position, not the last word on every genesis dispute.
- **Michaelis et al. (2013):** [APiCS Online](https://apics-online.info/) supplies the print/online bibliographic identities. Use its feature comparisons to resist a universal creole checklist; selected traits illustrate possibilities, not a frequency-based recipe for a new language.
- **Tok Pisin comparator:** APiCS [survey 22, §2–4 and §6](https://apics-online.info/surveys/22) traces trade/labor circulation, later domain expansion, regional variation, native and additional-language use, and grammatical constructions. Its labor history includes recruitment and kidnapping; it is not a model of wholly voluntary merchant contact. Counts and education-policy descriptions are historical to the survey, not present-day estimates.
- **Michif comparator:** APiCS [survey 75, §1–4 and §6–10](https://apics-online.info/surveys/75) explicitly distinguishes a mixed language from pidgin/creole and describes broadly French-derived noun phrases and Plains Cree-derived verbal structure. That summary is not a universal noun/verb split rule, a claim that all Métis speak one variety, or permission to copy Métis identity. The survey itself discusses phonological disagreements and scarce early written attestations.
- **Thomas (1991):** [publisher record](https://www.hup.harvard.edu/books/9780674257313) verifies the work's identity. Take the exchange/appropriation problem, not a generalized “Pacific aesthetic”; this workshop supplies no culture-specific Pacific clothing reconstruction.
- **Stewart & Shaw (eds., 1994):** [publisher DOI landing page](https://doi.org/10.4324/9780203451090) identifies the collection. Take the synthesis/purity politics questions, not the proposition that all religions converge when languages do. No claim about a particular living rite is established here.
- **Evidence limits:** early port records disproportionately preserve colonial officials, merchants, missionaries, legal categories, and outsiders' transcriptions. Sparse chronicles are not proof of absent household speech, oral traditions, women artisans, or native innovations. Check whose terminology a source records and what language/genre it can actually attest.
- **Thin-knowledge boundary:** no detailed historical pantheon, naming taboo, sacred textile, or initiation practice from the compared communities has been verified in this entry. Any proposed reconstruction of those particulars remains unverified in this entry and needs dated community-specific scholarship plus appropriate living-community perspectives; do not fill the gaps with generic “syncretism.”
- **Verification/recheck:** bibliographic identities and the accessible source material linked above were checked on 2026-10-17; this is not a full audit of every cited book. Recheck original editions and community/era-specific evidence before converting any analogy into a historical claim or a canon candidate. Restricted knowledge remains restricted even when a colonial source prints it.
