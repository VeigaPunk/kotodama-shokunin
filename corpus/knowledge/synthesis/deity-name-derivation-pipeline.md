## 1. The working model: distinguish referent, name, and occasion

- **Parker’s naming-practice approach:** ask who names which god, for whom, where, and in which medium. A dictionary gloss is not an account of a cult; a translation is not automatically a theological merger.
- **Cult epithet / epiclesis:** a designation used to specify or address a divine power in a worship context. An epithet occurring in poetry is not thereby an independently attested cult title.
- **Historical-comparative method:** establish forms, sound correspondences, morphology, and transmission before reconstructing a name. Resemblance, shared divine functions, and priestly wordplay are different kinds of evidence.
- **Typed onomastic network:** the practical record proposed here, not an ancient or universally accepted taxonomy. Store naming assertions as contextual relationships rather than making one master synonym list.

| Record | What it identifies | What must not be silently inferred |
|---|---|---|
| Cult-reference ID | A locally and temporally bounded worship context | One objectively separate supernatural being |
| Name-form ID | Script form, transliteration, pronunciation, language, era | One spelling has one pronunciation or one referent |
| Epithet occurrence | Head name, modifier, genre, place, officiant | Every epithet creates a new god or names the same cult everywhere |
| Identification assertion | “A is B,” asserted by a named actor in a dated source | All communities accept A = B |
| Use rule | Address/reference, speaker, audience, occasion, permission | A written name is pronounceable by every reader |
| Material witness | Inscription, seal, image, garment, inventory | An attribute supplies the historical etymology |

## 2. Derivation pipeline: inputs → operations → deliverables

1. **Bound the witness:** choose place, community, era, genre, and source language. Separate a hymn’s composition horizon, its surviving manuscript date, and the period it claims to describe.
2. **Choose the cult problem:** shrine patronage, seasonal petition, dynastic legitimacy, household protection, or another practice. Mythic role is one input, not a mandatory “god of X” slot.
3. **Classify the starting expression:** inherited proper name, ordinary noun, agent noun, participial title, place-derived adjective, relational phrase, borrowed name, or deliberately unanalysed form.
4. **Derive it grammatically:** select stems, compounding rules, agreement, case, and address forms from the fictional language. Keep literal morpheme gloss separate from idiomatic title translation.
5. **Apply a dated linguistic history:** ordinary sound changes, borrowing substitutions, lexical replacement, script reform, and analogical reshaping. An exceptional sacred pronunciation needs a transmission institution, not a free exemption.
6. **Assess transparency by audience:** record what an ordinary speaker, trained singer, bilingual merchant, and temple philologist can each analyse. Include “unknown,” not an invented root.
7. **Build the epithet repertoire:** identify the shrine, function, relation, praise register, and permitted combinations; distinguish recitation formulas from administrative labels.
8. **Add contact assertions:** identify who borrows, translates, equates, or refuses to equate the name. Store direction and date; do not overwrite either local record.
9. **Add avoidance rules:** specify forbidden operation, trigger, eligible speaker, replacement, exceptions, enforcement, and evidence. Predict what changes in speech, writing, and personal names separately.
10. **Couple the visible apparatus:** connect name-bearing inscriptions, divine attributes, dress, officiant access, festival, and material supply. Mark whether the link is attested, inferred, or a fictional design proposal.
11. **Run scenarios:** petition, foreign dedication, taboo breach, archival quotation, investiture, and shrine dispute. Produce alternative outcomes where doctrine or evidence leaves identity unresolved.
12. **Hand off:** deliver the derivation chain, use matrix, contact graph, era table, and uncertainty register as proposals. lore-shokunin accepts or rejects world facts; this entry does not establish any.

## 3. Transparent versus opaque: a relation, not an age ranking

| State | Diagnostic | Historical anchor / craft use |
|---|---|---|
| Lexically transparent | Speakers recognise an ordinary lexeme or productive derivation | Ugaritic `bʿl`, “lord/owner,” can function as a title or divine designation; context distinguishes uses, not capitalization in an English edition (Pardee 2002; Rahmouni 2008) |
| Transparent epithet, opaque head | Modifier parses while head has no secure contemporary analysis | A usable civic or saving epithet need not disclose the origin of Athena or another head name (Parker 2017) |
| Learned transparency | Scribal analysis uses obsolete vocabulary, another language, or sign values | Babylonian name exegesis is evidence of scholarship and theology, not automatically of historical word formation (Bottéro 1977; Lambert 2013) |
| Opaque after lexical replacement | Name survives but the ordinary source word is replaced | Retention can produce opacity without irregular sound change; useful fictional mechanism, not a dating rule |
| Opaque loan | Local speakers adapt the sound but not the meaning | A newly imported god may have the least transparent name in the region |
| Re-transparent reinterpretation | A later familiar word supplies a new explanation | Record the interpreter and date; a myth explaining a name can be etiological narrative rather than linguistic reconstruction |

- **Do not use “old gods opaque; young gods transparent” as evidence.** A productive title may remain intelligible for centuries; an imported name can be opaque on arrival. Opacity alone establishes neither age nor substrate origin.
- **Do not derive powers from glosses:** “lord” describes a relationship; it does not prove a universal sovereignty portfolio. A title can become a stable designation without losing all its ordinary uses.
- **Cognacy is not interpretatio:** Greek Zeus, Latin Iuppiter, and Vedic Dyauṣ preserve related inherited material; they are not a simple chain of translations from one historical cult into the next (Mallory & Adams 2006).
- A leading `*` marks reconstruction, not manuscript spelling. Preserve original-script witness, editorial transliteration, reconstructed form, and reader pronunciation as separate fields.
- Specific origins proposed for names such as Apollo or Athena require an etymological source and competing analyses; this entry supplies no definitive derivation for them (declared open debt, not concealed uncertainty).

## 4. Epithet systems: grammar plus jurisdiction

| Epithet family | Typical construction | Question it answers | Coupled consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Local / topographic | Locative, genitive of place, place-derived adjective | Which sanctuary or territory? | Rival shrines can share a head name but maintain distinct treasuries and personnel |
| Functional / situational | Agent noun, participle, verb phrase | What help is requested here? | Different offerings or petition formulas without a new genealogy |
| Relational | Possessive, kinship term, community adjective | Whose patron or ancestor? | Household and civic registers need not address the power identically |
| Praise / superlative | Evaluative adjective, repeated title, elevated comparison | What status does the invocation proclaim? | Expansion in public dedications, hymns, and donor display need not prove a new cult |
| Poetic / formulaic | Metrically useful phrase, repeated descriptive formula | Which traditional diction fits this performance? | Singing register can preserve obsolete inflection absent from everyday speech |
| Propitiatory / avoidance | Favorable description or circumlocution | How is a dangerous power approached? | A replacement may coexist with an explicit name rather than abolish it |
| Composite / contact | Native head plus foreign title or a double designation | Which audience or identification is being negotiated? | Bilingual dedication is evidence for a claim, not proof of universal fusion |

- **Greek anchor:** Athena Polias (“of the city”) and Zeus Sōtēr (“Saviour”) illustrate intelligible modifiers; the cult record, not the gloss alone, establishes the occasion and local reference (Parker 2017).
- **Ugaritic anchor:** `rkb ʿrpt`, conventionally “Rider on the Clouds,” belongs to Baal’s epithet repertoire; keep the consonantal reading and the edition’s translation, rather than inventing unrecorded vowels (Rahmouni 2008).
- **Babylonian anchor:** *Enūma eliš* VI–VII culminates in Marduk’s fifty names. Name accumulation can perform theological exaltation and incorporate claims to powers; it is not a transparent list of fifty independently documented cult mergers (Lambert 2013).
- Store an epithet occurrence as `head + modifier + inflection + place + era + genre + user + evidence`; allow an epithet without a surviving head-name occurrence when the source actually does so.
- Decide fictional stack order and selection rules: whether place comes before praise, whether modifiers agree, and which title a petition must include. These are design choices, not cross-cultural constants.
- Test both directions: one epithet may qualify different gods; one god may receive incompatible or context-specific epithets. A shared epithet is not a unique identifier.
- A title becoming a standalone proper name is a possible history; require an intermediate usage trail rather than declaring that all theonyms began as job descriptions.

## 5. Interpretatio: god-equation without a false synonym collapse

- **Interpretatio graeca / romana:** identification or presentation of another community’s divine powers through Greek or Roman naming categories. Tacitus, *Germania* 43, explicitly calls his explanatory identification *interpretatione Romana*; source voice matters.
- Parker (2017) distinguishes transcription, translation, joining, and substitution in cross-cultural naming. Even a shared name can leave offerings, sanctuaries, images, and communities distinct.
- Treat each equation as `assertor → identification → audience`, with place, date, medium, basis, and acceptance status. A trader’s dedication, a royal decree, and an ethnographer’s report have different reach.

| Operation | Linguistic result | Possible institutional result | Evidence needed before a stronger claim |
|---|---|---|---|
| Borrow / transcribe | Foreign name adapted to local phonology/script | Cult imported, visited, or merely mentioned | Ritual or institutional witness, not just the spelling |
| Semantic calque | Title translated morpheme by morpheme or idiomatically | Translation for visitors without cult transfer | Parallel texts and contextual equivalence |
| Functional equation | Foreign deity labelled by a familiar divine name | Selective understanding, useful misunderstanding, or adopted worship | Who makes the equation and which features they select |
| Double designation | Native and foreign labels appear together | Coexistence, negotiated identity, or eventual blending | Repeated local usage; punctuation in modern editions proves little |
| Replacement | One designation displaces another in a particular medium | Administrative standardization, devotion, coercion, or fashion | Dated distribution plus evidence for the proposed cause |
| Rejection / non-translation | Foreign form retained or equation denied | Boundary maintenance or simply a stable borrowed name | Explicit refusal before describing retention as resistance |

- **Do not assume conquest is the only driver:** travel, migration, trade, bilingual worshippers, and local priests can produce identifications. Parker’s account does not warrant a universal top-down imperial renaming program.
- **Do not assume symmetry:** A-community equates foreign B with A; B-community may retain B, equate A differently, or never encounter the assertion.
- **Do not assume transitivity:** an identification A≈B in one port and B≈C in another does not establish A=C across the world. Keep the scopes on both edges.
- Iconographic resemblance can facilitate a naming equation, but an imported crown, weapon, or dress silhouette is not alone evidence of divine identity or linguistic descent.
- For fictional application, offer separate outcomes: translator’s convenience; dual-address sanctuary; priestly theological synthesis; state-sponsored replacement; rejected equation. Each predicts different inscriptions, garments, and conflicts.

## 6. Name taboo: specify the forbidden act, not just “a secret name”

| Restriction | Reshaping mechanism | Expected residue | Warning |
|---|---|---|---|
| Avoid direct address | Title, favorable epithet, kin term | Public prayer differs from narrative reference | Reference may remain unrestricted |
| Restrict pronunciation | Authorized speaker/occasion; substitute reading elsewhere | Stable written name with divergent oral practice | Written survival does not disprove avoidance |
| Restrict inscription | Omission, substitute graph, special script, restricted copying | Graphic variants distributed by medium or institution | A strange spelling needs evidence before being called taboo |
| Avoid a dangerous referent | Propitiatory designation | Favorable names coexist with fearful narratives | Courtesy, praise, and fear cannot be separated from gloss alone |
| Restrict knowledge | Initiatory transmission or concealed designation | Unequal access and explicit transmission claims | A name-power story is not evidence of an everyday prohibition |
| Renew an avoided substitute | Replacement title becomes restricted in turn | Successive naming layers | Euphemism cycling is a fictional option unless local history attests it |
| Avoid a name-bearing derivative | Replace or reshape personal/place names containing it | Possible mismatch between old documents and later name pools | Absence in a corpus alone cannot prove motivated suppression |

- **Directly attested context split:** Mishnah Sotah 7:6 contrasts pronouncing the divine name as written in the Temple with a substitute outside it; Yoma 6:2 describes the congregation’s response to the High Priest’s explicit pronunciation.
- These are rabbinic texts redacted after the Temple period, preserving prescriptions and memories; they are not contemporary transcripts of every Temple service or evidence that pronunciation was always forbidden everywhere.
- **Written name / spoken substitute:** YHWH is the conventional scholarly consonantal representation. Adonai is a substitute used in reading contexts, not the historical etymology of YHWH or simply a new spelling of it.
- Exodus 3:14–15 associates disclosure of the divine name with `Ehyeh`; the JPS 2023 note expressly marks the phrase’s meaning uncertain. Do not promote one English rendering into a settled historical etymology.
- **Propitiatory Greek naming:** distinguish Eumenides (“Kindly Ones”), Semnai Theai (“Revered Goddesses”), and the literary Erinyes by locality and genre; a uniform story that “the Greeks officially renamed the Furies” erases the evidence (Henrichs 1994).
- **No universal name-magic:** reverence, fear, identity protection, ritual correctness, and social hierarchy are different explanations. A Frazer-style single “primitive belief” is not an acceptable substitute for local evidence.
- A fictional rule needs `operation + trigger + speaker + audience + location + permitted substitute + exception + sanction + era`. “Nobody says the true name” is not enough to stage a scene.
- Keep consequences separate: social punishment, supposed ritual failure, and actual supernatural effects are different claims. Only lore-shokunin can authorize the last as world fact.
- Do not manufacture taboo sound changes from odd forms: test dialect, scribal convention, ordinary phonology, borrowing, and damage first. An avoidance-driven alteration needs evidence for both the form and the motive.

## 7. Comparative culture cards: keep every system era-bound

**Mesopotamia — bilingual scribal traditions, not one timeless pantheon**
- Language/writing: Sumerian is a language isolate; Akkadian is East Semitic. Their cuneiform traditions combine logographic and syllabic uses. The divine determinative conventionally transliterated superscript `d` is not an automatically pronounced name prefix.
- Myth/pantheon and symbols: *Enūma eliš* exalts Babylon’s Marduk; horned divine headgear and deity-specific attributes belong to an iconographic system, not a one-symbol/one-god universal key (Lambert 2013; Black & Green 1992).
- Era and material/dress: distinguish third-millennium imagery, Old Babylonian evidence, and first-millennium scholarly copies; tufted or fringed robe imagery must be dated before coupling it to a name. A carved textile surface does not securely identify its fiber.
- Naming: Sumerian Utu and Akkadian Šamaš are associated solar divine names, not cognates created by regular change from one language into the other; titles such as Bēl require contextual identification (Black & Green 1992).
- Take/caution: port bilingual name lists, learned sign-based exegesis, and accumulated praise names. Composition dates, manuscript dates, and ordinary worshippers’ understandings are not interchangeable; the date of *Enūma eliš*’s composition is contested.

**Greek communities — local cult address within overlapping mythic traditions**
- Language/writing: Greek is Hellenic Indo-European; Mycenaean Linear B evidence and later alphabetic evidence are different chronological witnesses, not a continuous unchanged spelling system.
- Myth/pantheon and symbols: Zeus, Athena, and other shared mythic names coexist with local cult addresses; Athena’s helmet/aegis imagery does not explain the etymology of her name (Parker 2017).
- Era and material/dress: separate Mycenaean, Archaic/Classical, Hellenistic, and Roman-period contexts. Peplos, chiton, and himation supply a dress vocabulary, not one uniform national or priestly costume; consult `culture-material/dress-through-eras.md`.
- Naming: transparent civic/function epithets can specify an invocation without supplying a new divine biography. Literary diction, inscribed dedications, and sanctuary usage must be indexed separately.
- Take/caution: port nested local identities and prayer registers. Do not infer cult continuity from a late antiquarian report, or a society-wide taboo from a favorable epithet alone.

**Roman-contact settings — an observer’s mapping is one layer**
- Language/writing: Latin is Italic Indo-European, normally written alphabetically; Greek and local languages coexist in imperial settings. Treat Alcis as a Latin-transmitted form, not a native-script witness to the indigenous name’s grammar or pronunciation.
- Myth/pantheon: Tacitus compares the Alcis with Castor and Pollux and reports that they are venerated as young men and brothers. The Roman divine pair supplies his interpretive category, not a surviving local catechism ([*Germania* 43, Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/tacitus/tac.ger.shtml)).
- Symbols: he reports no divine images at this grove; retain that as his local assertion, not a demonstration that all neighboring peoples lacked images or symbolic systems.
- Era and material/dress: anchor the report to the early imperial literary context. Tacitus describes the presiding priest’s attire as feminine (*muliebri ornatu*, §43); this identifies neither a garment/fiber nor the wearer’s gender identity. Roman tunic, toga, and priestly head-covering practices require separate wearer/rite evidence (Sebesta & Bonfante 1994).
- Naming: local name retention, double designations, and Roman labels can coexist; investigate each occurrence rather than replacing the local name field with a Roman “equivalent” (Parker 2017).
- Take/caution: port asymmetric diplomatic translation and visibly distinct officiant dress. Roman ethnography is elite, external, and shaped by imperial categories; it is not a neutral inventory of other peoples’ beliefs.

**Late Bronze Age Ugarit — title grammar and poetic epithets**
- Language/writing: Ugaritic is Northwest Semitic; alphabetic cuneiform generally records consonantal structure, while syllabic Akkadian also occurs in the city’s written environment. Do not substitute Biblical Hebrew grammar without checking Ugaritic usage.
- Myth/pantheon and symbols: El, Baal, Anat, and Athirat occur in the religious corpus; distinguish narrative relations from offering lists. Baal’s cloud-rider epithet is not a complete definition of his cult (Pardee 2002; Rahmouni 2008).
- Era and material/dress: situate the archives in the Late Bronze Age before the city’s destruction around 1200 BCE. The conventional “Baal with Thunderbolt” stele offers horned-headdress and short-garment imagery; indigenous garment terms and image-to-fiber identifications remain unverified here (declared gap, see Open debt).
- Naming: `bʿl` can be an ordinary title as well as a divine designation. Record local referent, phrase construction, and witness; the English capital “Baal” is an editorial convenience, not a Ugaritic grammatical marker.
- Take/caution: port a productive title plus inherited poetic diction. Court/temple archives and damaged tablets unevenly preserve practice; knowledge here is thin on everyday regional speech and garment nomenclature. Ugarit is not a transcript of all “Canaanite religion.”

**Ancient Israel/Judah → Temple and rabbinic traditions — continuity is not sameness**
- Language/writing: Hebrew and Aramaic are Northwest Semitic; distinguish earlier Hebrew script traditions from later Jewish square-script transmission. A later pointing system is not direct evidence for an ancient everyday pronunciation.
- Myth/religious core and symbols: Exodus’ liberation and divine self-disclosure narratives, covenant, and sanctuary organize the selected textual examples. This does not reduce all historical Israelite/Judaean practice to one uniform theology.
- Era and material/dress: distinguish Iron Age evidence, later scriptural transmission, the Temple destroyed in 70 CE, and Mishnah redaction around 200 CE. Exodus 28:36–38 prescribes an inscribed gold priestly frontlet; it does not describe everybody’s clothing.
- Naming: YHWH in writing, authorized pronunciation, and substitute address can occupy different registers. Sotah 7:6 even connects the divine inscription on the frontlet with a dispute over priestly gesture: name, body, office, and dress are coupled in the text.
- Take/caution: port medium-specific access and exception rules, not a copied sacred name. Normative texts, retrospective ritual accounts, archaeological witnesses, and modern Jewish observances require distinct dates and provenance.

## 8. Era staging: allow overlapping histories, not a universal evolution

| Possible transition | Name-layer change | Myth/symbol consequence | Costume/material consequence to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lexical change | Old name remains while source noun is replaced | A new explanatory myth may reinterpret the name | Old inscriptions remain legible only to specialists |
| Shrine differentiation | Place epithet becomes necessary | Shared myth gains divergent local tellings | Distinct badges or inventories are possible, not inevitable |
| Bilingual contact | Borrowed form, translated title, or equation | A selective analogy becomes available | Combined regalia may appear; require dated witnesses |
| Royal/temple consolidation | Longer praise series or authorized designation | Exaltation or ancestry claims grow | Dedications and authorized display become political surfaces |
| Restriction or reform | Public substitute diverges from restricted address | Correct speech becomes an institutional concern | Name-bearing objects may require licensed handling |
| Antiquarian revival | An old spelling or title is reintroduced | Claimed ancient continuity can mask new interpretation | Reconstructed vestments need not reproduce earlier practice |

- These are optional mechanisms that can coexist or reverse; no “primitive magic → rational religion” ladder is licensed. Name, ritual, iconography, and garment chronologies can move at different speeds.

## 9. Non-canon worked exercise: derive relationships before inventing names

- Use variables only: `N` is a divine designation derived from ordinary noun `R` in era E0; `P` is a shrine-place expression; `F` is a petition-specific title. No actual world, god, or culture is established.
- E0 output: `N + P` must obey the language’s compounding/case rules. Record the ordinary noun independently so later name changes can be compared with ordinary vocabulary.
- E1 operation: apply a stated sound change to both the ordinary vocabulary and name; retain old spelling only if a scribal practice explains it. If `R` is later replaced lexically, the name can become opaque without any exceptional phonology.
- E2 operation: a foreign visitor uses local deity-label `X` for `N + P`. Record `visitor identifies(N + P, X)`; neither remove `N` nor infer that worshippers accept the identification.
- E3 operation: public address substitutes `F`, while a designated officiant retains access to `N` at a specified rite. Keep explicit-name narration and inscription rules separately selectable.
- Visible coupling: propose a name-bearing badge or frontlet, its script, manufacture, wearer rule, and handling restriction. An image of the badge cannot independently prove what anyone pronounced.
- Deliver alternatives: `F` remains a title; `F` becomes a proper name; `X` remains an outsider label; a dual-address cult develops. lore-shokunin decides whether any alternative enters a world bible.

## 11. What a worldbuilder can take — and provenance cautions

- **Take:** a typed name ledger, audience-relative transparency, productive epithet grammar, directed contact equations, medium-specific avoidance, and an era table that also tracks visible name-bearing objects.
- **Do not take:** “old name means old god,” “one name equals one cult,” “same function proves cognacy,” “translation proves merger,” or “all sacred naming rests on one magical belief.” These shortcuts exceed the evidence.
- **Evidence hierarchy is question-dependent:** an inscription can establish local wording but not everyone’s beliefs; a hymn can establish poetic usage but not a statute; a statute can prescribe avoidance without demonstrating compliance.
- **Bias calibration:** Roman accounts privilege outside elite categories; court and temple corpora privilege literate institutions; modern comparative labels can reproduce imperial/colonial flattening. Missing vernacular and non-elite testimony is a limit, not permission to invent it.
- **Verification boundary:** direct passage checks here cover Tacitus’ Latin §43, the cited Mishnah passages, and JPS Exodus 3:14/28:36; the Parker review, SBL record, and Yale catalogue support bibliographic/contextual checks, not a claim to have collated every printed edition or ancient witness.
- **Open debt:** exact Apollo/Athena derivations are not supplied; Ugaritic garment terminology and specific textile identifications remain unverified (declared open debt). Egyptian, South Asian, African, and Indigenous American naming traditions are outside this entry’s evidential coverage, not presumed to follow this sample.
- **Handoff boundary:** offer causal mechanisms, reference comparisons, and explicit alternatives. Which gods exist, which names are effective, which identities are accepted, and which dress rules bind fictional societies remain lore-shokunin’s canon decisions.
