# Era-language staging: one conlang, several historical worlds

- topic: Staging one constructed language across fictional eras through ordered sound changes, stratified registers, diglossic domain shifts, regnal naming, period labels, and independently dated costume/material practices.
- sources: Campbell (2013); Ferguson (1959); Fishman (1967); Agha (2007); Frellesvig (2010); Allen (2013); Vogelsang-Eastwood (1993); Wilkinson (2017 paperback reissue); Breen & Teeuwen (2010, to-verify); Milhaupt (2014, to-verify); Japan, Act on Era Names (1979). Editions, links, verification limits, and source-to-claim assignments appear in §12.

## 1. Scope and the clocks that must not be collapsed

- **Method, not canon:** the numbered stages and invented forms below are disposable demonstrations, not a fictional culture's established history.
- **Speech clock:** innovations diffuse through particular communities; a dynasty's accession does not automatically change its subjects' vowels (Campbell 2013, chs. 2, 7).
- **Institutional clock:** a court, temple, guild, or school can change the authoritative code, title, or permitted reading without changing domestic speech (Ferguson 1959; Agha 2007).
- **Document/material clock:** spelling, copied texts, garment construction, heirlooms, and museum classifications each have their own transmission histories. One object can preserve several dates (Frellesvig 2010, introduction; Vogelsang-Eastwood 1993, ch. 1).
- Keep **event date**, **linguistic stage**, **composition date**, **surviving-copy date**, and **later edition's normalization** in separate fields. An archaic passage copied yesterday is not yesterday's conversation.
- Calibration vocabulary: **attested** = evidence in a named source; **synthesis** = an interpretation joining evidence; **design** = a proposed craft operation; **(to-verify)** = an unresolved source or detail, not permission to assert it.

## 2. Frameworks and their useful limits

| Framework | What it licenses | What it does not license |
|---|---|---|
| Relative chronology; feeding/bleeding (Campbell 2013, ch. 2) | Order changes by whether one creates or removes another's environment | A fixed number of changes per century |
| Regular conditioned sound change (Campbell 2013, chs. 2, 7) | Apply a stated change to eligible forms in its speech community and time window | Random exceptions for prestigious names; denial of diffusion or contact |
| Borrowing versus analogy (Campbell 2013, chs. 3–4) | Give loans entry dates; record paradigm remodelling separately | Passing every present-day word through the entire ancestral history |
| Enregisterment (Agha 2007) | Explain how features become recognizable as priestly, provincial, fashionable, or vulgar | Assuming every social group naturally has one immutable voice |
| Diglossia, original prototype (Ferguson 1959) | Model socially allocated H/L varieties, acquisition routes, and specialized domains | Calling every formal/casual contrast diglossia |
| Extended diglossia (Fishman 1967) | Distinguish societal domain allocation from individual bilingual competence; allow distinct languages | Treating every multilingual encounter as a stable H/L system |
| Written norm versus speech stage (Frellesvig 2010, pp. 1–7) | Preserve an older written grammar while spoken forms continue changing | Inferring old pronunciation from unchanged spelling alone |

- **Calibration:** H (“high”) and L (“low”) are conventional labels for institutional valuations, not measures of intelligence or grammatical completeness. The cycle in §6 is a design menu, not a universal historical law.

## 3. Ordered sound laws: a reproducible miniature

**Design assumptions:** a single local transmission stream; E0 forms have first-syllable stress; vowels `/a e i o u/`; relevant consonants `/p t k m n s r l/`; affricate `/tʃ/` is one segment, not a `/t/` plus `/ʃ/` sequence. No unstated vowel shortening, assimilation, or stress shift applies.

| Order and window | Law | Eligibility/control |
|---|---|---|
| L1, E0 → E1 | `k > tʃ / _ i` | Before `/i/`, stressed or unstressed; not before `/e/` |
| L2, E0 → E1, after L1 | `t > d / V _ V` | Plain `/t/` between vowels; does not target the affricate |
| L3, E1 → E2 | `i > e` in an unstressed syllable | Initial stressed `/i/` remains `/i/` |
| L4, E1 → E2, after L3 | `e > ∅ / _ #`, only if unstressed | Deletes final unstressed `/e/`, not final `/a/` or stressed vowels |

Notation: `V` = vowel; `#` = word boundary; `∅` = loss. Forms in the next table are phonemic strings with initial stress understood; angle brackets elsewhere indicate spelling.

| E0 form; toy gloss | After L1 | After L2 = E1 | After L3 | After L4 = E2 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| `naki`, oath | `natʃi` | `natʃi` | `natʃe` | `natʃ` |
| `nake`, cloak | `nake` | `nake` | `nake` | `nak` |
| `pati`, ruler | `pati` | `padi` | `pade` | `pad` |
| `kima`, spirit | `tʃima` | `tʃima` | `tʃima` | `tʃima` |
| `sata`, seal | `sata` | `sada` | `sada` | `sada` |

- **Feeding:** L3 creates final `/e/` for L4: `padi > pade > pad`. Original final `/e/` also qualifies: `nake > nak`.
- **Bleeding, counterfactual:** on an extra test input `sate`, L2 then L4 yields `sade > sad`; L4 before L2 would yield `sat`, removing the intervocalic environment. These are different histories, not optional spellings.
- **Counterbleeding/opacity:** L1 applies before L3 removes its `/i/` trigger: `naki > natʃi > natʃe`. At E2, `/tʃ/` need not stand before `/i/`; the conditioning belonged to an earlier stage.
- **Order falsification:** putting L3 before L1 would instead give `naki > nake > nak`. A law list that generates both outputs for the same inherited item needs an explicit branch or remodelling event.
- **Loan stratum:** an unrelated word adapted as `naki` after L1–L2 cease, but before L3–L4, yields E2 `nak`, not `natʃ`. Its late entry explains the apparent exception; the loan is invented, not borrowed from a real-world example.
- **Learned doublet:** an E2 antiquarian can reintroduce archival `⟨naki⟩` as `/naki/`, provided that pronunciation is licensed in the learned register. This is a new transmission event, not reversal of L1.
- Run names, divine epithets, garment words, and ordinary vocabulary through the same applicable laws. Preserve a different result only with a dated reason: another dialect, loan, analogical remodelling, taboo substitution, or cultivated reading (Campbell 2013).
- Keep `sound-law`, `analogy`, `semantic-change`, `borrowing`, and `official-renaming` as different event types. Replacing a feared god-name with “the Veiled One” is lexical/taboo change, not a phonological law.

## 4. Stage packets, not “ancient-sounding” filters

Each packet records `stage_id | relative interval | community | phonology | grammar | lexicon strata | written norm | reading convention | sources/status`. A political era can contain several packets, and adjacent eras can share one.

| Optional fictional epoch | Speech packet | Institutional/lexical action | Costume/material cross-check |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: foundation | E0 | Oath formula and ruler-title coined; decide who can record them | Establish construction and fiber supply, not merely garment names |
| B: consolidation | E1 | L1–L2 completed locally; a chancery preserves older spellings | A ceremonial silhouette can persist while its name changes in speech |
| C: expansion or schism | E2 | L3–L4 completed; late loans and rival recitations create strata | Imported trim can acquire a loan-name without replacing the whole outfit |
| D: reform | E2 unless further changes are explicitly supplied | A new standard or dating formula changes authority, not necessarily phonology | A newly prescribed uniform need not be worn immediately in every province |

- These event associations are **design choices**, not claims that foundation, expansion, or bureaucracy causes those particular sound changes.
- For a character moving between eras, choose a **production profile** and a **comprehension profile**. Reading an old charter, understanding a hymn, and participating in ordinary conversation are different abilities.
- Track morphology independently: case loss, new auxiliaries, and honorific replacements need their own histories. Sound erosion may motivate a reanalysis; it does not mechanically dictate which grammar must follow.
- An extinct distinction cannot simply reappear from its merged output without new conditioning, analogy, contact, or another information source. A restoration movement may invent a distinction rather than recover one (Campbell 2013).

## 5. Register stratification inside each era

| Domain/community | Possible retained or innovative features | Acquisition and access | Visible coupling to test |
|---|---|---|---|
| Temple recitation | Older formula/grammar, but potentially contemporary pronunciation | Apprenticeship, memorization, recitation correction | Vestment and formula jointly authorize the rite; neither dates the other by itself |
| Chancery/legal work | Older spelling, fixed title order, current administrative vocabulary | Scribal schooling and exemplars | Seal, office garment, and date formula may expose an impostor |
| Court performance | Selected archaisms or new honorifics; not necessarily an older dialect wholesale | Patronage, etiquette instruction, imitation | A fashionable sleeve-name and a cultivated pronunciation can index the same circle |
| Market/household networks | Locally transmitted speech with its own complete grammar | Daily participation; potentially substantial multilingual competence | Workwear lexicon may retain words excluded from elite inventories |
| Guild, regiment, diaspora | Technical loans, trade synonyms, mixed features | Workplace or network membership | Material technique and specialist vocabulary can travel by different routes |

- A person can command several rows; a courtier need not use chancery language at home. Region, occupation, age, occasion, and audience intersect rather than forming one ladder.
- **Enregisterment evidence:** specify who calls a feature “ancestral,” “affected,” or “uncouth”; give that judgement a medium such as a school correction, comedy, etiquette text, or guild test (Agha 2007).
- **Orthography is not audio:** E2 priests might write `⟨naki⟩` but recite `/natʃ/`; an antiquarian faction might demand `/naki/`. Record the reading convention rather than assuming that sacred spelling freezes mouths.
- Separate a script restriction from a language restriction: a vernacular can be written in a prestigious script, and a learned code can be transliterated into an accessible one.
- Do not make poverty, illiteracy, oral transmission, or an unfashionable accent equivalent to linguistic deficiency. Institutional exclusion is a social fact to model, not a grammatical ranking.

## 6. Diglossia cycles: domain reallocation, not inevitable progress

Ferguson (1959) supplies a relatively stable functional H/L prototype; Fishman (1967) extends the societal analysis beyond closely related varieties. Neither establishes a universal cycle from temple language to national vernacular.

| Optional transition | Mechanism that must be specified | Observable linguistic result | Myth/era/costume coupling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Differentiation | A learned text/code acquires restricted institutional domains | Everyday and authorized usages diverge in function | Ritual specialists claim custody of founding words and associated regalia |
| Reproduction | Training, copying, recruitment, and funding reproduce that division | Written H can remain conservative while L changes | New rulers inherit the same liturgical code and vestment vocabulary |
| Domain breach | Translators, patrons, litigants, or performers demand L in an H domain | Vernacular contracts, sermons, or scholarship gain standing | A rite can change language while retaining its garment and sacred emblem |
| Reallocation | A court, church, association, or state changes recognized usage | Former L may become a codified H; old H may survive in narrower domains | New era rhetoric claims renewal; old clothing is retained, rejected, or reinterpreted |
| Layered aftermath | Competing institutions keep different settlements | Several standards, a continuum, stable multilingualism, or language shift | Rival calendars and uniforms need not align with rival native languages |

- No printing press, conquest, or school law guarantees a transition: name the teachers, audiences, incentives, exclusions, and regional reach that make the change effective.
- **Distinguish outcomes:** replacing H in administration is not the same as losing its liturgy; vernacularization is not necessarily mother-tongue shift; a foreign H need not be an ancestor of local L.
- **Scene test:** can a litigant speak the household variety yet require a notary for the written petition? Can a trained speaker quote H without adopting it as an everyday identity? If not, the model may be conflating domains and persons.

## 7. Regnal names and period vocabulary: type every label

| Label type | Naming/temporal operation | Required disambiguation |
|---|---|---|
| Birth/personal name | Identifies a person before or outside office | Address form, public reference, and archival identity can differ |
| Regnal/throne name | A ruler's accession-associated name in systems that use one | Not universal; distinguish it from a birth name and an honorific title |
| Titulary/royal epithet | A sequence of office and legitimacy claims | Component titles are not necessarily additional personal names |
| Era name | Names an officially recognized count or interval | Not necessarily a monarch's name; change-trigger and issuer are separate fields |
| Regnal year | Counts years relative to a ruler's reign | Specify accession-year treatment, calendar boundary, co-rule, and rival claimant |
| Posthumous name/title | Assigned or conventionalized after death | Do not put it in a living contemporary's mouth without an explicit retrospective frame |
| Dynasty/house designation | Groups a succession or ruling lineage | Establish whether contemporary, retrospective, hostile, or editorial |
| Period label | Groups events by a historian's chosen criterion | Linguistic, artistic, political, and archaeological periods need not coincide |
| Restoration/golden-age label | Evaluates the past and claims continuity | Attribute the evaluation to a speaker or institution, not the neutral timeline |
| Costume/style period | Classifies a silhouette, construction, or fashion cluster | A later museum label is not automatically the wearer's garment term |

- **Name record:** `referent_id | label_id | label_type | issuer/user | valid interval | composition/copy date | spoken form | spelling | later normalization | status/source`.
- Apply sound laws to an **inherited spoken label**; separately track an unchanged written form, learned rereading, translation, exonym, or official replacement. One referent can accumulate all of these without becoming several people.
- **Era-name formation recipe, design:** specify the authority, permitted morphology, semantic/allusive pool, taboo/reuse rules, pronunciation, written form, and announcement medium. Auspicious wording is a political claim, not a prediction that the era will prosper.
- **Date formula recipe:** define whether the count has a year zero, whether succession resets immediately or at a later boundary, which calendar supplies month/day, and how rival reigns are cited. Do not import these answers from a familiar real calendar by accident.
- A new count does not automatically invalidate coins, contracts, or old calendars. Write separate rules for continued validity, overstriking, conversion, and retrospective dating.
- Keep an **emic label** beside an **editorial label**: “year N under title T” and “Later Court Phase” can identify the same date while making different claims. A later editor must not silently replace the primary wording.

## 8. Synchronizing language, myth, symbol, and clothing without determinism

| Shared event, proposed | Language mechanism | Myth/symbol mechanism | Material control |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cult reform | New epithet, euphemism, translation, or authorized recitation | Existing deity/emblem is reinterpreted, not necessarily replaced | Identify whether vestment changes are prescribed, manufactured, or actually worn |
| New court | Prestige koine, title borrowing, or register revaluation | Accession performance claims inherited or novel legitimacy | Date insignia, cloth supply, and distribution separately |
| Garment adoption | Loan, calque, semantic extension, or reuse of an old word | Foreignness may be praised, stigmatized, or later forgotten | Show the imported object/technique rather than inferring it from the word alone |
| Antiquarian revival | Learned doublet, spelling pronunciation, invented archaism | “Return to origins” is an actor's interpretation | Revival clothing can use new fibers, cuts, or fasteners under an old name |

- A garment word may survive after its referent changes; a new object may inherit an existing word; a religious metaphor may outlive both. Record **form history**, **sense history**, and **object history** separately.
- For a disputed relic, compare the inscription's language, the fabric's construction, the current ritual reading, and any later repair. Agreement is useful; disagreement may indicate reuse, archaism, forgery, or a wrong attribution—not automatically one preferred explanation.
- Never infer a pantheon, moral worldview, gender order, or era from a phoneme inventory. A coupling requires a transmission mechanism and evidence, not resemblance alone.

## 9. Bounded comparison: Egyptian language and pharaonic institutions

| Required system | Reference anchor and staging consequence |
|---|---|
| Language family + writing | Egyptian is an Afroasiatic branch. Separate Old/Middle/Late Egyptian stages from hieroglyphic and hieratic writing; Demotic names both a script and a language stage. Coptic uses a Greek-derived alphabet with additional Egyptian-derived signs and supplies crucial later vowel evidence (Allen 2013). |
| Mythic/pantheon core | Solar divinities and the Osiris–Isis–Horus complex interact with local cults and kingship; do not flatten different places and centuries into one unchanging divine family chart (Wilkinson 2017). |
| Symbolic system | Divine forms, royal insignia, and named royal identities participate in legitimacy; a sign's use and interpretation require context, not a universal symbol dictionary (Wilkinson 2017). |
| Era model | Regnal/dynastic organization and modern Egyptological period labels answer different questions. Old/Middle/New Kingdom labels must not be substituted for a speaker's own dating expression (Wilkinson 2017, chronology; exact documentary formulas (to-verify)). |
| Costume/material signature | Surviving wrapped and sewn garments, including linen examples, offer evidence independent of idealized depictions. Compare construction, use-wear, and find context; do not treat a royal relief as a population wardrobe census (Vogelsang-Eastwood 1993, chs. 1, 4, 7–8). |
| Naming practices | Separate a royal birth/personal name, throne name, and elements of titulary rather than treating every royal designation as an era name; reign-specific sequences and exact formulas require specialist checking (to-verify). |

- **Era-staging lesson:** Allen's long diachrony allows distinct speech-stage and written-tradition tracks; do not map each dynasty to an invented wholesale language replacement. Degrees of literary conservatism need text-specific evidence.
- **What a worldbuilder can take:** parallel institutional transmission streams; several labels for one ruler; old ceremonial forms alongside changed daily usage; garments as an independent check on textual periodization.
- **Provenance cautions:** continuous phonetic/chronicle evidence is uneven, especially early; much survives through elite or funerary contexts. Colonial-era collection histories and thin excavation records can weaken object provenance; Vogelsang-Eastwood explicitly warns about scant textile reporting.
- **Thin coverage here:** this entry does not adjudicate specialist Egyptian vocalism reconstructions. Conventional Egyptological reading vowels are not recovered ancient audio; exact early vocalizations and detailed royal titulary remain (to-verify), not license for invented certainty.

## 10. Bounded comparison: Japanese language, courts, and era names

| Required system | Reference anchor and staging consequence |
|---|---|
| Language family + writing | Japanese belongs to Japonic; Chinese-derived writing does not make it Sinitic. Distinguish written Chinese, Japanese renderings of it, and Japanese written phonographically/logographically; script affiliation is not language descent (Frellesvig 2010, ch. 1). |
| Mythic/pantheon core | Court ancestry narratives involving Amaterasu coexist historically with changing kami–Buddhist relations; projecting modern Shinto unchanged into every period is unsafe (Breen & Teeuwen 2010; passage-level check (to-verify)). |
| Symbolic system | Court texts, ritual objects, and clothing can jointly signify authority, but specific motif/rank/occasion equations require dated evidence; no “timeless Japanese meaning” is assumed here (Breen & Teeuwen 2010; Milhaupt 2014; detail checks (to-verify)). |
| Era model | The current Act on Era Names (1979), main provisions (1)–(2), assigns era names to Cabinet Order and limits alteration to imperial succession. This is verified current law, not a rule for all earlier centuries. |
| Costume/material signature | Treat kosode/kimono histories and the modern framing of “traditional dress” as changes requiring dates, not one national outfit outside time; exact local adoption and terminological dates are (to-verify) against Milhaupt (2014) and garment evidence. |
| Naming practices | Keep personal identifiers, office/address forms, retrospective imperial labels, and nengō/gengō era names in distinct fields. The statute establishes an era-name mechanism, not a universal personal-naming system; century-specific name inventories are (to-verify). |

- **Era-staging lesson, verified excerpt:** Frellesvig (2010, pp. 1–5) distinguishes Old, Early Middle, Late Middle, and Modern Japanese; a relatively fixed Classical written norm persisted while spoken language changed. Historical spellings and editors' modern proper-name forms are not direct phonetic transcriptions of every era.
- **What a worldbuilder can take:** separate written prestige from spoken stage; allow old grammatical norms to survive political transitions; distinguish the legal era-name change from a ruler's other names and from costume change.
- **Provenance cautions:** court records and literary corpora underrepresent many speakers; ancient ancestry narratives are not neutral contemporary chronicles. Frellesvig explicitly limits prehistory and dialect coverage; Christian-period sources add valuable but purpose-shaped evidence. This comparison does not cover Ryukyuan or Ainu traditions and must not stand in for them.

## 11. Application and handoff checklist

1. Choose a speech community and ancestral inventory; write relative chronology before adding absolute dates. Leave unattested intervals explicit rather than filling them with invented historical claims.
2. Supply positive and negative environment controls for each law; derive a core vocabulary, ruler-label, divine epithet, and garment term. Label each item inherited, borrowed, remodelled, or newly coined.
3. Build a stage packet and register/domain grid for each scene horizon; give writing and recitation independent conventions. State who learns which variety, where, and for what purpose.
4. Select, reject, or branch the §6 transitions. Document the institution that sustains or reallocates each domain; do not insert an automatic “civilization ladder.”
5. Build the §7 name/date ledger. Preserve repeated titles, rival counts, posthumous labels, and later editorial normalizations as separate records with shared referent IDs.
6. Cross-date words, symbols, garments, and repairs. Require an explicit mechanism for every asserted synchronization; otherwise retain independent intervals and uncertainty.
7. Stage an inscription reading, an accession announcement, and a garment-name misunderstanding. Test whether participants' production, comprehension, status, and material cues actually support the scene.
8. Hand lore-shokunin **proposals**: law list with windows, derivation table, stage/register grid, label/date ledger, material cross-checks, sources, alternatives, and unresolved `(to-verify)` items. No option becomes world-bible canon through this entry.
