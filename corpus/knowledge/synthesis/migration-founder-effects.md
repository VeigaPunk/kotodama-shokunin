## 1. Scope and calibration

- This is a reference and design procedure, not a fictional migration history, demographic simulation, or canon entry.
- **Founder principle:** an initial population can disproportionately shape the linguistic feature pool encountered by later arrivals; settlement sequence, contact ecology, and transmission matter (Mufwene 1996).
- A linguistic founder population need not coincide with the earliest land claim, a ruling dynasty, a genetic ancestry cluster, or everyone later called an ethnic group.
- Separate settlers, receiving populations, displaced people, enslaved people, and later arrivals. Their access to households, schools, worship, and institutions is unequal.
- **Diaspora** and **colony** are not synonyms: dispersal need not confer territorial sovereignty; settler institutions can dispossess existing populations.
- Here, **drift** means a change in the distribution of linguistic variants, not an inherited destiny toward a particular grammar. Do not transfer population-genetic equations to speakers without an independently justified model.
- **Calibration:** founder effects and selective conservatism are usable explanatory hypotheses; a universal rule that small communities change faster is unsupported.
- Tables of fictional consequences below are designer heuristics. Historical comparisons are bounded regional/period cases, not interchangeable civilization packages.

## 2. Mechanisms: distinguish the process before designing its traces

| Framework/process | Mechanism to specify | Observable or fictional trace | Calibration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Founder principle — Mufwene (1996) | Early networks weight which variants later learners encounter | A founding minority's variant becomes ordinary local speech | Conditional; founding date alone cannot predict dominance |
| Dialect mixing and leveling — Trudgill (2004) | Contact between mutually intelligible varieties reduces some competing variants | A new regional norm differs from every input dialect | Koineization is not simply borrowing words |
| Reallocation — Trudgill (2004) | Surviving input variants acquire new social or stylistic distributions | An old regional pronunciation becomes a ceremonial or occupational marker | Requires evidence of distribution, not just two surviving forms |
| Heritage-language development — Polinsky (2018) | Early exposure to a minority language interacts with later dominance and changing input | Uneven proficiency across discourse, vocabulary, literacy, and grammar | A heritage speaker is not necessarily a late classroom learner |
| Enregisterment — Agha (2003) | Features become recognized as signs of a social persona or activity | Speech advertised as ancestral, courtly, rural, or authentically diasporic | Recognition is a social history, not a property of sounds alone |
| Critical toponymy — Rose-Redwood et al. (2010) | Institutions authorize, circulate, contest, or erase place-names | Maps, deeds, signs, and oral routes use conflicting names | A gazetteer is evidence of power as well as vocabulary |

- Trudgill's subtitle, *The Inevitability of Colonial Englishes*, states a strong theoretical position; do not promote it into a universal law of migration.
- The relative explanatory weight of demographics, accommodation, prestige, and identity is contested. Retain competing accounts when the historical record cannot separate them.
- Mufwene's population analogy is an explanatory model developed in creole studies, not a license to identify languages with races or to treat coercion as neutral sampling.
- Contact does not automatically produce a pidgin or creole. Dialect mixture, multilingual repertoires, language shift, and stable bilingualism require different histories.

## 3. Drift acceleration: what can actually accelerate?

| Proposed cause | Plausible effect | Missing condition to check | Invalid shortcut |
|---|---|---|---|
| Narrow founding feature pool | A previously uncommon input variant starts locally common | Which source communities supplied actual everyday models? | Small population means fast sound change |
| Dense interaction among newcomers | Rapid accommodation or leveling in some features | Shared work, households, peer groups, and mutual intelligibility | Isolation alone causes simplification |
| A locally raised peer cohort | Local norms consolidate across families | Who plays, learns, and works together? | Every change is completed in one generation |
| Sustained new immigration | Founder weighting may weaken, persist, or be redistributed | Relative timing, settlement clustering, and access to institutions | First arrivals always win |
| Schooling or administrative standardization | Selected written or prestigious forms spread | Attendance, enforcement, literacy, and private-domain use | Official spelling proves uniform speech |
| Contact with another language | Borrowing, transfer, or shift becomes possible | Bilingual speakers and specific interaction domains | All foreign-looking forms share one substrate |

- **Working distinction:** faster diffusion or fixation of a variant is not the same as faster creation of new variants, faster grammatical change, or faster vocabulary replacement.
- Contrast founder **selection from existing variation** with a **post-settlement innovation**. A homeland form absent from today's standard may still have been widespread at departure.
- Compare like with like: a colony's present vernacular against its documented source dialects at departure, not only against a later metropolitan standard.
- Track phonology, morphology, lexicon, orthography, and ritual performance independently. A conservative spelling system can accompany innovative pronunciation.
- No generation count, population threshold, or percentage here is an empirical universal; quantitative predictions require a specified dataset and model.

## 4. Build a migration ecology, not a purity pedigree

Record these fields before assigning sound changes or an ancestral costume:

| Field | Questions | Coupled consequence to test |
|---|---|---|
| Departure sample | Which regions, classes, occupations, ages, and languages are represented? | Input variants; craft vocabulary; available storytellers and textile skills |
| Mode of movement | Voluntary settlement, trade, refuge, expulsion, enslavement, military relocation? | Ownership, legal status, interrupted kinship, and memory politics differ |
| Receiving society | Who already names, farms, worships, and governs here? | Local toponyms, ecological loans, intermarriage, resistance, unequal bilingualism |
| Transmission network | Which adults and peers provide daily speech? | Childhood acquisition may outweigh the prestige of an absentee founder |
| Reconnection | How often do people, letters, books, goods, and teachers travel? | Imported reforms, fashions, spellings, and competing notions of authenticity |
| Institutional carriers | Household, sanctuary, guild, school, regiment, printer, broadcaster? | Different registers can survive through different institutions |
| Material bottleneck | Which fibers, dyes, tools, books, and skilled makers are missing? | Garment substitution may precede a change in garment names or ritual meaning |
| Documentary visibility | Whose speech, clothes, and names are recorded, and by whom? | Elite chronicles may conceal the people doing most everyday transmission |

- A ship list is not a complete speech network; a genealogy is not a neutral census. Record absences and the purpose of each document.
- Do not infer language from ancestry alone, myth from a surname alone, or ethnicity from a textile style alone.
- For a forced migration, identify destroyed or rebuilt institutions rather than describing survivors as a conveniently isolated experimental population.

## 5. Diaspora conservatism is a vector, not a whole-culture state

| Dimension | What can be retained | What can change simultaneously | Evidence needed |
|---|---|---|---|
| Lexicon | Departure-era words or senses | Loans for housing, trade, food, and administration | Dated meanings and source-dialect comparison |
| Grammar/phonology | Particular contrasts or constructions | New distributions, mergers, leveling, or contact effects | Comparable speech samples, not spellings alone |
| Sacred performance | A recitation formula, divine name, or narrative sequence | Pronunciation, explanation, musical setting, participant competence | Performance context and transmission history |
| Writing | An inherited alphabet and conventional spelling | New typography, scripts, readership, and language mixing | Manuscripts/print by date, genre, and audience |
| Costume | An inherited garment category, fastening, or ritual role | Local fabric, dye, cut, manufacture, and wearing occasion | Dated objects, images, production evidence, and terminology |
| Collective memory | A remembered homeland or expulsion narrative | Emphasis, protagonists, political uses, and claims of return | Different versions, including dissenting and non-elite accounts |

- **Conservative relative to what?** Specify feature, comparison population, period, and genre before using the adjective.
- A diaspora can preserve one older feature while innovating elsewhere; the homeland can itself adopt new standards. Neither side is an unchanging original.
- Make conservatism costly and institutional: practice time, teachers, books, marriage networks, ritual schedules, and access to materials need carriers.
- Do not equate conservation with greater authenticity or heritage-language difference with deficient intelligence or loyalty (Polinsky 2018).
- Heritage development can involve divergent acquisition, later attrition, and contact; these are not interchangeable diagnoses. A single modern recording cannot establish which process occurred.

## 6. Heritage registers: separate competence, repertoire, and belonging

- A **heritage language** concerns acquisition and social setting; a **heritage register** here is a working design label for socially recognized ancestral styles. They are not equivalent categories (Polinsky 2018; Agha 2003).
- A proficient bilingual may switch registers freely; someone with limited productive speech may nevertheless know ritual formulas or recognize family names. Neither profile represents every diaspora.

| Register slot | Design its carrier | Linguistic distinction | Myth/symbol/costume coupling |
|---|---|---|---|
| Household | Caregivers, siblings, visiting relatives | Productive conversation, kin terms, endearments | Domestic stories and ordinary repair/work clothing |
| Ritual | Officiants, choirs, memorization groups | Fixed formulas versus ability to improvise or explain | Sacred narratives, restricted names, ritual garments |
| Trade/civic | Market partners, clerks, schools | Contact vocabulary and institutional conventions | Weights, seals, uniforms, imported fabric names |
| Commemorative | Associations, festivals, public performances | Selected archaisms or stylized accent | Homeland symbols and reconstructed ceremonial outfits |
| Revival/teaching | Teachers, dictionaries, recordings, online groups | Standardization may select among surviving variants | New badges, learning materials, newly authorized traditions |

- Give each slot an audience, learning route, permitted innovations, literacy level, and correction authority; do not make ethnicity itself an acquisition mechanism.
- Separate **knows the formula**, **understands it**, **can extend it**, **can read it**, and **may publicly perform it**. These permissions and abilities need not coincide.
- Enregisterment test: who calls a feature ancestral, who recognizes it, who mocks it, and who can profit from performing it?
- A garment may retain its ritual name after its material changes; a new garment can inherit an old emblem. These are design possibilities, not automatic consequences of migration.

## 7. Colony naming: motivation, linguistic form, and authority are separate

Use Tent & Blair's (2011) motivation-based approach as a prompt, not as a universal indigenous naming theory. The practical rows below are adaptations, not a reproduction of their complete taxonomy.

| Naming operation | Fictional construction pattern | Coupling question | Provenance trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Transfer | [home place], or New [home place] | Which homeland is being remembered, and by which cohort? | A transferred name does not demonstrate biological descent |
| Commemoration | [patron], [founder], [event] | Which authority becomes legitimate through repetition? | A dedication is not proof the honoree visited |
| Religious dedication | [saint], [sanctuary], [sacred event] | Mission, protection, calendrical date, or later reinterpretation? | Do not treat every sacred name as a pre-settlement survival |
| Description | [landform], [resource], [visible quality] | Whose ecological vocabulary describes the place? | A plausible modern meaning is not a demonstrated etymology |
| Indigenous-name adoption | Local name retained or phonologically adapted | What language, speaker, setting, and permission transmitted it? | A colonial recorder may missegment or mistranslate it |
| Translation/calquing | Existing name translated partly or wholly | Which semantic structure survives under a new language? | Similar meanings do not establish an actual translation chain |
| Administrative replacement | Official standard, number, or renamed district | Which signs, maps, schools, and deeds enforce the change? | Official replacement does not erase oral usage |

- For each name store: feature ID; earliest attestation; source language; recorded form; proposed analysis; naming actor; official status; competing forms; dated changes; confidence.
- Keep **motive**, **word formation**, and **institutional authority** in separate fields. An adopted name can be commemorative, translated, and contested at different moments.
- Preserve endonym/exonym and official/vernacular distinctions. A settlement can have several legitimate contextual names without requiring one canonical etymology.
- Personal names need their own track: given names, patronymics, surnames, ritual names, nicknames, and bureaucratic spellings do not obey one shared rule.
- Mythic etymology can be an in-world explanation without being the historical derivation; label these as separate candidate claims for lore-shokunin.

## 8. Era staging: use institutional events, not a fixed diaspora clock

| Stage label — design scaffold only | Language and naming work | Myth/symbol and material work |
|---|---|---|
| Departure/rupture | Document the source repertoire and names actually available then | Distinguish remembered institutions from portable objects and lost skills |
| Initial settlement | Specify contact domains, receiving-population names, and rival input forms | Identify new materials, sacred sites, labor arrangements, and contested claims |
| Local transmission | Decide which networks pass variants to locally raised speakers | Track who teaches origin narratives, dress-making, and ceremonial competence |
| Institutional consolidation | Schools, worship, print, or administration privilege selected forms | Some stories and garments become public emblems; other practices remain ordinary |
| Reconnection/reform | New immigration, literacy reforms, travel, or media alter the repertoire | Imported homeland fashions can conflict with diasporic claims to authenticity |
| Memorialization/revival | Distinguish documentation, teaching standards, and everyday productive use | Festival costume and curated symbols may be recent selections, not untouched survivals |

- Stages can overlap, repeat, or never occur. Date them by events and evidence, not a universal first/second/third-generation timetable.
- Give departure, manuscript copying, object manufacture, museum collection, and modern revival different dates; they are different evidential anchors.

## 9. Historical comparison A — Norse-settled Iceland, not a frozen Viking refuge

- **Bounds and language:** settlement conventionally placed in the late ninth–tenth centuries; Old Norse–Icelandic belongs to North Germanic. The wider diaspora included multiple origins and unequal statuses, not a pure national founding sample (Byock 2001; Jesch 2015).
- **Writing:** medieval Icelandic book culture used the Latin alphabet after Christianization; runes belong to the wider Norse writing repertoire, not proof of universal founding-era Icelandic literacy (Jesch 2015). Early local runic evidence is sparse and mostly late-dated: the oldest surviving Icelandic inscription (the Viðey stick) is usually placed in the tenth–eleventh century, and regular runic use on the island is generally dated to around 1200 (Raschellà 2016, §1.2).
- **Mythic/religious core:** Norse religious inheritance followed by Christian institutions; much surviving mythic narrative is mediated by later Christian manuscript culture. Do not make a later written account a transcript of a settler's beliefs.
- **Symbolic system:** investigate genealogy, legal speech, religious signs, and object contexts separately; neither a ship motif nor a supposed pagan emblem is a universal ethnic identifier. Site-specific cross/hammer readings stay deliberately ambiguous: a tenth-century soapstone mould from Trendgården, Jutland, cast both Thor's-hammer amulets and Christian crosses (National Museum of Denmark, cat. C24451), and the Foss, Iceland, silver *vargkors* pendant (tenth–eleventh century) can be read as either (Viking Archaeology).
- **Era model:** distinguish settlement, the medieval commonwealth, Christianization, and later manuscript production; linguistic conservation in some structures does not collapse these into one period (Byock 2001; Jesch 2015).
- **Costume/material signature:** North Atlantic wool textile production is a stronger anchor than a generalized warrior outfit. Cloth manufacture, value, and gendered labor require attention; precise founding-era cuts and dyes need dated local evidence because clothing survives mainly as fragments and even recent reconstructions have been revised — treat any founding-era outfit as an inferential model, not a portrait (Hayeur Smith 2020; Lester-Makin & Owen-Crocker, eds., 2024).
- **Naming practices:** genealogical naming and names attached to farms/land claims are useful comparison domains (Byock 2001; Jesch 2015). A later settlement genealogy does not independently prove every named founder or etymology.
- **What a worldbuilder can take:** couple selective linguistic retention with connected households, legal institutions, textile labor, and retrospective settlement memory; separate everyday speech from later literary conservatism.
- **Provenance cautions:** settlement narratives are retrospective and interested in genealogy/property; their coverage of enslaved people and non-elite speech is thin — scholars have proposed that many of the migrants were enslaved people, predominantly from Scotland and Ireland (Plomp et al. 2021). Cohort origins are debated and method-dependent — patrilineal genetic estimates run about 75% Scandinavian while matrilineal estimates run about 60% British-Isles, and one skeletal study suggests roughly equal numbers (Plomp et al. 2021) — so dialect-influence weights cannot be read off heroic surnames or any single study.

## 10. Historical comparison B — eastern Sephardic communities in Ottoman settings

- **Bounds and language:** focus on Judeo-Spanish-speaking communities formed/reconfigured after Iberian expulsions, not all Sephardim or all Ottoman Jews. Judeo-Spanish is Romance/Ibero-Romance; later contact includes Turkish, Greek, and other languages (Harris 1994; University of Michigan Library 2023).
- **Writing and register:** Hebrew-script traditions and later Latin-script publishing must be dated by community and genre. Hebrew/Aramaic religious repertoires are not simply earlier stages of Romance. The script shift was gradual: Hebrew letters (Rashi script; cursive *solitreo*) remained dominant into the early twentieth century, with Latin script rising from around 1900 and particularly after World War I (Schwarzwald, Jewish Languages; Hassán 1995). The term *Ladino* is also narrower among some scholars (literal calque translations of Hebrew/Aramaic) than in popular use, so keep nomenclature a per-community, per-genre field rather than forcing one label across scholarship and speakers.
- **Mythic/religious core:** Jewish monotheism and scriptural narratives, including Exodus, coexist with historically specific memories of expulsion and place. Do not invent a Sephardic pantheon or flatten sacred narrative, family recollection, and folktale into one genre.
- **Symbolic system:** inscribed ritual objects, synagogue textiles, and marriage-document decoration provide contextual evidence. A motif's local meaning needs its object, inscription, date, and use; no generic symbol dictionary suffices (Juhasz, ed., 1990).
- **Era model:** distinguish post-expulsion settlement, later Ottoman community life, nineteenth-/early-twentieth-century change, and subsequent dispersal/revival. These are not one timeless medieval enclave.
- **Costume/material signature:** embroidery, garments, and furnishings combine Iberian inheritance, Ottoman settings, and modern European influence. The Juhasz collection synthesis emphasizes late nineteenth-/early twentieth-century material; it cannot furnish a complete 1492 wardrobe (Juhasz, ed., 1990; Centro Primo Levi, n.d.).
- **Naming practices:** keep ritual/vernacular name forms, surnames, and official transcriptions as separate research fields. Sephardic surnames are layered — Iberian names, names adopted by converso families, later local creations — and records are patchy, so a Spanish-looking surname or congregational label needs local, dated attestation before it can carry a migration itinerary (Beider, n.d.).
- **What a worldbuilder can take:** model retained language features beside contact innovations, changing scripts, locally made ritual textiles, and multiple authorities over communal memory; continuity does not require cultural stasis.
- **Provenance cautions:** Harris's *Death of a Language* is not a present-tense verdict of extinction. Documented publishing and revitalization complicate that framing (University of Michigan Library 2023); neither book title nor a revival event alone measures everyday intergenerational transmission.
- **Thin areas here:** city-specific naming histories, women's vernacular repertoires in earlier periods, exact ritual formula distributions, and departure-era clothing inventories are not established in this entry and need primary/community scholarship; part of the difficulty is structural — Sephardic communities often had neither civil records nor censuses for long periods, so family chronologies are hard to document (Beider, n.d.).

## 11. How to apply to a fictional culture and hand off without making canon

1. Draft a cohort/contact table from §4; include receiving populations and unequal legal statuses, not only celebrated founders.
2. Select candidate outcomes separately: one input-variant retention, one plausible post-settlement innovation, and one contact effect; specify a transmission route for each. This is a design exercise, not a required historical ratio.
3. Make a register matrix from §6; state who speaks, understands, reads, and may perform each style. Include internal variation and disagreement.
4. Build a name ledger from §7 with competing forms and confidence; distinguish a founder legend from the proposed historical derivation.
5. For each proposed heritage garment, specify maker, material supply, technique, occasion, vocabulary, and oldest in-world evidence. Do not derive a language family from the silhouette.
6. Stage the model through §8; mark where a ritual, label, or emblem was selected or reinvented rather than merely retained.
7. Test alternatives: could the apparent archaism be a later borrowing, a source-dialect feature, a spelling convention, or a modern performance? Could the artifact be trade rather than migration?
8. Hand lore-shokunin a **candidate-method packet**: bounded period/region; cohort model; feature/register matrix; naming ledger; material constraint chain; competing explanations; reference pointers; unresolved `(to-verify)` items. Lore chooses canon and records any accepted world facts.

- **Final audit:** no universal drift clock; no ancestry-to-language equation; no empty-land premise; no uniform heritage competence; no frozen ancestral wardrobe; no modern gazetteer masquerading as the complete naming history.
- **What a worldbuilder can take:** migration produces asynchronous continuities — speech, sacred language, names, stories, symbols, and clothing can preserve different pasts through different carriers. Design those carriers and their conflicts, not a single ancestral essence.
- **Provenance cautions:** colonial gazetteers, elite chronicles, retrospective genealogies, and museum displays select what survives. This entry has thin coverage of specific African, Indigenous American, Oceanian, and South Asian migration histories; apply the questions, not the two case histories as universal templates.
