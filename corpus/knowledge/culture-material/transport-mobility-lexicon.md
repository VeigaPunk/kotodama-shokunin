# Transport and Mobility Lexicon — Roads, Vehicles, Draft, Watercraft, and the Words That Travel With Them

- gap: transport/mobility register absent from culture-material (partition covers textiles/cuisine/architecture/trade but no vehicles, roads, or movement vocabulary) — this entry fills it
- topic: mobility as a four-layer material system for fictional cultures — ROUTE (the road and its maintenance institution), VEHICLE (sledge, wheeled cart, litter, watercraft), DRAFT (human porterage, ox, horse, camelid, dog, wind), and WORD (the vehicle vocabulary that travels with the vehicle and outlives it). The master claim: a transport system is three quantities wearing a culture's clothes — payload per unit, terrain law, and clock (how fast a message, an army, or a bride moves) — and the lexicon is evidence of which quantity the culture chose to optimize. Load-bearing cases: the Persian Royal Road (2,699 km Susa–Sardis; courier relay nine days, ninety days on foot; 111 posting stations — [Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road)); the Roman *cursus publicus* (Augustus's state courier service, Suetonius *Divus Augustus* 49; *mutationes*/mansiones, *diploma* permits, late split into *cursus velox* and *cursus clabularis*; ~50 km/day typical overland — [Cursus publicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cursus_publicus)); the Inca Qhapaq Ñan (~40,000 km, UNESCO World Heritage 2014; chasqui relay runners, tambo lodges, qullqa warehouses, mita corvée maintenance; llama load ~30 kg at 20 km/day, up to 45 kg short trips — [Inca road system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system)); the Maya sacbeob ("white road"; Coba–Yaxuna ~100 km; *beh* also means "the road of life" and the Milky Way is *sacbe* — [Sacbe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacbe)); the Q'eswachaka grass-rope bridge (28 m, renewed annually, UNESCO ICH 2013 — [Queshuachaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queshuachaca)). Secondary claims: wheel history is not a progress ladder (solid disc wheels by the Copper Age; spoked wheels Sintashta c. 2000 BCE; Mesoamerican wheeled toys ~1500 BCE with no practical wheel; West African wheeled vehicles largely ceremonial — [Wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel)); the harness sequence (ox yoke → breast collar → rigid padded horse collar, with the "horse-collar revolution" as a contested efficiency thesis — [Horse collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_collar)); the stirrup's late arrival and its "Great Controversy" (White's feudalism thesis vs Bullough/Bachrach — [Stirrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirrup)); and the word-travel physics by which vehicle loans outlive vehicles (*coach* < Hungarian *kocsi* "cart of Kocs"; *canoe* < Arawakan *canaoua* via Spanish; *rickshaw* < Japanese *jinrikisha*, English 1879; *junk* < Javanese *djong* via Portuguese *junco*).

## 1. The frame: four layers, three quantities

- **Four layers** — a mobility system is ROUTE (a prepared surface plus a maintenance institution), VEHICLE (the thing that carries), DRAFT (the power source: human, animal, wind, water, gravity), and WORD (the vocabulary, which is the only layer that survives the vehicle). Design them together or the culture will have horse vocabulary and no horses.
- **Three quantities** — every transport choice answers: **payload per unit** (what moves in one load), **terrain law** (what the ground permits: wheel, hoof, sledge, keel), and **clock** (how long a message, an army, a tax shipment, or a bride takes). The Persian Royal Road's two clocks — nine days by relay, ninety days on foot — are the whole system in one number pair ([Royal Road](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Road)).
- **Craft rule**: a route is only real if someone maintains it. The Inca case makes this explicit — mita corvée labor, a manager of royal roads and a manager of bridges among the named officials, rope bridges on a two-year replacement cycle and log bridges on eight ([Inca road system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system)). If your fictional road has no maintenance clause, it is a description, not an institution.
- **Second craft rule**: mobility is a *capacity*, not a virtue. Where a society has no draft animal of the right build, the wheel is a toy — the Mesoamerican case — and where the terrain or the labor economics favor heads and backs, porterage persists ([Wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel)). Build the capacity chain first; let the vehicle follow.

## 2. The route layer — road as imperial grammar

- **Royal Road (Achaemenid)**: Susa–Sardis, 1,677 miles / 2,699 km, reorganized in the 5th century BCE, probably perfected under Darius I; 111 posting stations with fresh horses; the *angarium* relay and the *pirradazis* couriers; Herodotus 5.52–54 and 8.98. The famous "neither snow nor rain nor heat nor gloom of night" sentence is Herodotus on these couriers, inscribed on New York's Farley Post Office; the USPS has no official motto — a reminder that an ancient route can be quoted for two thousand years after its stations are gone.
- **Roman *cursus publicus***: Augustus's state service (Suetonius *Divus Augustus* 49), built on Persian precedent; the relay-vs-single-courier choice is a design decision with an intelligence rationale — one courier who travels the whole way can be interrogated on arrival. Access was by *diploma*, a permit naming holder, route, means and lodgings; the article records forgery and family abuse of permits, and Pliny–Trajan correspondence on expired licences. Late Antiquity split it into *cursus velox* (horses, mules) and *cursus clabularis* (oxen, heavy freight) — the fast lane and the freight lane, a distinction any worldbuilder should copy. Speeds: 38–62 miles/day typical, ~50 km/day on the Ramsey/Eliot estimates, with Rome–Alexandria news taking about 63 days in 193 CE.
- **Inca Qhapaq Ñan**: ~40,000 km, UNESCO World Heritage 2014; two north–south spines plus transversal routes; relay *chasquis* with quipu messages; *tambos* at day intervals; *qullqas* for storage; the road starts at Cusco and radiates to the four *suyus*, so the network is a cosmogram with a logistics function. Use was reserved to authorities, with an undetermined amount of private traffic — the road as a privilege, not a public utility.
- **Maya sacbeob**: raised, plastered white roads; Coba–Yaxuna ~100 km with a five-ton surface roller left in place; *beh* extends past "road" to "the road of life", and the Milky Way is *sacbe* — the strongest in-corpus case for coupling a route lexicon to cosmology.
- **The proverb trap**: "all roads lead to Rome" is Medieval Latin, first written in 1175 by Alain de Lille, *mille viae ducunt homines per saecula Romam*; Chaucer renders it in the *Astrolabe* prologue. It is not Roman self-description and should never be cited as evidence about the actual network ([All roads lead to Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/All_roads_lead_to_Rome)).

## 3. The crossing layer — bridges as institutions

- **Rope, log, stone, pontoon, basket**: the Inca repertoire is the richest documented set — log bridges on stone abutments (replaced about every eight years), stone-slab bridges, totora-reed pontoon rows, catenary rope bridges (replaced about every two years), and *oroya* hanging baskets spanning over 50 m ([Inca road system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system)).
- **The bridge as calendar**: Q'eswachaka, the last remaining Inca rope bridge (28 m, ichu grass, Apurímac at Huinchiri), is rewoven annually in the second week of June by four communities; UNESCO Intangible Cultural Heritage 2013. It collapsed in 2021 after pandemic maintenance lapses and again in 2025 through vandalism, and was rebuilt both times ([Queshuachaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queshuachaca)). Craft lesson: the maintenance rite *is* the bridge's constitution — design the festival before the structure.
- **Rank at the crossing**: who may cross, in what order, at whose cost, is a governance question. Give a fictional culture a bridge word that means both the structure and the obligation (*bridge-due*, *crossing-rank*), and the sumptuary layer writes itself (couples `culture-material/sumptuary-law-rank-display.md`).

## 4. The wheel layer — and its alternatives

- **Sequence (verified)**: rollers and sledges precede wheels; solid disc wheels and axle appear in the Copper Age (Ljubljana Marshes, Stare Gmajne, Slovenia: wheel 3340–3030 BCE, axle 3360–3045 BCE); two early wagon types are distinguished by whether the wheel-and-axle rotate together (circumalpine) or the axle is fixed (Baden culture), c. 3200–3000 BCE; spoked wheels are much later (Sintashta, c. 2000 BCE, Krivoye Lake); iron rims come with Celtic chariots in the 1st millennium BCE; wire spokes and pneumatic tires only in the 1870s ([Wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel)).
- **The alternative set**: sledge and travois, litter, riding, pack caravan, watercraft, and porterage. Wheeled transport is one branch, not the trunk — the article lists maglev, sleds, litters and walking as the modern alternatives for a reason.
- **Design question, not progress test**: ask of a fictional culture (1) is there a draft animal of the right build, (2) is the terrain and substrate wheel-friendly, (3) is there a road institution, and (4) does labor economics favor heads over axles. Mesoamerica answered "toys only" despite wheeled figurines from c. 1500 BCE; West Africa used wheeled vehicles largely ceremonially into the 19th century, with head porterage as the main method ([Wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel)).
- **Wheel vocabulary as symbolic capital**: the spoked wheel carried prestige in the Middle Bronze Age; the sun cross replaces solar-barge imagery with solar-chariot imagery; the wheel becomes *dharma*, the Wheel of Fortune, the Wheel of the Year ([Wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel)). Couple to `myth-symbol/celestial-symbolism-calendars.md` and `myth-symbol/symbolism-synthesis-method.md` before borrowing any of it — these are symbolic loans, not engineering.

## 5. The draft layer — harness, saddle, stirrup

- **Ox and yoke**: the yoke was made exclusive to each individual animal; a heavy plough could take up to sixteen oxen — the capital barrier that made the horse attractive once harness allowed it ([Horse collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_collar)). Vocabulary worth building: yoke terms carry bondage metaphors in many languages (pairing, subjugation, "passing under the yoke"), so a yoke word doubles as a political word in-world.
- **Horse collar sequence**: throat-and-girth harness (load on a strap around the barrel, restricting breathing) → breast collar (China, ancient-to-medieval period) → rigid padded collar over shoulders with hames and traces; earliest legitimate depiction on a Dunhuang cave 257 mural (Northern Wei, 477–499 CE); common in Europe by the 12th century ([Horse collar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horse_collar)).
- **The efficiency thesis is contested**: Lefebvre des Noëttes (1910) found throat-and-girth limited two horses to ~1,100 lb while one collared horse drew ~1.5 tons; Spruytte, Littauer and Raepsaet built more faithful reconstructions and found early harnesses pulling nearly as much, with the collar's real advantage in the lower point of attachment for ploughing. Use the sequence; do not sell the revolution.
- **Saddle and stirrup**: the solid saddle tree is the precondition for a load-bearing stirrup; toe loops appear in India by the 2nd century BCE; the earliest paired stirrups are Chinese Jin dynasty (mounting stirrup figurine 302 CE; double-sided representation 322 CE; extant double stirrups, mulberry wood with bronze and iron plates, from Feng Sufu's tomb, 415 CE); the iron stirrup reaches Europe with Avar material in 7th-century Hungary; Bachrach counted 13 stirrups in 704 eighth-century German male burials ([Stirrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirrup)).
- **The "Great Stirrup Controversy"**: White (1964) made the stirrup the enabler of mounted shock combat and so of feudalism; Bullough (1970) and Bachrach (1970) dispute it, arguing stirrups mainly improved balance and lateral fighting reach. House rule: the controversy is a generator (it produces two rival in-world doctrines of why cavalry rose), never a fact to assert.
- **Stirrup as rank word**: Ottoman *rikâb*/*üzengi* and Mughal *hazir-i rikab* ("presence of the stirrup") make the stirrup a metonym for the ruler's presence; Japanese *abumi* types run from cup-shaped *tsubo abumi* to the open-sided *fukuro abumi* designed so a fallen rider is not dragged, with *suiba abumi* perforated to drain river water ([Stirrup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirrup)). This is the kotodama seam: an implement's name becomes an office, a court phrase, a rank.
- **Load figures to copy (with units)**: llama ~30 kg at 20 km/day, up to 45 kg short trips; llama herds rather than carts because the animal is nimble on mountain roads ([Inca road system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_road_system)). Give every fictional pack animal a load, a daily distance, and a terrain preference; those three numbers generate caravan sizes, fodder demand and station spacing.

## 6. The water layer — hulls, rigs, wayfinding

- **Hull logics**: dugout (a tree's diameter caps the beam), skin-on-frame (light, repairable, Arctic), reed bundle (totora, papyrus), plank-built (sewn vs nailed; clinker vs carvel), outrigger (the stability solution that opens the open ocean), and the battened junk sail. The corpus already owns the Polynesian side — canoe cognates (Māori *waka*, Tongan *vaka*, Sāmoan *vaʻa*, Hawaiian *waʻa*), Thompson's star compass with swell and bird piloting as a mental bearing framework (`civilizations/polynesia-navigation.md:23,37,53,60`). Cite that file rather than restating it.
- **Wayfinding as a lexicon**: horizon star houses, swell direction, bird species, cloud over lagoon, and named winds. A fictional navigation register is a *closed technical vocabulary* — the same design move as the workshop lexicon in `culture-material/craft-artisan-lexicon.md`, with the same failure mode (terms that nobody aboard can act on are poetry, not seamanship).
- **Ports and the route word**: where the water layer meets the market layer, the vocabulary changes owners — the ship becomes a legal person (bottomry, respondentia in `culture-material/trade-economy-lexicon.md:136`), the voyage becomes a unit of risk, and the port becomes a register (couples `synthesis/contact-scenario-builder.md`).

## 7. The word layer — vehicle loans outlive vehicles

- **The physics**: a vehicle word arrives with the vehicle, is adapted to the recipient's phonology, and then outlives the object, shifting domains (coach → railway carriage → economy class → tutor). Four verified chains:
  - ***coach***: Middle English *coche* < Middle French < German *Kutsche* < Hungarian *kocsi (szekér)*, "wagon from Kocs" — first English use 1556; the tutor sense arrives in the 1840s from the idea of carrying a student through an examination ([Merriam-Webster](https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/coach), [Wiktionary *Kutsche*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/Kutsche)).
  - ***canoe***: English 1550s, West Indian context, from Spanish *canoa*, from Arawakan (Haiti) *canaoua*; generalized to dugouts; the idiom "paddle one's own canoe" by 1828 ([Etymonline](https://www.etymonline.com/word/canoe)).
  - ***rickshaw***: from Japanese *jinrikisha* (人力車); first English use 1879; the vehicle is c. 1869 Japan, replacing *kago* and *norimono* by 1872 — a Japanese vehicle written in Chinese characters, exported as a loanword, with the colonial-era French *pousse-pousse* as a parallel name ([Rickshaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rickshaw)).
  - ***junk***: English from Portuguese *junco*, from Javanese *djong* / Malay *adjong* "large vessel"; a competing derivation from Chinese *chuán* exists in the literature — mark the dispute, keep both names if your world has two ship traditions ([New World Encyclopedia](https://www.newworldencyclopedia.org/entry/Junk_(ship)); divergence noted via the HandWiki *Djong* entry).
- **Craft rules for the word layer**: (1) type the loan — a vehicle word that passes through the recipient's phonotactics is `borrowed-strata`, and it opens a second clock: the word can enter before the vehicle arrives in numbers, or after (couples `synthesis/loanword-material-dating.md:43-57`); (2) never date a road, a saddle, or a ship type by its loanword's oldest attestation; (3) give the culture a native coinage for the thing it invented and a loan for the thing it imported — the split is evidence of the contact story, and it is the cheapest way to make a fictional transport lexicon look lived-in; (4) the vehicle word often becomes the *office* (coachman, *kusk* from German *Kutscher*, rickshaw-runner, chasqui as a role, *rikâb* as a court term) — plan the role noun with the vehicle noun.
- **`to-verify` items carried from this round**: the 17th-century French "vinaigrette" hand-carriage as a rickshaw predecessor (reported from Mellot & Blancart 2006 via the Rickshaw article; not adjudicated); the rival rickshaw-inventor claims (Goble, Tolman, Birch — the article presents them as theories); the Chinese-*chuán* derivation of *junk*; the claim that spoked-wheel horse cultures "moved deep into the Greek peninsula" as a migration narrative (traditional framing on the Wheel page; the broader steppe-migration story is contested in current archaeology); and any quantitative harness figure beyond the Lefebvre des Noëttes experiments and their critiques.
