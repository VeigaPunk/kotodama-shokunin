## 1. How games and play are classified

Compare the frameworks instead of choosing one. An ethnographic classification of games by chance, skill, and physical action, an account of play as constitutive of culture, a set of play categories, a survey of play's competing rhetorics, and the anthropology of sport answer different questions; a design may borrow from several without treating any as the definition.
- Culin divides tribal games into chance and dexterity types, excluding chess-like calculation and documenting geographic variation. (S750)
- Participants dramatize local values, staging civic institutions before a shared audience. (S756)
- Culture and play can recast existing festivals as durable rites of local authority. (S757)
- Caillois classifies play by whether competition, chance, simulation, or vertigo dominates, allowing several rubrics to combine in one game. (S758)
- Sutton-Smith treats rival play theories as rhetorics debated across disciplines from biology to sociology rather than settled facts. (S759)

## 4. Ballgames, ball materials and stick games

Separate what a ball is made of, where it is played, and what the game is taken to mean. Material evidence for rubber and solid balls is not by itself evidence for the scale or meaning of a contest.
- Maya ballgame shaped political, religious, and social life beyond athletic competition. (S761)
- Olmec offerings paired jade axes with rubber balls, leaving their role as sporting equipment uncertain. (S762)
- World Lacrosse's history traces the genesis of its sport to Indigenous peoples of North America playing stick-game variations by the 12th century. (S771)

## 5. Athletic and ritual contests

Keep festival setting, contest procedure, and the status of competitors as three separate design variables. A contest can be ritual in framing, organised in procedure, and political in consequence at the same time.
- Teams each pull one end of a rope during village rites, treating the contest as a communal ceremony rather than a sport. (S754)
- Historians caution that Greek athletes were not modern amateurs. (S760)
- Clapping, attention to gods, and extended arms mark a scripted ceremony preceding competition. (S772)

## 6. Lots, dice and gambling toys

Treat the chance device as an object with a history: bone, stone, clay, or a spinning body with marked faces. Wager, divination, and children's play may attach to the same object without being the same practice.
- Astragaloi, used as gaming pieces in antiquity, also appear in Greek graves, complicating a play-only reading. (S755)
- Folsom-period deposits in Wyoming, Colorado, and New Mexico produced the earliest known dice, placing gaming in the late Pleistocene. (S767)
- London players marked top faces with letters — take all, half, put down, nothing — to settle each spin’s stake. (S768)

## 7. Cards

Describe cards as manufactured objects: paper, suits, faces, and the collections that preserve them. A deck's design is evidence of production and trade before it is evidence of a rule set.
- Woodblock printing made cards cheap to produce in sheets, widening play beyond the rich to every social level. (S766)

## 8. Children's playground repertoire

Record children's games as repertoire that children transmit to each other: counting-out formulas, skipping, singing and ring games, seasonal rounds, and local variants.
- The blurb groups children's rhymes, jeers, and games as everyday child culture beyond adult-approved nursery entertainments. (S769)
- Gomme splits the repertoire into two divisions, descriptive and singing or choral, before cataloguing variants. (S770)

## 10. Design prompts

Use the rows as conditional design prompts, not prescriptions. Select an option, combine alternatives, or specify exceptions for a named setting.

| Worldbuilder choice | Possible consequence for play scenes | Possible consequence for social structure |
|---|---|---|
| A game is decided by a chance device | Show the device, the throw, and the reading of the result rather than the outcome alone. | Decide who may hold the device, who wagers, and what a loss transfers. |
| A game rewards skill and strategy | Make training, memory, and calculation visible as competence. | Tie rank or office to demonstrated play skill, or refuse to. |
| A contest is attached to a festival or ritual calendar | Fix the occasion, the order of events, and the spectators in the scene. | Decide which offices stage, sponsor, or arbitrate the contest. |
| A ballgame requires special equipment | Specify the ball's material, the court, and who can supply or repair them. | Decide whether equipment access marks status or is communal. |

| A game is borrowed from a neighbouring region | Mark the route, the renamed pieces, and the local reinterpretation. | Decide who claims the game's origin and what that claim legitimizes. |

## 11. Cross-cultural patterns

These are cross-row readings of the bullets above, not new source claims; no citation is added here.

- Definitions of play disagree in a way a design can use: one account separates play from ordinary life by a boundary and a mood, another sorts play into categories of competition, chance, pretence, and vertigo, and a third treats play's meanings as multiple rhetorics rather than one essence. Choosing a definition is a design decision, not a finding.
- Board and lot games recur across regions as portable assemblages of a marked surface, movable pieces, and a chance device or counting rule. Classification by chance versus skill cuts across those finds rather than dividing cultures.
- Elite and mortuary settings preserve games that ordinary play rarely leaves visible: a royal grave or a tomb assemblage can fix a board's form while leaving its rules unrecoverable.
- Board games travel with trade, conquest, and scholarship, and the further a game is from its documented occurrence, the more of its rule set is inference. Origin claims and transmission routes are statements about sources, not about a game's essence.
- Ballgame evidence couples equipment and institution: a worked rubber ball and a built court appear together with political and ritual use, so a design that keeps the ball but drops the occasion loses the documented relation.
- Contests staged on an occasion bind procedure to calendar and spectatorship. Festival setting, eligibility of competitors, and the arbitration of outcomes are separable choices even when a source reports them together.
- Chance devices cross the boundaries between divination, wager, and children's play: the same bone, die, or spinning top can carry different practices in different hands. Describe the object and then state which practice it serves.
- Children's play is transmitted as repertoire rather than as a fixed curriculum: counting-out formulas, singing games, and seasonal rounds persist with local variation and without adult instruction.
- Manufactured play objects show standardisation and its limits: dice settle into a small set of conventional forms while card faces and suits remain regionally variable.
- Team contests and stick games are claimed both as community practice and as heritage, and the claim itself carries authority: origin accounts, governing bodies, and museum collections can describe the same game under different ownership.
- Organised sport is a cultural institution in its own right: organisation, spectatorship, and identity are available as objects of description alongside the play itself.
- Documented play spans adult contest and children's playground repertoire, and the two are collected by different methods: ethnographic catalogues, schoolyard observation, and printed collections each see a different slice.
- Betting and race play attach wager to a board or a track, so a design can keep the board, the stake, and the adjudication as three separate features.
- Throwing and knucklebone games share a minimal kit: an object to throw and a target or a fall to read. Elaborate rule sets are a later layer over that kit.

## 12. Limits and evidence

Evidence limits: no single source gives a universal history of play. Board rules are often reconstructed from boards, pieces, and later texts rather than directly recorded; museum and survey pages describe objects and collections, not universal practice. This register samples particular regions and periods, so absence here is not evidence that a game is absent from a culture. Treat each bullet as scoped to its cited source; the registry's source, scope and quote cells carry the evidence boundary.
