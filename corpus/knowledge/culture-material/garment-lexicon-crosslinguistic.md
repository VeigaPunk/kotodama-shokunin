## 1. The cutting kinship: shirt / skirt / sheath

- **Doublet mechanics**: *shirt* < OE *scyrte*; *skirt* < ON *skyrta* (Danelaw loan); both < PGmc \**skurtijō(n)-* 'short garment' < PIE \**sker-* 'to cut' — same root family as *short* (OE *scort* < \**skurta-*). One Norse borrowing split the field: the native form kept 'upper garment', the Norse form took 'lower garment' (Onions 1966; Kroonen 2013; OED s.vv.). Named heuristic: **doublets don't stay synonyms — they carve the field**. Borrowed/native pairs routinely become register or slot contrasts (see §4–5).
- **One root, three garment slots** — cognates drift independently in sister languages:

| Cognate | Language | Garment slot |
|---|---|---|
| shirt | English | upper body, under layer |
| skirt | English | lower body, outer |
| skjorta / skjorte | Swedish / Danish | shirt (upper) — kept the "shirt" sense |
| Schürze | German | apron — 'short cloth' sense (same \**skur-* 'short/cut' family; exact OHG chain: Kluge/Pfeifer) |

- **Sheath**: OE *scēaþ* < PGmc \**skēþō-*, standardly tied to PIE \**skei-* 'to cut, split' — a sheath is "the split thing" (split wood/leather forming a scabbard) (OED; Kroonen 2013). So the whole cluster is **manufacture-metonymy**: garments named by how they are made (cut cloth), containers by how they are made (split material). You name a thing by its making.
- **Calibration**: the \**sker-* / \**skei-* sibling-'cut' pair is standard comparative material; the exact PIE shapes are reconstructions and drift between editions — in canon docs cite the dictionaries, not the asterisked forms.
- **Design lever**: choose the culture's dominant derivation strategy for dress words — act of making (*cut-, wrap-, bind-, sew-*), body part (kataginu = *kata* 'shoulder' + *gi* 'wearing'), dimension (*short*-thing → shirt), or function (yukata < *yu* 'hot water' + *katabira* 'thin garment': bath dress → summer wear). One dominant strategy reads as one culture; two strata read as contact history.

## 2. Name politics: qipao / cheongsam / changshan

- **旗袍 qípáo** 'banner robe' — named for the *qírén* 旗人, the Manchu Banner population. The garment as known is a 1920s Shanghai restyling of Manchu women's dress into modern urban fashion: the ethnic name stuck while the garment's social meaning inverted. Finnane (2008) documents the Republican-era debates over it as national dress vs Manchu vestige; Clark (2000) is the monograph on the Cantonese-named version.
- **長衫 chángshān / Cantonese coeng4saam1 → "cheongsam"** 'long gown' — a shape/length name, historically the *male* gown. In English the Cantonese loanword settled on the woman's dress; in Hong Kong usage 長衫 also names the female robe, in Mandarin-world usage 旗袍 does. Same robe, two names: one ethnic-political (qipao), one descriptive (cheongsam) — and **the loanword switched referent in transit** (Mandarin male gown → English female dress).
- **Parallel**: Vietnamese áo dài 'long shirt' — again a length name, reattached to a redesigned garment in the 1930s; the same decade cheongsam's English career began. Shape-names (long, short, half) are the neutrally productive naming channel; ethnic tags are the politically loaded one.
- **Design levers**: (a) give contested garments *two coexisting names* — ethnic tag vs shape description — assigned to different speech communities; (b) renaming events (revolution, independence, sumptuary law) are free plot; (c) a loanword that changed referent mid-travel works as an in-world shibboleth (the HK tailor and the Shanghai customer say different words for the same dress).

## 3. Kamishimo and the set-name; kimono's fall from generic

- **裃 / 上下 kamishimo** = *kami* 'upper' + *shimo* 'lower': Edo-period formal wear — kataginu (sleeveless shoulder-piece) + hakama — named by **merism**: the outfit *is* its two halves, cited in canonical order. Dvandva-style set-names recur cross-linguistically: Hindi *kurta-pyjama*, Japanese *haori-hakama*, Korean *chima-jeogori* (skirt-jacket) (Dalby 1993; Milhaupt 2014).
- **着物 kimono** = *kiru* 'to wear' + *mono* 'thing' — literally "the thing worn": historically the *generic* word for clothing. After 洋服 *yōfuku* 'Western clothes' arrived, the generic slot split into a wafuku/yōfuku pair and **kimono narrowed from 'clothing' to 'the robe'** (Dalby 1993; Shibatani 1990; Milhaupt 2014). The textbook case of generic→specific narrowing under lexical competition.
- **Design levers**: (a) when two dress systems meet, expect a new generic, old generics narrowing, and a native/foreign lexical border (wafuku/yōfuku) that then maps onto class, generation, and occasion; (b) merismatic set-names ('upper-lower') are cheap to coin and instantly legible; (c) a displaced generic survives in compounds and fixed phrases — plant fossils (English *dress* in *dress code*, *dress circle*; *kimono-ya* shop names).

## 4. Loan vs coin: how wardrobe words travel

- **Garment words are prototypical cultural borrowings** (Weinreich 1953's cultural-vs-core distinction): the artifact travels with its word — blouse (Fr.), pyjama (Urdu/Persian *pāy-jāma* 'leg-covering'), kimono, anorak (Kalaallisut *annoraaq*), poncho (Mapudungun), sari (Skt *śāṭī*), khaki (Urdu/Persian *khākī* 'dust-colored'), muumuu (Hawaiian), parka (via Russian; donor Samoyedic/Sámi — debated, to-verify). Haspelmath & Tadmor (2009) find clothing/grooming among the more heavily borrowed semantic fields cross-linguistically.
- **Loan chains**: *fez* < Fez (Morocco toponym; Ottoman reform dress under Mahmud II, 1829) → outlawed by the 1925 Hat Law and replaced by *şapka* < Russian *šapka* (itself from a Germanic cap-word; Vasmer; Nişanyan) (Zürcher 1993). Three garment words, three source cultures, one century of political wardrobe. Note the **valence flip**: the fez was the modernizer's garment in 1829 and the obstacle to modernity by 1925.
- **Coining strategies when loans are blocked or styled out**:

| Strategy | Example | Note |
|---|---|---|
| calque compound | German *Handschuh*, Dutch *handschoen* 'hand-shoe' = glove | body-part + container logic; transparently native |
| semi-phonetic hybrid | Mandarin T恤 *T-xù* | letter-shape metonymy + sound-chosen character |
| descriptive coinage | 连衣裙 *liányīqún* 'connected shirt-skirt' (one-piece dress) | names the cut |
| relexified pseudo-loan | Japanese ワンピース *wanpīsu* 'one-piece' = dress; セーター *sētā* | borrowed phonology, native concept-cut |
| letter-shape metonymy | T-shirt, A-line, V-neck, Y-fronts | productive in industrial eras |
| diminutive morphology | hoodie, undies | English's live coinage machine |

- **Register stratification of strata**: native vs borrowed = humble vs elevated — *shirt* (Germanic) vs *blouse* (Fr.); *clothes* vs *attire, raiment, vestments* (Latinate formal). Assign etymological stratum to social classes and occasions; readers feel the hierarchy without being told.
- **Hobson-Jobson (Yule & Burnell 1886) is the model trove**: pyjama, bandana (Hindi *bāndhnū* 'tie-dye' < Skt *bandhana* 'binding'), chintz (Hindi *chīṇṭ* < Skt *citra* 'variegated'; later pejorated to *chintzy*), dungaree (< Dongri, Bombay quarter — toponym), calico, shawl (Persian *shāl*). Colonial trade = wardrobe-vocabulary churn; a period glossary is a dated archive worth imitating in-world.

## 5. Generic vs specific competition

- **Frameworks**: onomasiology (many names for one concept) vs semasiology (one word, many senses) — Geeraerts (2010); change mechanisms (narrowing, broadening, metonymy, eponymy) — Blank (1997); Traugott & Dasher (2002). For every wardrobe word ask: **which slot did it hold before?**
- **dress**: < OF *drecier* 'to set straight, arrange' → verb 'to attire' → noun 'attire in general' → narrowed to the one-piece woman's garment; the general sense survives in compounds (*dress code*, *dress circle*, *dressed*). German *Kleid* holds both jobs at once ('dress'; *Kleidung* 'clothing') — live generic-specific polysemy.
- **The coat cascade**: *coat* (general outer garment) → *petticoat* (petit + coat: originally a small/padded under-coat, then underskirt) → *waistcoat* (coat cut/worn at the waist; AmE *vest*) → *overcoat* needs a modifier. The children left home and the parent narrowed; each derivative is a dated stratum.
- **Displaced generics**: *clothes* < OE *clāþ* 'cloth' (material→object metonymy); *habit* < L *habitus* 'condition, attire' → narrowed to monastic and riding habit; *weeds* 'garments' (OE *wǣd*), fossilized in *widow's weeds*. A retiring generic leaves fossils — collect them; they are free period texture.
- **Regional register split for one referent**: *frock* (BrE, dated/informal) / *dress* (neutral) / *gown* (formal). Transatlantic trap: AmE *vest* 'waistcoat' (< Fr *veste*) vs BrE *vest* 'undershirt' — false friends inside one language from one French source.
- **Design lever**: a believable wardrobe lexicon shows exactly one live generic (or two fighting, kimono-style), a fossil layer of narrowed generics, and register-split specifics. Competition, not inventory, is what reads as real.

## 6. Classifiers: how a language ontologizes garments

| Language | Counter | Counts | Implied ontology |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mandarin | 件 *jiàn* | shirts, coats, dresses (一件衬衫) | garment = discrete artifact |
| Mandarin | 条 *tiáo* | trousers, skirts, scarves (一条裤子) | lower garment = long flexible thing |
| Mandarin | 套 *tào* | suits, ensembles (一套西装) | outfit = enclosing set |
| Mandarin | 身 *shēn* | full-body wear (一身衣服) | outfit = second body |
| Japanese | 枚 *mai* | shirts, flat items (ワイシャツ一枚) | garment = flat sheet |
| Japanese | 着 *gi* | kimono, suits (着物一着) | garment = thing-worn-as-a-set (from *kiru*) |
| Korean | 벌 *beol* | clothing sets (옷 한 벌) | outfit = matched set |
| Korean | 켤레 *kyeolle* | shoes, socks (신발 한 켤레) | footwear = pair |
| Vietnamese | chiếc / cái | individual garments (chiếc áo) | garment = individuated thing |
| Vietnamese | bộ | sets (bộ đồ) | outfit = set |
| Thai | ตัว *tua* | shirts, jackets (เสื้อหนึ่งตัว) | garment = a **body** |
| Thai | ชุด *chut* | suits (ชุดสูท) | outfit = set |

(sources for the table: Aikhenvald 2000; Li & Thompson 1981; Norman 1988; Shibatani 1990; Sohn 1999; Iwasaki & Ingkaphirom 2005; Thompson 1987)

- **English used to have this**: the plural-pair classifier — "a pair of breeches / drawers / hose", and 16th–17th-c. **"a pair of bodies"** for a corset: the garment came as two symmetric halves laced together, so it was counted like scissors (Arnold 1985; Tiramani et al. 2011; OED s.v. *pair*). Standardization flattened the system, leaving only the trousers/pants/scissors residue. A classifier system is what English lost, not what it never had.
- **What counter choice encodes**: piece-cultures commodify (shop talk: 件), set-cultures dress persons (벌), body-cultures sacralize (ตัว; 身). Counting the same robe by 'body' at the altar and by 'piece' in the market is register you can play scene by scene.
- **Design lever**: fix the culture's garment ontology — piece / set / body — *before* coining words; counters and honorifics then do half the worldbuilding. A mismatch (sacred robes counted in trade counters) is characterization, not error, if someone notices in-world.

## 7. Eponyms and toponyms: war and place in the wardrobe

- **Eponym clusters date eras**: the Crimean War (1853–56) minted *balaclava* (battle site), *cardigan* (7th Earl of Cardigan), *raglan* (Lord Raglan) — one war, one garment lexicon (OED; Onions 1966). *Pantaloon* < Pantalone, the Venetian merchant of commedia dell'arte — a stage costume became a trouser word (and English *pants* rides on it). *Bloomers*: named for Amelia Bloomer, who popularized (did not design — Elizabeth Smith Miller did) the 1851 dress-reform garment (OED; Picken 1957).
- **Toponym under-layer — cloth words are place names that garments ride on**:

| Word | From |
|---|---|
| denim | serge *de Nîmes* |
| jeans | *Gênes* (Genoa) — fabric name first, pants second |
| calico | Calicut |
| muslin | Mosul (traditional trade etymology) |
| damask | Damascus |
| satin | Arabic *zāytūnī* 'of Zaytūn' (Quanzhou) |
| fustian | Fusṭāṭ (Cairo) (traditional) |
| kersey / worsted | Suffolk / Norfolk villages — home weave |
| fez | Fez, Morocco |
| gauze | Gaza — traditional and doubted (to-verify) |

(OED; Onions 1966)

- **Design lever**: every trade route leaves toponym cloth-words; every war leaves eponym garments; every costume-era stage character can mint a word. The wardrobe is a **dated stratigraphic archive** — hand lore-shokunin the method, not the canon decisions.

## 8. Calibration (contested or misremembered — say this plainly in any lore doc)

- *shirt/skirt/short* < \**sker-* 'cut' is solid (Kroonen 2013; Watkins 2011); *sheath* < \**skei-* 'split' is the standard account, but proto-forms are reconstructions — cite dictionaries, not asterisked forms, in canon.
- *parka*'s donor (Sámi vs Nenets via Russian) is stated both ways in references — verify before use (to-verify). Vietnamese *áo* 'upper garment' is commonly derived from Chinese ǎo 袄 'padded jacket' — plausible and standard in Sinitic-loan discussions, but confirm against Thompson (1987) before canon (to-verify).
- *muslin* < Mosul and *fustian* < Fusṭāṭ are plausible traditional trade-name etymologies cited by the OED, not proven manufacturing origins; *gauze* < Gaza is frankly traditional.
- *şapka* is from Russian *šapka*, **not** directly from French *chapeau* — a common folk-correction error; Russian *šapka* is itself from a Germanic cap-word (Vasmer; Nişanyan).
- qipao politics: popular "Han reclamation of Manchu dress" narratives outrun the scholarship; Finnane (2008) supports naming debates and Republican-era stigma, not clean modern-vs-feudal stories. Attribute carefully.
- kimono's generic→specific narrowing is standard kimono historiography (Dalby 1993; Milhaupt 2014) — a strong modern-period pattern, not a law of lexical change.
- Borrowability: Haspelmath & Tadmor (2009) support clothing/grooming as a high-borrowing field; donor direction and prestige still have to be argued per contact situation, never assumed.

## 9. Applying to a fictional culture — craft checklist

1. Fix the garment ontology first: piece-, set-, or body-culture → counters and honorifics follow from it.
2. Pick a dominant derivation strategy for dress words (manufacture-act / body-part / dimension / function); let a second stratum show contact history.
3. Install at least one doublet with slot division (native humble vs borrowed fancy, shirt/skirt pattern).
4. Give one contested garment two coexisting names — ethnic tag vs shape description — politically assigned to speech communities.
5. Stage one generic-competition event: new generic arrives, old generic narrows, fossils survive in compounds and set phrases.
6. Mint an eponym cluster after a war and a toponym family along a trade route.
7. Let a state rename a garment once (Hat Law pattern: toponym-loan out, foreign-loan in); legal naming is worldbuilding gold.
8. Leave register traps: same word, two referents across dialects (AmE/BrE *vest* pattern).
9. Write the counting scene: ritual robe counted by 'body', market robe by 'piece'.
10. Handoff discipline: this file supplies method + comparative data only; which words exist in-world is lore-shokunin's canon call.
