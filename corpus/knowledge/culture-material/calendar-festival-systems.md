## 1. The three clocks and the unavoidable lie

- Constants: mean solar day 1; synodic month 29.53059 d; tropical year 365.2422 d. Twelve lunar months = 354.37 → 10.88 d short of the year; twelve 30-day months = 360 → 5.24 short; 19 tropical years ≈ 235 lunations to within ~2 hours (the Metonic fit). Julian 365.25 drifts ~1 d per 128 yr; Gregorian 365.2425 ≈ 1 d per 3,300 yr against the mean tropical year, nearer 7,700 against the vernal-equinox year — name your yardstick when quoting accuracy.
- Every calendar pays a debt somewhere. The design questions: who pays, when, and does the payer get a festival? The sky-observation layer lives in myth-symbol/celestial-symbolism-calendars §8; this file is what happens below the observatory — decree, arithmetic, tax, feast.

| Type | Mechanism | Exemplars | Signature consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pure lunar | 12 months, no seasonal anchor | Islamic (observational and tabular variants) | festivals migrate through every season in ~33 yr; the fast crosses all weathers — strong cultural differentiator |
| Lunisolar | 12 or 13 months, by cycle or by observation | Babylonian, Hebrew, Chinese, Hindu, pre-1873 Japan/Korea | leap months owned by an authority (§2); festivals stay seasonal but float within a month |
| Solar civil | 365 / 365.25 / 365.2425 + patch | Egyptian; Julian; Gregorian; Persian Solar Hijri; Coptic/Ethiopian | months detach from the moon; "month" becomes a vocabulary fossil (OE mōnaþ from mōna — OED s.v. month) |
| Procedural | cycles defined by fiat; astronomy optional | Maya tzolkʼin–haabʼ interlock; Balinese pawukon; Aztec 260/365 | unrelated wheels grinding against each other; coincidence days sacralized; no astronomers required |
| Dual mismatch | two calendars run in parallel, reconciled ad hoc | Athens (festival months vs 10 prytanies, Ath. Pol. 43); Bali (Saka lunisolar alongside pawukon); Ottoman mālī vs hijrī | contracts in one calendar, feasts in another; built-in legal and devotional friction |

## 2. Lunisolar intercalation: four strategies and the politics of the leap

| Strategy | How | Exemplars | Worldbuilding handle |
|---|---|---|---|
| Ad-hoc decree | priests or king judge ripeness (equinox, star, barley) and insert a month late in winter | early Babylonian; Second Temple Judaism (witness testimony, signal fires — Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 1–2); Rome before 46 BCE | maximum priesthood power; every declared leap is an event with motives |
| Fixed cycle | 7 leap years per 19, scheduled in advance | Babylonian 19-yr scheme regularized by the 5th–4th c. BCE (Parker & Dubberstein 1956); Greek "Metonic" (Diodorus 12.36: Meton announces at Olympia, 432 BCE — Babylonian priority predates the Greek name); Hebrew fixed calendar (traditionally credited to Hillel II, 359 CE — contested; Stern 2001) | discretion removed, power moves to arithmeticians |
| Doctrinal prohibition | intercalation forbidden by revelation | Islamic; pre-Islamic nasīʾ condemned (Quran 9:36–37) | time left deliberately untuned; a theological statement that the divine clock needs no committee |
| Absorbed anomaly | the intercalary unit gains a status of its own | Hindu adhika māsa ("not-month", Malamasa, rehabilitated by Vishnu as Purushottama month — Padma Purana tradition); Latin Mensis Intercalaris/Mercedonius appended after a truncated February; Chinese leap month = the month lacking a major solar term (zhongqi) | the patch can be inauspicious, salvaged, or married into the system's mythology |

- Hebrew refinement: leap years are 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17, 19 of the cycle (Adar I/II); postponement rules (deḥiyyot) stretch or compress individual years — lengths run 353–385 days, so the most "processed" of ancient calendars needs tables, not moons. The new-moon moment (molad) is computed, announced, and ceremonially meaningful.
- Chinese: the 24 solar terms (jieqi) form a solar spine inside the lunisolar body — which is why pure-solar observances like Qingming keep near-fixed Gregorian dates (§8) while everything tithi- or month-based floats.
- Politics: Macrobius (Sat. 1.14) reports Roman pontiffs stretching or skipping intercalation to lengthen friendly magistrates' terms. Whoever declares the leap owns feast days, tax dates, and office terms — in-world, the office that forgives time is a murder-motive generator.
- Kotodama hooks: Second Temple intercalation declared by proclamation and signal fire (speech and light as legal acts); the leap month keeps a liminal name (Adar II; rùnyuè "inserted month"); the molad announced in synagogue — time-law as recitation.

## 3. Drift by design: the Egyptian 365 and its children

- Structure: 3 seasons (Akhet inundation, Peret emergence, Shemu harvest/low water) × 4 months × 30 days + 5 epagomenal days = the birthdays of Osiris, Horus (Arueris), Seth, Isis, Nephthys (Plutarch, De Iside 12). 36 decans give ten-day "weeks" (sky layer: sibling file §4).
- The 0.25 d/yr debt: the civil new year walks through the seasons and meets the true Sothic new year (heliacal rising of Sopdet/Sirius) once every ~1460 Julian (1461 civil) years; Censorinus (De die natali 21) marks a renewal at 139 CE. A handful of recorded Sothic risings (Middle Kingdom onward) are what make Egyptian chronology computable — a drifting calendar is a historian's gift and an administrator's nightmare (Parker 1950). Farmers in any case timed work by flood and stars, not by the civil date: the civil and natural calendars are separate institutions.
- The failed fix: the Canopus decree (238 BCE, Ptolemy III) proposed a 6th epagomenal day every 4 years plus a new festival for the Benefactor Gods; the priesthoods declined, and a leap day arrived only with the Alexandrian calendar under Rome. Reform offered as royal gift, refused for centuries.
- Children: the Coptic and Ethiopian calendars keep the exact shape (12×30 + Pagume 5/6; the Ethiopian year runs 7–8 years behind the Gregorian) — a ~2,300-year-old design still alive in liturgy.
- Priestly-schism parallel: the Qumran/Enochic 364-day solar calendar (1 Enoch 72–82; Jubilees 6: four quarters of 13 weeks; 364 = 52×7, so every festival falls on a fixed weekday, no intercalation needed). Its clash with the lunisolar Jerusalem mainstream is calendrical schism as identity politics.
- Worldbuilder's gold: a deliberate drift calendar means every festival migrates across the seasons over centuries — "the Harvest Queen crowned in midwinter" is a real consequence, and the debate over fixing the year becomes an era-defining crisis.

## 4. Reform as political act: Julian, Gregorian, and the adoption map

- Caesar, 46 BCE: the "last year of confusion" (445 days by the standard count, Macrobius Sat. 1.14), a ¼-day leap rule, Alexandrian consultant Sosigenes (Pliny NH 18.211). Augustus then had to suspend misapplied leap years until 8 CE — the first patch release. January begins the year because consuls entered office then (since 153 BCE); Caesar did not invent the January new year.
- Gregory XIII, bull Inter gravissimas (24 Feb 1582): Thursday 4 Oct 1582 followed by Friday 15 Oct; century-leap exception (÷400); Easter re-anchored (§7). Adoption is a confession of jurisdiction:

| Adoption | Who |
|---|---|
| 1582–87 | Catholic core: Spain, Portugal, Poland–Lithuania, most Italian states, France; Bavaria and allies 1583– |
| 1583–1700 | Protestant Germany, Denmark (1700), Dutch provinces one by one (Holland first; Groningen last, 1700/01) |
| 1752 | Britain and empire (Wed 2 Sept → Thu 14 Sept); Sweden had already muddled through a staggered 1700–1712 transition that once produced a 30 February |
| 19th c. | Japan 1873 (Meiji 6: Dec 3 1872 → Jan 1 1873); Egypt (civil) 1875 |
| 20th c. | China 1912 (dual-calendar life ever since); Soviet Russia 1918 (31 Jan → 14 Feb); Greece 1923 (last in Europe); Turkey 1926 |

- Sequelae: Old Style/New Style double dating ("born 11 Feb 1731 OS / 22 Feb 1732 NS" — Washington's two birthdays); the British tax year moved to 5 April (1753) and again to 6 April (1800) so no revenue days were lost — fiscal time outlives reform and quietly rewrites it.
- Calibration — the "calendar riots of 1752" are largely mythical: the slogan "Give us our Eleven Days!" is known from Hogarth's satire; documented disturbances are not (Poole 1995). Popular histories still print riot scenes; a lore bible must not.
- Failed global projects: the World Calendar (Achelis, 1930; League of Nations → UN, shelved 1955, in part over sabbath-continuity objections to a perpetual year); the International Fixed Calendar (Cotsworth; adopted by Kodak 1928–1989: 13 months × 28 days + "Year Day"); the Pax calendar (intercalary week). The recurring killer: religions whose rest day must run uninterrupted forever.
- Soviet week wars: 1929 nepreryvka (continuous 5-day week; rest staggered by color — yellow, orange, red, purple, green — one worker in five off each day, no shared rest day), 1931 shestidnevka (6-day), June 1940 return to the 7-day week. Intent: dissolve the weekend as sacred/free-time institution; result: unpopularity, then repeal. The best-attested case of week engineering as social engineering.

## 5. Designed calendars: the French Republican case study

- Decree of 5 October 1793, names by Fabre d'Églantine: 12 months × 3 décades × 10 days; poetic month names keyed to season and husbandry (Vendémiaire vintage, Brumaire mist, Frimaire frost; Nivôse snow, Pluviôse rain, Ventôse wind; Germinal sprouting, Floréal blossom, Prairial meadow; Messidor harvest, Thermidor heat, Fructidor fruit); décade days Primidi–Décadi; ordinary days renamed for plants, animals, farm tools — a rural almanac as civic catechism. Epoch: 22 September 1792 (Republic proclaimed, near the autumn equinox) = 1 Vendémiaire An I; new year defined by true equinox at Paris.
- Decimals everywhere: 10-hour day, 100-minute hour, decimal second — clock faces were actually manufactured; decimal time was suspended in April 1795, the calendar abolished effective 1 January 1806. The décade's 9:1 work/rest ratio was hated by bodies trained to the 7-day rhythm: week systems have bodies attached (Zerubavel 1985).
- Sansculottides: 5–6 complementary days closing the year, festivals of Virtue, Genius, Labour, Opinion, Rewards (+ Revolution in leap years) — saints' days replaced by civic virtue days. A complete kit for fictional revolutions: rename time, decimalize it, re-anchor the epoch to the founding act, legislate the festivals.
- Lineage of the designed calendar: Comte's Positivist Calendar (1849; 13 months named Moses … Bichat) — the same move for a religion of humanity; the Persian Solar Hijri (law of 1925) kept equinox astronomy and pre-Islamic Zoroastrian month names (Farvardin, Khordad = Haurvatat, Mehr = Mithra, Bahman = Vohu Manah …) — calendar as re-legitimation from an older sacred register, on the Jalali precedent of Malik-Shah's Isfahan commission (1079).

## 6. Weeks: the seven-day empire and its rivals

- The 7-day week is not celestial: 29.53/4 ≈ 7.4 gives lunar-quarter omens (Neo-Babylonian 7th/14th/21st/28th "evil days", via Zerubavel 1985), but the continuous, moon-independent, rest-bearing week is Jewish, then planetary-Hellenistic (planetary hours order the days; attested at Pompeii; sibling file §3 has the mechanism). Christianity (Lord's Day) and later global commerce built the empire.
- Day-name systems are contact-history receipts: planetary calques (Latin → Germanic Tiw→Mars etc.; Japanese 七曜 via the 8th-c. Buddhist Xiuyao jing); numbered days (Portuguese segunda-feira …, feira "fair day"; Slavic ponedel'nik "after-do-nothing", vtornik "second" …, subbota ← Hebrew shabbat; Icelandic hybrid — only Sun- and Moon-day planetary, else þriðjudagur "third", föstudagur "fast-day", laugardagur "bath-day"); church-replacements (German Mittwoch "midweek" retiring Wodan). "Week" itself (OE wice) means "a turning" — the unit is rotation, not astronomy (OED s.v. week).

| Week length | Culture | Anchor | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3-day (triwara) | Bali | market | one of ten concurrent weeks (pawukon row below) |
| 5-day (pasaran) | Java | market/dirge etyma | Legi, Pahing, Pon, Wage, Kliwon; combined with the 7-day into the 35-day weton cycle read for birth-day character — a divinatory weekday industry |
| 5 / 6-day | USSR 1929 / 1931 | state | no shared rest day (§4) |
| 7-day | Jewish → planetary → global | rest day | the incumbent |
| 8-day (nundinal) | Rome | market (nundinae) | letters A–H in the fasti; superseded by the planetary week in the imperial period |
| 10-day | Egypt (decans), China (xún 旬), France 1793 (décade) | astronomical / state | three independent coinages of one wheel; the décade is the cautionary tale |
| 13-day (trecena) | Mesoamerica | 260-day sacred round | 13 stacked on 20 = tzolkʼin (sibling file §8) |
| 10 concurrent weeks | Bali (pawukon) | none — pure interlock | yields the 210-day "year"; Galungan recurs on it (Eiseman 1989) |

- Market weeks vs rest weeks: most alternatives are market-day counts; the Jewish seventh day is rest-without-market — a religious innovation riding on an economic rhythm. Decide a culture's week from its market/temple cadence, then decide which institution defends it.
- Kotodama hooks: Japanese rokuyō (senshō, tomobiki, senbu, butsumetsu, taian, shakkō) — a luck lexicon riding on the week, still consulted: weddings on taian "great peace", funerals avoided on tomobiki "pulling-a-friend"; Roman dies fasti/nefasti and dies ateri (black days) — the law itself is timed; a week can carry a per-day register of what may be said.

## 7. Computus: the guild's hardest problem, briefly

- Easter: first Sunday after the first full moon after the spring equinox. The principle is fixed at Nicaea (325) but the actual conciliar text is lost — the rule as we state it is 4th–6th c. reconstruction (calibration!). Machinery: 19-year golden number; the 532-year Easter cycle (19 × 28); tables standardized by Dionysius Exiguus (525), who also gave us the AD era and no year zero; Bede's De temporum ratione (725) as the standing textbook for centuries.
- The Gregorian fix resets both wheels: solar (÷400 leap rule) and lunar (epact corrections). Orthodox churches keep the Julian paschalion, so Easters usually diverge — a live schism in software form, 1,400 years and counting. The computus is a serious candidate for the longest continuously maintained piece of institutional arithmetic on earth; any fictional theocracy owes its scribes the same almanac discipline.
- System consequence: week + movable feast + fixed feast form three nested registers (Carnival–Lent polarity, Ember days) — couple myth-symbol/ritual-liminality for the inversion logic.

## 8. Festival cycles: the engine, its standard parts, and the era as statute

- New-year placement typology (a culture can run several at once — see the Hebrew case):

| Anchor | Exemplars | Logic |
|---|---|---|
| Spring equinox | Nowruz; 1 Nisan (new year of kings); Roman March, war season reopening | renewal, kingship, campaign season |
| Autumn harvest / full moon | Rosh Hashanah (Tishrei); Mid-Autumn (zhōngqiū / Chuseok, 8th-month 15th day); Thesmophoria (sowing-month Demeter rites) | harvest stored → tithes, debts, assembly |
| Winter solstice / midwinter | Dongzhi; Germanic Yule (Bede's Giuli); Saturnalia 17–23 Dec as the licensed inversion party; Roman January consular year | the turn of the light; oaths, gifts, role-reversal |
| Heliacal | Egyptian Wep Ronpet (Sirius); Māori Matariki; Hawaiian Makahiki (detail in sibling file §5) | the star switches the year |
| Founding act | French An I; nengō changes; corporate fiscal years | time restarts at the political event |

- Four new years at once (Mishnah Rosh Hashanah 1:1): 1 Nisan for kings and festival counts, 1 Elul for cattle tithes, 1 Tishrei for years, sabbatical and jubilee, 15 Shevat for trees — separate legal clocks on separate new years. Superb portable mechanism: a state with distinct tax-year, reign-year, temple-year, orchard-year epochs.
- Sabbatical and jubilee: 7-year fallow, 50-year land return and debt release (Leviticus 25); the Byzantine 15-year indiction did the same for tax. Fiscal calendars are ritual calendars wearing suits.
- Season-of-the-dead slot: Roman Lemuria (May; the paterfamilias ejects ghosts with black beans — Ovid, Fasti 5); Athenian Anthesteria (pots-day, spirits roam, "Out, Keres! The Anthesteria is over"); Zhongyuan / O-Bon (7th-month full moon). Post-reform lag is real: after Japan's 1873 switch, many regions let Bon slide to 15 August while some eastern regions kept 15 July — reforms fracture festivals by region and sect; bake the fracture into your worlds.
- Assembly and games cycles: Olympiad dating (from 776 BCE; adopted as an era by historians, e.g., Timaeus); Aztec 18 veintenas of 20 days + 5 nemontemi, "nameless days" — ill-omened, births unlucky then (Sahagún Bk II); Athenian friction between festival months and the council's prytany calendar (Ath. Pol. 43).
- Era and epoch: the count as statute. Seleucid era (312/11 BCE); ab urbe condita (Varro's 753); Olympiads; Byzantine Anno Mundi 5509 + indiction — with the count nearing 7000 AM in 1492 CE, documented end-time paschal anxieties in Muscovy; AD (Dionysius 525, popularized by Bede); Hijri 622; Minguo 1912; Fascist Era 1922; Japan's kōki epoch (660 BCE) used for the 2600th-anniversary mobilization of 1940. The epoch memorializes an act; changing it re-founds the world — no observation required.
- Kotodama hooks: Japanese month names — Kannazuki "godless month" (the kami travel to Izumo, which keeps Kamiarizuki "month-with-gods"): the calendar itself narrates theology; Minazuki 無 ambiguity ("waterless" vs na as possessive → "month of water") — contested etymology, don't resolve it; nengō (from Han Wudi's Jianyuan, 140 BCE; Reiwa 2019) as state speech-acts — to name the year is to define it; the French de-Christianized month names and the Soviet anti-calendar are the same move in another idiom.

## 10. Calibration shelf (contested or discredited — flag before anything reaches the bible)

- Calendar riots of 1752 — myth (Poole 1995); grievance satire (Hogarth) real.
- Nicaea's Easter canon — the exact conciliar ruling is lost; the stated rule is later reconstruction; write "the principle", not "the decree".
- Hillel II "created the fixed calendar in 359 CE" — traditional attribution; Stern (2001) argues the fixed calendar crystallized gradually, with thin evidence before the geonic period.
- "Meton discovered the 19-year cycle" — Babylonian regularized intercalation predates him (Parker & Dubberstein 1956); the Greek name is a priority claim.
- Eostre — Bede (De temporum ratione 15) is the sole ancient witness for the goddess behind the English Easter name; Grimm (1835) built "Ostara" on it; historicity unresolved. Report as "Bede's attestation, unverifiable further".
- Sothic cycle 1460 vs 1461 years — depends on Julian vs civil year counting, and Sothic-based chronology depends on observation latitude; do not stack fiction on the fine digits.
- Khayyam and the Jalali calendar — a committee attribution; "Omar invented the Persian calendar" overstates a probable role.
- Khmer Rouge "Year Zero" (1975) — widely reported framing; a formal decree is thin in the record; use as rhetoric-attributed.
- Gregorian accuracy figures vary with the reference year (≈1 d/3,300 yr vs mean tropical; longer vs the equinox year) — quote with the yardstick named.
- "The week comes from a Babylonian seven-day creation" — etiological legend; the attested chain is lunar-quarter omens → Jewish rest day → planetary week → church calendar (Zerubavel 1985).
- West African market-week structure (Akan adaduanan; Rattray 1923): "forty days" is the colloquial name — structurally a 42-day cycle of six prefixes × seven day-names, days classed dapaa/dabɔne/dahunu (Konadu 2012, who contests the Mande/Islamic-conjunction origin thesis). The cycle is standard; name and structure differ.

## 11. Application protocol — building a fictional culture's calendar

1. Fix the three clocks and choose the lie (§1): which cycle is sacred, which administrative, which ignored. Ignoring astronomy entirely (Balinese move) is a real design, not a simplification.
2. Choose the intercalation strategy (§2) and name the office that owns the leap; give it a past abuse scandal (the Macrobius move).
3. Name months and days from contact history (§6): calques from the dominant neighbor, numbered relics, one church-replacement; a weekday luck lexicon is optional but high-yield (rokuyō move).
4. Place the new year(s) (§8): one sacred, one fiscal; consider a four-new-years system for legal texture.
5. Lay the festival arc: one inversion festival, one season-of-the-dead, one harvest assembly, one founding-act civic cycle; dress them (couple costume) and give each a named day inside the month.
6. Decide era mechanics (§8, "era as statute"): regnal, founding-act, or nengō-style renaming on crisis; write one double-dated document into the lore corpus.
7. Stage at most one reform event per era: lost days, a year of confusion, quiet grumbling, tax shifts — the riot is the cliché (§10); the revenue adjustment is the realism.
8. Write the drift: lunar ⇒ holy days wander; Egyptian ⇒ festivals migrate through the seasons over centuries; reformed ⇒ regional lags (the O-Bon move). The mismatches are plot.
9. Run the computus check: can the priests in-world compute next year's movable feast? If not, who can — and what do they charge?

## 12. Canon boundary
