## 1. The calibration rule — port the grammar, not the garment names

- A costume system is a **grammar with three slots**: *silhouette* (which lines carry status: shoulder, waist, hip, hem, head), *material* (fiber, weave, dye, fastening tech — what the economy can actually make), *wearer rule* (who may show which line, in which material, on which occasion). A garment name is just one solution of those constraints; **never port names, port constraints.** "Kimono" and "toga" cannot travel; "a flat, sleeve-surfaced layered silhouette with a lining calendar and rank legibility" can be re-derived for any culture.
- **Each era-cluster below is a worked example of the three slots**, stated so a scene can be staged from it. The misread-garment hook in each table is the in-world test: a culture has a dress system only when a character can be *wrong* in it (envoy in mourning colors, merchant's silk lining discovered, chalked toga recognized as electioneering).
- **Silhouettes are body technologies**: wrapped vs. cut vs. layered answers three different questions — how the body is displayed (draped cultures choreograph movement; tailored cultures choreograph posture; layered cultures choreograph *opacity*, what is revealed and when). Choose per culture and keep it consistent with its furniture (chairs vs. floors vs. cushions), since seating and dress evolve together.

## 2. Named frameworks — what each is good for, what to distrust

| Framework | Claim | Craft use | Trap |
|---|---|---|---|
| Laver's law (1937) | A style passes: indecent → shameless → outré → smart → dowdy → hideous → amusing → romantic → beautiful as it ages | Dating fictional fashion cycles; old/new conflict between generations | A witty heuristic, not a law; phases are Euro-century-specific |
| Flügel's "Great Male Renunciation" (1930) | Men abandoned display to women after c. 1800 | Sharp label for the 19th-c. dark-suit regime | Contested as a thesis: Hollander (1994) argues male display *migrated into cut and tailoring*, not out of dress — do not build a culture on the "men stopped dressing" premise |
| Hollander (1978, 1994) | Clothes are read as pictures of the body; pictorial fashion drives real dress | Designing "why the silhouette changed" arcs | Her canon is Western pictorial art; extend to non-pictorial cultures only by analogy |
| Roche (1994) | Ancien-régime dress is a ledger: rank, credit, wardrobe economics | Costing a court wardrobe; gift-dress diplomacy | Statistics are French; don't universalize wardrobe sizes |
| Killerby (2002) | Sumptuary laws encode ideals and anxieties; enforcement selective | Writing laws characters can actually circumvent | Treating statutes as descriptions of what people wore — they describe what states *feared* |
| Steele (2001) on the corset | Undermines the "organ-crushing torture" mythos; corsetry varied wildly by era and class | Calibrated corsetry (§12) | The pop horror-image as history |
| Older survey reconstructions (Kohler 1928 and line) | Eras rebuilt as plausible drawings | Silhouette reference at a glance | Reconstructions are period illustrations of *their* century's taste, not archaeology; anchor specifics in finds (Herjolfsnes, Shōsōin) before citing a reconstruction |

## 3. The ancient axis — draped robe vs. tailored trouser (Mesopotamia, Egypt, Greece, Rome)

| Feature | System |
|---|---|
| silhouette | Wrapped rectangles and pinned tubes: shoulder-line and drape carry status; no waist engineering; heads crowned (nemes, tiara, wreath) |
| key garments | Mesopotamian fringed wrap-shawls and tufted *kaunakes* skirts (a Greek word describing the fleece look); Egypt: *shendyt* kilt, sheath dress (*kalasiris* is Herodotus' word, 2.81, for both sexes), *wesekh* broad collar, Old Kingdom beadnet dress from Giza (MFA Boston); Greece: pinned/short-sleeved wool *peplos* vs. longer linen *chiton*, *himation* mantle; Rome: tunica, toga, matron's stola and palla |
| materials | Egypt: near-monoculture of pleated linen (Vogelsang-Eastwood 1993); Greece/Rome: wool and linen, silk only as ultra-import; Mesopotamia: wool predominant |
| wearer rule | Rome's *habitus* ladder is the model system: toga virilis (coming of age), praetexta (magistrates and freeborn children), pulla (mourning), candida (chalk-whitened election-seeker), picta (triumphator) (Sebesta & Bonfante 1994); matrons wear stola — satirists assign the *toga* to the adulteress (Juvenal 2); Egyptian priests: linen only, no wool inside temples (Hdt. 2.37, 2.81) — dress as cult purity, not rank display |
| misread hook | A chalked toga is visible electioneering; an unveiled citizen woman reads as a different class (Llewellyn-Jones 2003 argues Greek veiling was normative for citizen women — contested as universal); Persian dress on a Greek reads as *Medism*, political treason-suspicion (Miller 1997) |

- **The trouser axis**: trousers = horse-riding frontier, robe = settled civility. Rome mocks Gallic *braccae* (→ English "breeches") while soldiers quietly wear them; China runs the same axis earlier and in reverse — King Wuling of Zhao's 307 BCE *hufu* (胡服, "Hu dress") cavalry reform trades robes for trousers and mounts (*Shiji* 43). One reform logic, two opposite directions: a fictional frontier culture can inherit either reading.
- Median/Persian riding dress — *anaxyrides* (trousers), *kandys* (coat with false sleeves), tiara (Hdt. 7.62; Xen. *Cyr.* 8.3) — is the wardrobe of the barbarian-luxury Other for Greeks, and treasure for Macedonians.
- **Silk as chronometer**: male silk legislated against in Rome (Suet. *Tib.* 34); silkworm eggs smuggled to Byzantium c. 552 ends the Chinese-Persian monopoly (Procopius, *Wars* 8.17) — a datable material-tech break (§ era files).

## 4. Han-Chinese robe systems — the cosmogram robe, and the Qing rupture

| Feature | System |
|---|---|
| silhouette | Vertical column, wide sleeves, no waist; status worn at the *hem lengths, sleeve widths, cap, and badge*, not at the body line |
| key garments | *shenyi* 深衣, the one-piece "deep robe" of the *Liji* "Shenyi" chapter: twelve panels for twelve months, sleeves round as a compass, collar square as a carpenter's square, back seam plumb-straight, hem level as scales — the robe is a cosmogram; *ruqun* 襦裙, separate short jacket + skirt (Tang chest-high *qixiong* variant); Tang round-collar *yuanlingpao* absorbed from steppe/Sogdian *hufu* fashion; Ming black gauze *wushamao* cap + *buzi* 补子 rank badges (Hongwu-era 1391: birds for civil ranks, beasts for military — Garrett 1994) |
| materials | Silk as rank fabric with hemp/cotton beneath; dragon-robe brocade codes read from cloud-collar to hem (Cammann 1952); Qing compendium *Huangchao liqi tushi* (1759) fixes every court garment by rank |
| wearer rule | Correct colors vs. intermediate colors in layering (zhengse/jianse — see color-symbolism.md §3); badge = rank readable at courtly distance; Qing break: 1645 hair-and-dress edicts (*tifa yifu*) force the queue and Manchu robe on Han men, horse-hoof cuffs on official robes, while Han women largely keep Ming-style ruqun — the folk formula "men obey, women don't; the living obey, the dead don't" ((to-verify) for the "ten obey, ten not obey" wording and attribution) |
| misread hook | 乌纱帽 wushamao "black gauze cap" = the office itself ("lost his wushamao" = dismissed); 旗袍 qipao "banner robe" names the Manchu bannermen's dress that 1920s Shanghai re-tailored into the modern qipao (Finnane 2008); 衣冠 yiguan "cap and robe" = civilization/decorum, with the idiom yiguan qinshou "beast in cap-and-robe" popularly linked to the bird/beast badges ((to-verify) — the badge etymology is traditional, not proven) |

- **Craft use**: the shenyi's *numbered-panel* logic is a portable generator — a fictional bureaucracy can encode its calendar, its directions, or its ministries directly into panel counts and collar geometry, then suffer schism when a rival school cuts the collar differently.

## 5. Medieval Europe — dress as layer grammar (c. 1100–1500)

| Feature | System |
|---|---|
| silhouette | Slot grammar, outer to inner: mantle/cloak → surcot/surcoat → cotte/kirtle → chemise; braies (linen drawers) and chausses (footed hose) as the leg layer; heads hosed, hooded (chaperon), or crowned |
| key garments | Chemise, cotte, surcoat (heraldic), sideless surcoat over plastron (moralists' "gates of hell" sobriquet ((to-verify) for the primary sermon locus)), houppelande; the 1340s **tailoring revolution** — fitted cottes, lacing, buttons — the fit-moment fashion historians date by (Newton 1980); everyday proof-text: the fitted, buttoned 14th-c. garments from the Herjolfsnes Greenland burials (Nörlund 1924) |
| materials | Wool broadcloth as the economic base; linen next to skin; fur as rank (ermine for royalty — its "dies rather than sully its whiteness" bestiary legend is the animal-symbolism file's); cloth of gold = metal-thread weft, the top of the ladder; 1363 Statute of Apparel assigns fabrics and furs by income tier (37 Edw. III) |
| wearer rule | Rank shows as *layer count and trim*: clergy in habit by order; nobility in furs and buttons (early buttons are jewelry-priced); townsmen by the statute's tiers; the heraldic surcoat displaying arms is literally a "coat-armour" → "coat of arms" (Woodcock & Robinson 1988) |
| misread hook | A worker in buttons and scarlet is a statute offense and a scene; a hood worn as a chaperon becomes the *word* "chaperone" (French "hood" → the escorting metaphor); livery colors worn by the wrong household = impersonation |

- Killerby (2002) caution applies Europe-wide: apparel statutes encode what states feared, not what streets held — write enforcement as selective, bribable, and generational.

## 6. Heian Japan — layering as language (jūnihitoe)

| Feature | System |
|---|---|
| silhouette | Flat columnar mass; the *sleeves and collars are display surfaces* — the body disappears, the ensemble speaks |
| key garments | Layered *uchiki* robes (informal: 5–20+, count varies by rank, season, purse); full formal = *itsutsuginu-karakoromo* (five jackets + Tang-style karaginu + mo train) — **"jūnihitoe" ("twelve layers") is a later sobriquet, not a Heian term** (Dalby 1993); men: sokutai full court dress (kanmuri cap, sekitai stone belt, shaku baton), kariginu hunting wear as relaxed style |
| materials | Silk weaves keyed to season: lined *awase* winter, unlined *hitoe* spring/autumn, gauze *usumono* summer — a **lining calendar** binding wardrobe to almanac |
| wearer rule | *Kasane no irome* 襲の色目: named color-layer combinations (plants, seasons — wisteria, maple) whose outer/inner hues are read like poetry; Sei Shōnagon's *Pillow Book* shows courtiers composing and judging them; rank + season + occasion all legible at a glance; white face-powder and blackened teeth (ohaguro) as court-uniform beauty |
| misread hook | A wrong-layer combination is a social sentence: too drab = gaucherie, wrong season = incompetence in the almanac, borrowed layers exposed = poverty comedy; the proverb sode furiau mo tashō no en — "even brushing sleeves in passing is a bond from a past life" — makes the sleeve a person-proxy |

- **Craft use**: this is the strongest model for a *dress-as-semiotics* culture — layer names form a vocabulary, combinations form sentences, and a character's wardrobe is literally their writing. Coupled with linguistics/politeness-taboo-euphemism.md for the register system.

## 7. Sengoku → Edo — camp wear becomes court wear, kosode becomes the garment

| Feature | System |
|---|---|
| silhouette | From layered court amplitude to belted practicality: kosode closed by sash (obi) creates a waist the old robes never had; sleeve shape becomes the fashion variable |
| key garments | The **kosode** ("small sleeve(s)", named for the sleeve *opening*) rises from under-layer to THE garment for all classes; the samurai camp pair kataginu + naga-bakama crystallizes as the **kamishimo**, Edo formal dress (Dalby 1993); haori jacket climbs from battlefield wear to male fashion |
| materials | Nishijin (Kyoto) silk monopoly vs. cotton/hemp commoners; Edo sumptuary orders (*shōrui*) restrict townspeople's silk and sleeve length — documented subterfuge: rich silk spent on the **lining (ura)** under plain surfaces (Dalby 1993) |
| wearer rule | Sleeve taxonomy = life-stage grammar: **furisode** (long swinging sleeves) = unmarried women; at marriage, sleeves are cut to **tomesode**, pattern migrating below the obi; samurai classes in kamishimo, actors (esp. onnagata) and courtesans as trendsetters; Genroku (1688–1704) is the flamboyant peak, with ukiyo-e actor prints functioning as fashion plates (Slade 2009) |
| misread hook | A chōnin's plain coat with a spendy lining is quiet defiance; furisode on a married woman signals either mourning-for-youth or scandal; a merchant's wife out-dressing a samurai's household is a shōrui case |

- **The word froze the fashion**: "kimono" (着物) just means "thing worn" — a generic word for clothing that became the *ethnic-garment* word only in Meiji, set against *yōfuku* (Western dress); the "timeless kimono" trope is false — kosode sleeve shapes changed drastically per decade (Milhaupt 2014).

## 8. Early modern Europe — the rigid body, starched (c. 1500–1789)

| Feature | System |
|---|---|
| silhouette | **Engineered**: farthingale hoops (Spanish cone; French wheel), padded doublets, ruffs — the body is a frame the clothes mount; 17th c. relaxes into justacorps + cravat; stays ("pair of bodies" in Elizabethan English — Arnold 1988) shape the torso for both sexes' fashion |
| key garments | Doublet, hose, ruff (starched), slashed and pinked surfaces (the "Swiss soldiers slashed plundered silk" origin is **legend** (§12)); Elizabethan wardrobe is documented to the yard — Arnold (1988) mines Elizabeth I's wardrobe accounts; codpiece extinct by the 1590s (Ashelford 1988); robe à la française sack-back gown for elite women |
| materials | Black wool/silk as Habsburg-Spanish prestige fabric (dye-expensive black = power, Pastoureau 2009 — see color-symbolism.md §8); starch, wire, whalebone, paste and metal thread; English Burial in Wool Acts (1666; affidavits from 1678) legislate fiber on the dead for the wool trade |
| wearer rule | Elizabethan apparel proclamations (1574 onward) tier silks, velvets, and gold lace by rank; Versailles turns dress into etiquette tech — at the king's lever, courtiers compete for the honor of handing the shirt (Roche 1994); court gift-dress and wardrobe ledgers are credit instruments |
| misread hook | Black on the wrong court = Spanish sympathies; a ruff too large for the wearer's station is a proclamation case; the man handing the shirt is *closer to the king* than the man holding the sword |

- **Word receipt**: cravat (1660s court French) < *cravate* < "Croat" — the neckwear of Croatian cavalrymen adopted as the modern necktie ancestor; fabric names carry trade routes: calico ← Calicut, muslin ← Mosul, damask ← Damascus (gauze ← Gaza is the traditional but disputed etymology ((to-verify))).

## 9. Worn words — the language↔costume interface (this lane's core coupling)

- **Garment → institution loans**: *candidatus* (chalked toga) → "candidate"; "coat of arms" ← heraldic coat-armour; chaperon (hood) → "chaperone"; cravat ← Croat; braccae → "breeches"; gymnasion ← *gymnos* (naked). When a costume practice becomes an institution, its garment-word travels with it — mine every fictional institution for its buried garment.
- **Fabric names as trade receipts** (§8): the lexicon of cloth encodes who sold it where — a fictional silk road leaves the same fossil words (linguistics/language-contact.md owns the contact mechanism).
- **The body of dress as person-proxy in idiom**: Chinese 连襟 liánjīn "connected lapels" = brothers-in-law; 结发 jiéfà "hair tied (at first marriage)" = first-spouse (结发夫妻); 拂袖 fúxiù "brush the sleeve and leave" = storm out; 衣冠 yīguān "cap and robe" = decorum itself; Japanese sleeve idioms (§6). Idioms like these are the cheapest proof that a dress system is *lived* — invent them for any fictional culture and stage the gesture they fossilize.
- **The hem speaks** (portable scene device): cutting or grasping the garment hem substitutes for the person or the bond — David spares Saul's cut robe-corner (1 Sam 24:4–6); Samuel's torn robe = the kingdom torn from Saul (1 Sam 15:27–28); Ruth asks Boaz to "spread your kanaph (wing/garment-corner) over me" (Ruth 3:9 — the same word as the tassel-bearing corners of Num 15:38, see color-symbolism.md's citation of that verse); Mesopotamian practice treats garment fringe as identity-token in ritual and oath — conventional Akkadian lemma *sissiktu* ((to-verify) exact lexical form). A fictional culture should know what *touching the hem* means.

## 10. Who-wears-what — the rule generator

- Every era above enforces its wearer rules by a small set of mechanisms; mix at least two per fictional culture, or nothing binds:

| Mechanism | Real exemplar | Fiction lever |
|---|---|---|
| statute (sumptuary law) | 1363 Statute of Apparel; Elizabethan proclamations 1574+; Edo shōrui; Italian city laws (Killerby 2002) | selective enforcement, informers, the merchant's loophole |
| etiquette/ceremony | Versailles lever; Heian lining calendar and kasane names | the wrong-layer envoy; dressing-race politics |
| cult/liturgy | Egyptian linen-only priesthood (Hdt. 2.81) | purity breach visible to all at once |
| rank badge/institution | Ming buzi birds/beasts; Roman habitus ladder; Qing dragon counts (Cammann 1952) | counterfeit badges, dead-rank heirlooms |
| life-stage rule | toga virilis; furisode→tomesode | the marriage that changes a wardrobe overnight |
| gender boundary | stola vs. toga; trouser axis; Manchu-vs-Han women's dress after 1645 | boundary crossing as scandal or disguise |
| market/monopoly | Nishijin silk district; wool burial acts | smuggling, substitution, fiber wars |

- **Write the ledger per garment row**: garment | silhouette slot | material | who may wear | occasion | enforcement | era-of-change. An empty era-of-change column means the system is dead in-story or you haven't found its plot.

## 11. Application protocol — staging a fictional culture's dress

1. **Pick the body-technology** (§1): wrapped, cut, or layered — then commit seating, posture, and architecture to match.
2. **Derive the material stratum from biome and trade** (flax river-valley? wool uplands? silk monopoly? cotton import?): no dye or fiber without a supply chain (color-symbolism.md §10 owns chromophore strata; era files own the tech breaks — §3's silk chronometer is the model).
3. **Define the silhouette slots**: which lines carry status, and what the top of the system spends (Habsburg black; dragon-count brocade; twelve-layer mass) — scarcity at the top, not beauty, is the signature.
4. **Write the wearer-rule table** (§10) with two enforcement mechanisms and one deliberate anomaly (a gender-flip like Qing women's Ming dress; a cult like linen-only priests).
5. **Give it a lining calendar or equivalent time-lock** (season, rite, mourning) so wardrobe choices read as literacy, not taste.
6. **Invent the idioms** (§9): at least one person-proxy body-part idiom, one institution loan from a garment, one hem-or-equivalent touching rite.
7. **Stage the misread-garment scene** (per-era hooks above): if no scene can misfire — no counterfeit, no wrong season, no statute case — the system is decoration.
8. **Plan era drift**: name which reform (Zhao's hufu, the 1340s tailoring revolution, the 1645 edicts, the 1920s qipao re-cut) is your culture's "the camp wear became the court wear" moment, and keep the old garment as ritual fossil (Heian dress under the samurai; white shinishōzoku under Meiji black — cross-ref color-symbolism.md §1).

- **Failure modes**: one national costume outside time (the "kimono" trap, §7); garment names ported without constraints; sumptuary laws obeyed by everyone; no misreads; a feudal court with a tailor's revolution but no buttons (fastening tech gates fit); dress vocabulary with no idioms behind it.

## 12. Debunk shelf — keep these out of fiction

- **Chastity belts**: largely a 19th-century museum fabrication — Classen (2007) traces the myth-making; no reliable medieval examples exist. Cross-ref lore-shokunin before any canon use.
- **Horned Viking helmets**: 19th-century opera costuming (Doepler's 1876 *Ring* designs), not archaeology.
- **The torture corset**: rib-removal and organ-crushing horror imagery is largely later exaggeration; corsetry's shape, rigidity, and health effects varied enormously by era and class (Steele 2001).
- **"Sumptuary law shows what people wore"**: laws encode anxiety and ideals; enforcement was selective and often failing (Killerby 2002) — see §5.
- **"Great Male Renunciation" as fact**: a coinage (Flügel 1930), contested — display moved into cut, not out of dress (Hollander 1994).
- **Jūnihitoe = always twelve layers**: layer counts varied by rank, season, and means; the name is retrospective (§6).
- **"The kimono is timeless"**: Meiji word-freezing over a fast-changing fashion (§7, Milhaupt 2014).
- **Bridal white as ancient**: Europe's white-wedding norm dates to Victoria's 1840 wedding and 19th-c. dress culture; Rome's bride wore flame-orange — cross-ref color-symbolism.md §1, which owns this calibration.
- **Doric-vs-Ionic Greek dress as ancient fact**: the schema is largely a modern (19th-c.) reconstruction from vase-painting interpretation; treat Hdt. 5.88's dress-change story as an emic anecdote, not a typology key.
- **Swiss-slash origin of slashing** (§8): a good story, no solid documentation — treat as in-world folklore, which is a better use for it anyway.
- **Laver's law**: heuristic, not mechanism (§2).
