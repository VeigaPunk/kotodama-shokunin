## 1. What the mask is said to be

Decide this first; every later rule follows from it. The opened sources give four answers, and one culture may hold more than one.

- **Concealment or transformation.** The *World Archaeology* special-issue editors separate ontologies in which a mask conceals or disguises a body from ontologies in which it transforms one, and they treat these as different world rules (S239, "Transformations", ¶1). Where the mask transforms, the audience need not be deceived: the editors describe a rite in which the relationship between mask and wearer is openly performed (S239, "Transformations", ¶1, ¶8).
- **What it does, not what it depicts.** The same volume asks what masks do rather than what they depict, and its editors conclude that a search for universal definitions of masks is futile (S239, "Power and representation", ¶1; "Conclusion", ¶1). Give each culture its own answer and let the answers conflict.
- **The whole apparition.** In van Beek's survey of African masking, the mask is the entire costumed appearance — headpiece, costume, ornament and what is carried — together with the unseen dancer, the dance and the music; swapping accessories can make it a different mask (S240, "The African Masking Configuration", ¶2). Budget the ensemble, not the face.
- **A portrait.** The Roman ancestor mask was judged as a likeness of features and complexion (S241, 6.53.5), and Pliny's wax faces stood in niches ready for the family's funeral processions (S242, 35.6). A culture that defines its mask as a likeness must also define who is good enough at likeness to make one.
- **A type.** The Nanfeng Nuo masks are carved types whose features let a viewer read role, sex, age and temperament (S243, p. 856); the commedia masks bundle a city of origin, a class and a stage speech (S244, p. 7, Table 1).
- **A condition.** The Sri Lankan sanni masks portray diseases: the review describes a vomiting demon with a green face and protruding tongue, and a pale face read as anaemia (S245, body, the paragraph on the figures).
- **A being with two states.** A Kwakwaka'wakw transformation mask opens by strings at the right moment to reveal an inner face, typically a different character (S246, "History of use", ¶1); the holding museum reads such masks as expressing the dual nature of the beings they represent (S246, "Iconographic meaning").

## 3. The masking complex

A mask in use is five things a designer can set separately: the object, the ensemble it belongs to, the wearer, the presence it makes, and the keeper who holds it between appearances.

- **Keeper and storage.** In Nanfeng the masks are worshipped on the altar during the Spring Festival, and the holy box and props are kept in an attic above that altar (S247, p. 477); the village's hereditary Wu-family headman runs the troupe (S247, p. 476). In Guichi (Anhui) a designated person arranges the sacred masks in a prescribed manner, and after the performance they are counted and put back into the trunk (S248, Guichi section, ¶2–¶3).
- **Consecration.** The Nanfeng authors report masks endowed with power through consecration ceremonies (S243, p. 859); the mask before and after that rite are different objects in play.
- **Presence.** In Pingxiang performers are reported to take on divine agency temporarily when they put the mask on, and practitioners treat the figures as participants acting on cosmic order (S249, p. 101). The Oslo museum records that the Garā Yakā's presence is said to bring success, purification and prosperity to those present (S250, Kōlam Theatre section).
- **Weight and training.** The Rubin Museum states that cham masks are heavy and the dances exact, so dancers must be strong and well trained (S251, "Watch a Cham Dance", ¶2); a sanni mask in the Science Museum Group is face-sized, 210 × 155 × 100 mm and 0.28 kg (S252, Measurements).
- **Made from a form.** The V&A records that its Pulcinella half-mask would have been moulded over a wooden form, and another mask from the same mould is recorded (S253, Materials and techniques; Object history): a type mask can be reproduced from its mould.

## 4. Access rules: who may make, keep, wear, see, know and unmask

Write each right as a separate cell; the sources show them held by different people.

- **The right is the thing inherited.** The U'mista Cultural Society lists among what a potlatch passes on "the right to wear, use, and display certain regalia" (S254, Potlatch page, purposes); the potlatch is a time for showing the masks and dances owned by the host, and guests are witnesses paid with gifts for acknowledging the inheritance (S254, Potlatch page, ¶3; Contemporary Potlatch, ¶1). After repatriation each family decided where its regalia would be held (S254, Our Masks Come Home page, ¶3).
- **Office, not wealth.** Smith's dictionary states that the Roman *ius imaginum* belonged to holders of the curule offices and could be withheld after death, citing the masks of Brutus and Cassius (S255, IMAGO, "Jus imaginum"). Admission to a family's set was a family decision: Pliny reports Messala forbidding the Laevini's images among his own, and the Salvitti gaming the set by adoption (S242, 35.6).
- **Who may know.** In van Beek's configuration the secret is that the wearers are costumed men — a secret he calls as public as it is obvious — and the uninitiated have no right to that knowledge (S240, "The African Masking Configuration", ¶4). Access is graded: postmenopausal women and "sisters of the mask" stand partly inside (S240, "The Masking Option", ¶4).
- **Who may wear.** The Drametse inscription names sixteen masked male dancers at a festival organized by one monastery (S256, Element description, ¶1–¶2); the Rubin Museum states that monks and nuns dance cham in the monastery courtyard (S251, "Watch a Cham Dance", ¶2). In Pingxiang whole lineages maintained the temples, paid for the festivals and trained the performers (S249, p. 100). A Sri Lankan carver recalled that only Oli-caste people were once allowed to perform in the Garayaka mask (S257, p. 64) — one informant's memory, not a documented rule.
- **Who makes.** In the Sri Lankan kolam, Karava households carved and Berava dancers supplied verse and drumming, and the paper's interviews find one Berava artist doing both (S257, Abstract p. 56; p. 64). At Mohács, self-organized busó groups pass on mask carving to younger generations (S258, description, sentence 6).
- **Who may unmask.** The Oslo museum states that of all Sinhalese masked dances only two end with the dancer removing the mask in front of the patient (S250, Sanniyäkuma section). Make unmasking a scripted event, not a stage habit.
- **Closed rooms.** The Pingxiang fieldworker reports that some sacred rituals were not fully accessible to him (S249, p. 99): a closed portion is possible, and a world document need not open it.
- **Sight as a purchased grade.** Among the Lak of southern New Ireland the ethnographer's lowest initiation bought the right to see the tubuan where the seniors build it, and women are excluded (S259, opening section); Lak clan masks may not be alienated, while masks bought from outside owners become personal property that can be sold (S259, "General Features of the Tubuan System"). The Lak say the tubuan is a spirit while knowing they make and wear it (S259, "Conclusion").
- **A society of women.** The Agnes Etherington record states that the sowei helmet mask was commissioned by the Sande society and is worn exclusively by women (S260, catalogue text, ¶1); Curnow notes that the captions credit the masks to a male artist while the performer is a woman (S261, fig. 324 caption). Phillips found no separate Mende word for "mask" — the closest phrase is "the head of sowei" — and was shown bare headpieces, sometimes in an enclosed area that men could not enter, while villages keeping stricter protocol brought out the fully costumed figure (S262, ¶2).
- **Who controls the ending.** Thomas Moulero told Drewal that the Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ mothers can prevent the mask from being removed after the festival, and that a mask was brought out for him to photograph only as an exception (S263, interview transcript, PDF pp. 5 and 8). UNESCO's record calls Gẹ̀lẹ̀dẹ́ "the only known masked society, which is also governed by women" (S264, Description, ¶2) — UNESCO's own superlative, not a finding to repeat.
- **Restricted traditions.** Where a community has published a rule, the rule is the whole of what a world document may take: the Grand Council of the Haudenosaunee's policy states that "The public exhibition of all medicine masks is forbidden" and that no institution has authority over them (S265, "Exhibition of Medicine Masks"; "Authority over Medicine Masks"), and the Hopi Cultural Preservation Office states that sacred rituals have been exposed out of context and without Hopi permission (S266, "Intellectual Property Rights", ¶4). This entry gives no image, construction detail or ceremonial sequence for either (see `../civilizations/haudenosaunee.md:139`).

## 5. The masked voice

A mask can be a voice before it is a face. Decide what the masked figure sounds like, who may hear it, and who translates it.

- **The device.** Driver calls mirlitons the most widespread devices for disguising the human voice for spiritual purposes in sub-Saharan Africa (S267, §The buzz aesthetic in Africa). Potter's Pitt Rivers Boki disguiser is a small bone tube with a spider-silk membrane, and her formulation is that "the human speaks and a spirit is heard" (S268, §The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?).
- **Heard, not seen.** Blench reports that Tarok orìm "are mostly heard, but emerge as masked figures under some circumstances" (S269, Abstract); the Kòmò headpieces Driver describes are forbidden to non-initiates, so for most listeners the spirit is only sound (S267, §Sources of power).
- **The register.** The orìm speak through voice disguisers in a language dotted with code words inside normal Tarok syntax, and their utterances are interpreted by unmasked figures (S269, Abstract). Blench separates substitution languages from replacement languages and surrogate tone-speech (S269, §1): a spirit register can be one device, not a whole invented tongue.
- **Audibility as rank.** In the Boki account the soundscape was gendered concentrically, with women kept to the outskirts during a spirit visitation (S268, §The voice-disguiser – an auditory object?).
- **The mask that does not speak.** At a Roman funeral the speech belonged to the orator, who recounted the ancestors' exploits while their images were present (S241, 6.54.1). The mask can authorize another person's voice.
- **A stage dialect.** Crick argues that the speech of each commedia mask was "not the pure dialect of the region, but a performatively comic version" (S244, p. 9), and that the accent stays while the type's valuation flips (S244, p. 11). The Sri Lankan kolam's name glosses as mockery and its stock parts include a police constable and a Mudaliyar (S257, p. 57; Notes p. 65): the form licenses lampooning officials.

## 6. Occasion and calendar

- **A season with fixed ends.** Binche confines its carnival to the three days before Lent (S270, description, sentence 2); the Kurenti rounds run from Candlemas to Ash Wednesday (S271, description, sentence 1); the Nanfeng cycle runs from the lunar new year to the nineteenth or twentieth day of the first month (S247, p. 476).
- **Where masking sits in the life course.** In van Beek's survey masking clusters at initiation and death and never appears at marriage or birth (S240, "The African Masking Configuration", ¶3).
- **An ordered programme.** Pingxiang Nuo runs invitation, exorcism, performance and dismissal (S249, p. 101); at Drametse a prayer dance inside the shrine precedes the dancers' appearance one by one in the courtyard, and one dance carries a calm part for peaceful deities and a rapid part for wrathful ones (S256, Element description, ¶2).
- **Theatre and cure kept apart.** Bailey and de Silva separate kolam (theatre) from tovil (exorcism), both of which use masks (S245, Abstract, ¶1); only the demons thought to cause a given patient's illness are staged (S245, Abstract, ¶1).
- **Entry routes and houses.** At Mohács more than 500 busós arrive in rowboats on the Danube (S258, description, sentence 2); Kurenti groups go from house to house, and the bells and sticks are believed to chase evil away (S271, description, sentences 3–4).
- **What the audience is owed.** The Rubin Museum states that cham is not meant as entertainment though it is a spectacle (S251, History); at Drametse people come to obtain blessings (S256, Element description, ¶3).
- **The price of the role.** Binche is known for its participants' financial commitment, and its Gilles appear after a ceremonial dressing in a costume that includes a wax mask (S270, description, sentences 12 and 8).

## 7. Ancestor and funerary masks

- **Kept in the house.** Polybius puts the image of the dead man in the most conspicuous place in the house, in a wooden shrine (S241, 6.53.4); the Greek calls it a little wooden temple (S272, 6.53.4). Smith describes the shrines in the alae with a titulus naming the dead man's deeds (S255, IMAGO).
- **Worn by look-alikes.** At a funeral the masks were worn by men chosen for resembling the dead in stature and carriage (S241, 6.53.6; S272, 6.53.6): the illusion is a whole-body casting problem.
- **The pedigree on the wall.** Pliny reports lines of descent drawn between the portraits (S242, 35.6).
- **Opened on occasion.** At public sacrifices the family opened and decorated the shrines (S272, 6.53.6); Smith adds laurel on festal days (S255, IMAGO).
- **Manufacture is a reconstruction.** Smith's account of a mould taken from the face and a fresh cast for the atrium is his synthesis, hedged as probable (S255, IMAGO) (to-verify against a modern study).
- **Likeness or presence.** The National Archaeological Museum's record keeps "mask of Agamemnon" as a conventional name for the gold mask from Grave Circle A, Tomb V, dated 1600–1501 BC, while reading in it an intention to depict the dead man's individual features (S273, Title; Description). The British Museum's cartonnage mummy-mask gives wide-open eyes that convey "vitality and alertness" and carries a funerary text (S274, Description): a face for the dead can be a likeness or a living presence, and the record says which it reads.

## 8. Reveal, removal and retirement

- **The reveal inside the object.** The Kwakwaka'wakw mask splits down the centre and opens to expose another face (S246, "Physical description"); it belongs to the opening of the winter ceremonial, when the deceased carrier of an ancestral name returns to pass on his rights (S246, "History of use", ¶1).
- **The chief mask holds the set.** The Maha Kola mask usually carries miniature figures of the other eighteen demons, and its appearance closes the rite (S245, Abstract, ¶1).
- **Display as argument.** The U'mista Cultural Centre arranged its returned objects in potlatch order and not in cases (S254, Our Masks Come Home page, film transcript ¶3).
- **Exit into collections.** The Oslo museum states that most early Sinhalese ritual masks in European collections came through Carl Hagenbeck's commercial ventures, whose shows it names "Ethnic Shows" and "Human Zoo" (S250, Hagenbeck section); the Science Museum Group catalogues a sanni mask under Asian Medicine (S252, Category). A mask's second life can re-classify it.
- **Decay of a tradition.** Pliny says the wax portraits had given way to brazen shields and silver faces admired for their material (S242, 35.6); Nanfeng counted 136 troupes in 2015 with about half active (S243, p. 853).
- **Made to be destroyed.** The musée du quai Branly states that most malagan works were used once and then destroyed, abandoned or sold, and that the Tabar marada heads were "wrongly called masks" because they were too large to wear (S275, "Malagan traditions"; "Calling for rain"). Küchler writes that months of preparation end in an hour and that what outlasts the carving is an image whose access defines rank, authority and rights to land (S276, Introduction, pp. 1–2). The Fondation Gandur essay says kavat masks are burned after use (S277, "Watch out for the Atutki!"), while the Horniman label says all masks except the kavat are burned or discarded (S278, Commentary): the sources disagree, so decide each mask type's fate separately.
- **The retired mask's second role.** Curnow reports that the *gonde* clown usually wears an old, damaged sowei mask retired from its first function (S261, Sande and Bondo section): a retired mask can invert the type it once carried.

## 9. Words for the mask

- Greek πρόσωπον is first "face, countenance"; the mask sense comes third, and under it LSJ files the Roman ancestor images (S279, sense III). Its fourth sense is the person, "in bodily presence" (S279, sense IV).
- Latin *persōna* covers a mask over the whole head, varied by character, and then the role, which once assumed must be acted (S280, senses I and II.B). Its legal sense carries an exclusion in the same clause, "not including slaves" (S280, sense II.B.2).
- A culture can name its mask after what the mask does; the Sri Lankan kôlam glosses as mockery (S257, p. 57). Whether the face-word, the mask-word and the person-word are one noun is a decision for the conlang (`../linguistics/`).

## 11. NON-CANON worked micro-example

**NON-CANON** — illustration only; decides nothing in any world. A river people keep a set of nine carved faces in a chest above their shrine. The right to carry the chest is inherited in one house; the right to wear a face is earned by age. The faces never speak: a seated reader calls each by its title while it dances. Once a year the reader counts them back into the chest. The language uses one word for "face" and "witness". Every element maps to an attested option in §10 and copies no single tradition.

## 12. Calibration bank

Codes as in `../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`.

- **C — "persona comes from per-sonare, to sound through."** Lewis and Short report the derivation as Gabius Bassus's, quoted by Gellius, not as their own finding (S280, etymology bracket); Gellius calls it clever and witty (S281, 5.7.1); a 2025 study files it as ancient etymology (S282, Resumo, p. 721). State it only as an attributed ancient argument.
- **R — "a mask always hides the wearer."** Transformative rites can show the wearer openly (S239, "Transformations", ¶8); van Beek's secret is public and obvious (S240, ¶4).
- **R — "masks are universal and mean the same everywhere."** The editors call universal definitions futile (S239, "Conclusion", ¶1); the Oslo museum records regional traditions representing the same demons differently (S250, Sanniyäkuma section).
- **B — "ancestor masks are portraits."** True for the Roman imagines as Polybius describes them (S241, 6.53.5); it does not fit type masks read for role, sex, age and temperament (S243, p. 856).
- **B — museum records.** A holding museum's record is authoritative for its own object's data, not for a tradition's meaning (S252; S253, which identifies its object only as "probably" Pulcinella).
- **C — "Venetian masks existed so that nobles could sin anonymously."** Neveu shows masking regulated and required for entry to the ridotti and theatres, and a 1732 law requiring theatre patrons to wear the bauta and tabarro (S283, pp. 149, 155); he contrasts the bauta, which let the wearer speak freely, with a mask whose wearer was unable to speak (S283, p. 155). The Venetian civic museums' record states that the bauta had the widest permission to roam when other masks were banned, and that its upper lip deformed the wearer's voice (S284, record description, sentences 2 and 4). Masking there was regulated civic dress with its own voice rules, not only a licence.
