## 2. Six design decisions

| decision | question the culture answers | what to record for the handoff |
|---|---|---|
| water regime | which waters the culture works — river, coastal bank with its weather windows, or deep sea out of sight of land | the default hull per water, and the voyage length each hull is trusted for |
| propulsion and material | what pushes the hull (oar, square sail, lateen or gaff sail, engine) and what it is built of (wood, iron, steel) — declared together, not separately | the era rung, the mixed-rig possibilities, and the words each rung adds |
| parts grammar | what the build order names first and what is fastened to it | the first-laid member, the internal binding member, and the head of the ship |
| working register | what the crew says to work the hull — commands, the wage cycle, the songs that pace shared labour | one command set, the pay-cycle rite, and who times the work |
| taboo surface | what may not be said or done aboard, and what is done instead or to make amends | each prohibition, its propitiation, and its enforcer aboard |
| story ship | which hull the culture's tales remember, and what that memory proves | the named ship, its deed, and the protection it is believed to carry |

## 3. Attested anchors (one source per line)

### Hull grammar: naming from the build order

- The keel is the principal piece of timber in a ship, and it is usually first laid on the blocks in building. (S898)
- Falconer's entry compares the ship's carcase to a skeleton: the keel is the back-bone and the timbers the ribs. (S898)
- The keelson (or kelson) is an internal keel laid over the floor-timbers, binding the whole by long bolts driven from without and clinched inside. (S899)
- A false kelson, or kelson rider, is a piece of timber wrought longitudinally above the main kelson. (S899)
- The figure-head is a carved bust or full-length figure over the cut-water of a ship. (S899)

### The apotropaic ship

- The figure-head is, in Smyth's wording, the remains of an ancient superstition. (S899)
- The Carthaginians carried small images to sea to protect their ships, as, Smyth notes, Roman Catholics do still. (S899)
- The head of St. Paul's ship, in Smyth's entry, was Castor and Pollux. (S899)

### The sea-taboo surface

- Whistling for the wind is a superstitious practice among old seamen, who are equally scrupulous to avoid whistling during a heavy gale. (S899)
- Old superstitious seamen scratch the backstays with their nails and whistle to invoke a cat's-paw — the light air that foreruns a steadier breeze. (S899)

### The working register: wage cycle and words

- The advance of two months' wages given to a crew before going to sea is worked off in what the service calls working up the dead horse. (S899)

### Propulsion and material: one coupled ladder

- The changes from sail power to steam power for propulsion, and from wood to iron and steel for construction, proceeded together, though at first very slowly. (S900)
- A 15th-century type carried square rigs on fore and main masts while the mizzen had a lateen sail. (S900)
- "Ship" is the generic name — Old English scip, German Schiff — for the invention by which man conveys himself and his goods upon water. (S900)

## 4. Craft checklist

- Fix the water regime before the hull: a river barge and a deep-sea trader do not share a vocabulary.
- Name the first-laid member and let the rest of the parts grammar hang from it, the way the keel carries keelson, frames and false kelson.
- Stage propulsion and material as one era move: a steam hull of wood reads as a transition, not an anomaly.
- Give the crew a prohibition with a propitiation and an enforcer: what is not said, what is done instead, who sees that it is done.
- Put one carved or carried protection on the hull and decide how old the custom is allowed to be.
- Let the wage cycle leave a phrase behind: the debt worked off before port becomes the culture's idiom.
- Keep period words period: a 1769 shipwright's term may be wrong for a 1500 hull; check before lending it backwards.

## 5. Worked example (non-canon, for the handoff only)

A channel-herring fishery culture, filled from the tables: coastal bank regime, weather-window voyages; single mast, square sail, small lateen mizzen on the larger boats; wood, oak first-laid on the slip with an internal mast-step rather than a full keelson; the build starts with the keel laid on offerings and ends with the head carved as the patron of the beach; the crew may not whistle on the grounds (the wind is already theirs to lose) but whistles aloft; wages advance two lunar phases and the debt is worked off mid-season in a rite the fleet calls carrying the horse ashore; the story ship is the one that came home under jury-rig with its figure-head broken and was never renamed.
