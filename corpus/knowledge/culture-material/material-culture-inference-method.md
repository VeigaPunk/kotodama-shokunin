## 1. The stance: artifacts are evidence of practice, not mirrors of essence

- Two axioms govern every inference in this category. (1) **No artifact-to-meaning dictionary** — Hodder's contextual archaeology: an object's meaning is relational, fixed by its total context of deposition, association, and use, and material culture is *active*: people use things to negotiate identity and power (Hodder 1982, 1986). (2) **No artifact-to-people dictionary either** — artifact style and ethnic group are only loosely coupled; the culture-historical "pots = people" equation is discredited as a default (Shennan 1989; §5).
- Calibration against the processual critique: Hodder's readings are checkable only when bridged by **middle-range theory** — experiment, refitting, use-wear, provenance science that ties what is found to how it got there (Binford 1962, 1981; Renfrew & Bahn). This file therefore treats Hodder as the stance and Binford as the audit: every claim a designer makes about a fictional culture's things should carry a *reason you could, in principle, dig it up*.
- Deliverables this file standardizes (each is a named sheet in §6): the **substrate inventory**, the **constraint stack**, the **production sheet** (chaîne opératoire), the **biography sheet**, the **deposition plan**, and **ladder-tagged claim packets** for lore-shokunin.
- Standing rule (mirrors comparative-frameworks.md §7): the ladder and frameworks below are **etic designer tooling** — never ascribe them to the culture. In-world scholars see only ruins and argue from them with worse calibration; their overreadings are features, not bugs (§6 step 8).

## 2. The inference-confidence ladder (recalibrating Hawkes)

- Hawkes (1954) ranked inference by difficulty: techniques easiest → subsistence/economy → socio-political institutions → religion hardest. Three recalibrations matter for design work: (1) **sourcing science** (isotopes, XRF, DNA) made provenance and exchange the single strongest lever, not a guess; (2) **monumental and cognitive archaeology** showed some cosmology — orientation, alignment, votive repetition — can be more tractable than fine economic detail (Renfrew & Zubrow 1994); (3) **identity** proved harder than culture-history implied, because style signals context-dependently (Wiessner 1983).

| Tier | Claim type | Primary method | Real exemplar | Canon tag |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| L1 | physical fact: material, form, dimensions, visible wear | catalog description (Renfrew & Bahn) | any catalogued vessel or hoard list | HARD |
| L2 | function: what a tool was used for | microwear + experiment + residues (Keeley 1980) | flint sickles with cereal-silica gloss | HARD |
| L3 | technique: production sequence, skill, apprenticeship | chaîne opératoire refit & experiment (Leroi-Gourhan 1964; Lemonnier 1992) | Levallois prepared-core reduction; folded steel blades | HARD |
| L4 | provenance & exchange: where a thing or its maker came from | sourcing science: XRF, lead/strontium/oxygen isotopes (Pulak 1998) | the Uluburun cargo (~10 t copper, ~1 t tin); Amesbury Archer isotope profile (formal pub to-verify) | HARD |
| L5 | behavior & economy: feasts, markets, specialization, hoards | converging deposits + residues + architecture (Dietler 2001; Bradley 1990) | feasting assemblages: brewing residues + serving wares + special buildings | SOFT |
| L6 | identity & boundary: group membership signaled by things | style analysis (Wiessner 1983; Sackett 1982; Hegmon 1992) | Kalahari projectile points: signaling strength varies with social context | SOFT |
| L7 | institutions: rank, polity, priesthood, sumptuary regime | monuments + iconography + settlement hierarchy converged (Trigger 2003) | palatial systems; Andean state logistics | DOCTRINE |
| L8 | belief & cosmology: gods, rites, what deposits mean | iconography + context, texts where they exist (Renfrew & Zubrow 1994; Lewis-Williams 2002 — contested) | cave-art readings; votive interpretations | DESIGN |

- Canon tags for the lore-shokunin handoff (canon decisions are theirs — AGENTS.md): **HARD** = safe to state as world-fact; **SOFT** = state only with two independent converging lines, in-world disagreement permitted; **DOCTRINE** = true because institutions enforce it — write as an institutional claim ("the temple says…"); **DESIGN** = designer's choice — present as in-world belief or antiquarian interpretation, never as settled fact. Most of what worldbuilders *want* to write lives at L7–L8; this method's job is to force the L1–L4 scaffolding that makes those claims feel dig-upable.

## 3. Framework toolbox

| Framework | Core claim | Fictional application | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| Chaîne opératoire (Leroi-Gourhan 1964; Lemonnier 1992) | techniques are learned sequences embedding social choices | every key artifact class gets a production sheet: steps, tools, skill-time, failure modes, apprenticeship | sequence ≠ function; refit and experiment are the checks |
| Formation processes & use-life (Schiffer 1976, 1987) | the record is transformed debris, not a snapshot of life | design discard, repair, recycling, curation as real systems; if your world has archaeologists, their digs obey these transforms | survivorship: perishables vanish (Hurcombe 2014) — plan what is *invisible* |
| Cultural biography of things (Kopytoff 1986; Gosden & Marshall 1999) | objects have social careers: birth, exchange, use, singularization, death | write biographies for regalia, heirloom blades, bells; decide when a thing may be sold vs only gifted/retired | biography ≠ owner's biography; each phase needs in-world norms (Holtorf 2002) |
| Inalienable possessions & gift (Weiner 1992; Mauss 1925) | some things define groups precisely by being kept-while-given | name the categories that may never be sold; the Kula (Malinowski 1922) shows circulation creating fame | pure gift or pure market is rare; mixed regimes are the norm (Sahlins 1972) |
| Art & agency (Gell 1998) | objects act as social agents; art distributes personhood through indexes | mechanics for oath-swords, idol courts, curse tablets, ancestor effigies | contested theory; use as a design engine, not as a truth claim (§7) |
| Technology style (Lechtman 1977, 1984; Pfaffenberger 1992) | among functionally equivalent techniques, cultures choose by value | define each culture's **optimization function**: Andean metallurgy chose color/surface over hardness; a bell-founder chooses sound over edge-holding | not all variation is stylistic — check material necessity first |
| Style theory (Wiessner 1983; Sackett 1982; Hegmon 1992) | emblemic style marks groups, assertive marks persons, isochrestic is unmarked habit | split every material kit into group-markers, individual-markers, invisible habit | signaling is context-dependent; never freeze one code per group |
| Architecture & house societies (Rapoport 1969; Lévi-Strauss 1982; Parker Pearson & Richards 1994; Bourdieu 1979) | the dwelling is a social machine and a cosmogram | derive house plans from kinship + ritual order; house-societies let noble houses outlive blood lineages | phenomenological readings (Tilley 1994) are contested; keep plans checkable |
| Feasting & commensality (Dietler 2001; Hayden 2001) | food events create and politicize obligation | pick a feasting mode per culture — work-party, patron-role, entrepreneurial — and derive vessels, venues, sums from it | feasts leave ambiguous residues; triangulate before canonizing |
| Deposition (Bradley 1990, 2005) | how things end — hoard, votive, grave, refuse — is a cultural grammar | write the death rules for objects: where do broken vows, worn tools, obsolete regalia go? | "ritual" is not a label for the unexplained (§5) |
| Display & fashion (Kroeber 1919; Douglas & Isherwood 1979; Bourdieu 1984; McCracken 1988) | goods make social categories visible; fashion cycles run even in "traditional" dress | give each culture taste classes and a cycle length; sumptuary law is a *reaction* to display pressure — model both together | meaning transfer (McCracken) is a marketing-era model; tune its speed to your era |

## 4. Material constraints → cultural forms: the reasoning spine

- The spine reads: **material properties → technique repertoire → economic organization → social differentiation → symbolic coding**. Constraints do not *determine* forms; they set cost gradients inside which the culture's optimization function (§3, technology style) selects (Lemonnier 1993; Pfaffenberger 1992). Steward's cultural ecology (1955) is the correct strength: constraints, not causes; strong environmental determinism is discredited.

| Constraint | What it pressures | Real exemplar | Derivation question |
|---|---|---|---|
| scarcity / source distance | exchange reach; elite gatekeeping | tin scarcity → Bronze Age long-distance nets and palatial prestige goods (Pulak 1998; Kristiansen & Larsson 2005) | what is far away, who carries it, who profits |
| energy & fuel | scale of pyrotechnology; industrial geography | charcoal-hungry smelting and mining as landscape industry (Craddock 1995) | what burns, who owns the fuel |
| transport & draft animals | state logistics; military reach | camelid transport in the Andes; wheeled toys but no wagons in pre-Columbian Mesoamerica (Trigger 2003) | what loads move, how fast, over what |
| workability & failure cost | apprenticeship length; craft secrecy | lithic chains and their skill curves (Keeley 1980; Lemonnier 1992) | what breaks, what a failed run costs |
| perishability | what survives to be seen; memory politics | textiles dominate daily identity yet vanish from most soils (Barber 1991; Hurcombe 2014) | what will your culture's ruins show |
| visibility & display | sumptuary law; taste classes | murex purple's cost → imperial color law (Pliny, NH 9); Elizabethan and Tokugawa apparel statutes | what shows at fifty meters — who may wear it |
| standardizability | coinage, units, armies, law | Uluburun oxhide ingots; minted coin (Pulak 1998) | what must be identical, who certifies it |
| danger & ritual framing of production | smithcraft taboos; guild religion | African iron smelting as ritual reproduction (Herbert 1984) | which crafts are sacred, polluted, or licensed |
| seasonality | calendar of labor and rite | dry-season firing and clay digs; sailing seasons | what can only be made *when* |

- Worked chains to imitate structurally: **tin → prestige goods** (scarcity forces trade; trade concentrates in palaces; palaces convert control into rank display); **color over hardness** (Lechtman 1984: Andean smiths optimized surface color — one metal, two cultural physics); **permanence split** (Kemp 1989: Egyptian houses of the living in mudbrick, houses for eternity in stone — a worldview legible in a materials ledger); **dye monopoly → sumptuary regime → color grammar** (Pliny NH 9; couples color-terminology.md and color-symbolism.md); **silk as state secret** (Procopius, Wars 8.17: smuggled eggs break a monopoly — scarcity, craft, and law in one line); **plough → gender norms** (Boserup 1965; Alesina, Giuliano & Nunn 2013 — constraint→form reasoning at statistical scale, though the causal mechanism is debated, to-verify).
- Anti-determinism guard, restated for use: when a fictional chain reaches its social layer, the culture still *chooses* — between open firing and kilns, between burying and hoarding the surplus, between displaying and hiding wealth. Record each such choice as a named technological choice (Lemonnier 1993); a chain with zero choices is a deterministic trap, not a culture.

## 5. Failure modes: the calibration table

- **Pots = people.** Treating artifact style as ethnic group membership. Calibrate: migration, fashion, and practice-change all leave similar traces (Shennan 1989); couple style-claims to Wiessner's context-dependence.
- **Grave goods = life résumé.** Burial is performed by the living; assemblages reflect funeral politics, succession claims, and mourning, not a dead person's CV (Parker Pearson 1999). In-world antiquarians may make this error on purpose.
- **Ritual as dustbin.** "Unexplained, therefore ritual" is the classic archaeologist's last resort; ritual and domestic life interpenetrate rather than forming a residue bin (Bradley 2005). Demand a deposition grammar (§3) instead.
- **Survivorship bias / absence of evidence.** The record is a lossy transform of behavior (Schiffer 1987); entire identity systems can be made of things that leave nothing (Hurcombe 2014). Decide your world's missing majority before its visible one.
- **Free analogy.** Ethnographic analogy needs similarity in *relevant respects*, licensed by direct historical continuity or shown regularity — not vibes (Wylie 2002). Every borrowed exemplar in this corpus should name its relevant respects.
- **Structural overreading.** Leroi-Gourhan's paired-composition grammar of cave art drew a strong critique for thin evidential support (Ucko & Rosenfeld 1967); formal-structural approaches survive only where distributional statistics back them. Calibrate: a "closed structural system" claim needs counts, not elegance.
- **Determinism vs pure constructivism.** Both single-cause stories fail; technique and society co-produce (Pfaffenberger 1992). When a fictional culture's tech looks arbitrary, first check physics, then check the optimization function.
- **Lexical order ≠ symbolic order.** Berlin & Kay (1969) constrain color *naming* sequence, not symbolism; the universalism critique is real (Saunders & van Brakel 1997). Dye scarcity is material fact (here); what blue *means* is grammar (color-terminology.md, color-symbolism.md).
- **Frozen "traditional" cultures.** Fashion cycles and taste drift run everywhere (Kroeber 1919); the "timeless peasant" is a museum effect. Deetz (1977) shows a full material worldview (the "Georgian order") can be read rigorously — adopt the rigor, not the confidence.

## 6. Briefing a fictional world from material culture (procedure, steps 0–8)

0. **Substrate inventory**: ecology, materials, energy, transport, calendar, skill pools, media (what can be woven, carved, fired, minted, written). This is the shared contract with symbolism-synthesis-method.md §3 step 0 — same input, both files consume it.
1. **Pick 1–3 optimization functions** (Lechtman-style generative choices): e.g. "ancestor permanence over living comfort", "color over hardness", "sound over durability". These are the culture's material value system, and everything below must be derivable from them.
2. **Derive the technique kit**: for each major artifact class, a production sheet (§3 chaîne opératoire) — steps, tools, skill-time, failure modes. Note where the kit *could* have gone otherwise and record the named choice (§4).
3. **Write biographies** for heirlooms and regalia (§3 Kopytoff/Weiner): birth, naming, exchanges, singularization, retirement. Categories that may never be sold are the culture's inalienables.
4. **Set the boundary kit**: emblemic vs assertive vs isochrestic layers per group; a sumptuary regime tied to *real* scarcity in the substrate, so the law has a physics.
5. **Write the deposition plan**: death rules for objects — hoard, votive, grave, refuse, recycle (Bradley 1990). This is what your world's future archaeology (and in-world antiquarians) will see.
6. **Ladder-tag every claim** (§2 table): HARD/SOFT/DOCTRINE/DESIGN, and hand the tagged packet to lore-shokunin with the tag semantics; they decide canon (AGENTS.md).
7. **Run the anti-pastiche audit**: transplant *structure* (prestige-goods economy, house-society, feasting mode, sumptuary regime), never a real culture wholesale; any single transplanted element must carry its material logic (silk requires sericulture or a route; a sword-cult requires sword-cost logic). Then the four tests — **Weiner test**: who inherits the inalienable? **chaîne test**: who can make it, in how long? **deposition test**: where does it go when it dies? **sumptuary test**: who is forbidden it, by what scarcity?
8. **In-world epistemics**: in-world scholars run the same ladder with worse calibration; their misreadings (grave-résumé, ritual-dustbin, pots=people) are published errors, court scandals, and revisionist monographs — hand them off as candidate lore, tagged, not as fact.
