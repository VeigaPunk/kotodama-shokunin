## 1. Core framework: the four gates

- **Fiber gate** — what the land raises (flax fields, sheep flocks, mulberry/silkworm sheds, cotton, hemp). Sets the default cloth, the prep-verb vocabulary (ret, scutch, hackle, card, comb, reel), and *who* performs each stage (gender/estate assignment is a design lever; see Barber 1994 on the household-female → guild-male shift when speed technology arrives).
- **Dye gate** — which colors are makeable, fast, and cheap. Sets the palette, the rank code, and the single most monopoly-able good: the rarest *fast* dye. Chemistry classes: **mordant dyes** (need a metal salt — alum brightens, iron "saddens"/darkens, tin brightens [Europe 16th c.+], chrome is industrial-19th-c.-only), **vat dyes** (indigo/woad/purple — insoluble until reduction in a fermenting vat, then air-oxidized blue on the fiber), **direct dyes** (turmeric, safflower yellow — vivid but fugitive; the poor's colors).
- **Loom gate** — maximum cloth width and pattern complexity. Sets garment *architecture* (panels vs tailoring) and the culture's cloth-measure words. The cloth physically encodes its loom (§4): an in-world archaeologist should be able to reconstruct your loom from a scrap.
- **Finish gate** — fulling, nap-raising, shearing capacity (§5). Sets the grade ladder (broadcloth vs kersey vs frieze), the mill/urban geography of the trade, and most of the idiom layer.
- Binding rule: **sumptuary law converts technology into vocabulary fastest** (Hunt 1996). The statute's tier words become the culture's status nouns within a generation; law tracks your grade ladder, and the ladder tracks the four gates.

## 2. The fiber hierarchy

| Fiber | Organism | Prep chain | Dye affinity | Default prestige position | Lexical residue |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| linen (flax) | *Linum usitatissimum* | ret (dew/water) → break → scutch → hackle → wet-spin | poor — bast fibers resist dye without heavy mordant | purity: Egyptian priestly linen, wool taboo in sacred precincts (Herodotus 2.81; his reliability is uneven — flag in-world) | linen, linsey-woolsey; *lingerie* < Fr. *linge* "linen" < *linum* |
| wool | *Ovis aries* | shear → wash (suint/urine, lanolin) → card or comb → spin | excellent — scaled fiber grips dye; richest colors are wools | the state economy: Ur III wool-ration workshops, English export, Flemish drapery (Breniquet & Michel 2014) | worsted, woolen, fleece, staple, *Walker/Tucker* = fullers |
| silk | *Bombyx mori* (domesticated, flightless) | rear larvae → boil cocoons → **reele** (never spun) → throw (twist) | excellent | imperial monopoly: China (Kuhn 1988) → Byzantium after 552 CE egg-smuggling (Procopius *Wars* 8.17) → Italian cities | silk, throwster, organzine (warp), tram (weft) |
| cotton | Old World *G. arboreum/herbaceum* (diploid); New World *G. hirsutum/barbadense* (tetraploid) | gin seeds (hand roller-gin; saw gin 1793 unlocks short-staple) → card → spin | poor unaided; solved by India's mordant-*printed* cottons (chintz) | commoner cloth + export giant (Riello 2013); Edo Japan: commoners legally pushed to cotton/hemp, silk reserved upward | calico, chintz, muslin, denim, khadi |
| hemp | *Cannabis sativa* | water-ret (stinking trade) → hackle → spin | poor | sacred + servile simultaneously: Shinto ritual fiber (*asa*); everywhere sails, cordage, workwear | canvas (< *cannabis*, OED), rope-walk, sailcloth |

- **Wool technology is the fork that matters**: combed long-staple = **worsted** (smooth, strong, drapey, takes fine pattern); carded short-staple = **woolen** (fuzzy, insulating, hides weave, fulls well). Ryder (1983) traces fleece evolution: wild sheep are hairy; wool-dominant fleeces are a Bronze-Age artifact of selection — your culture's fleece quality is a *chronology* you can date.
- **Silk is not spun** — the only natural filament fiber; one cocoon yields a single continuous filament (figures from ~300 to ~900+ m circulate; to-verify a canonical range before quoting). Reeling vs spinning is a whole separate toolchain, hence a separate trade vocabulary (throwster, filature).
- **Linen is the prestige fiber that resists its own prestige**: dye-poor, so elite linen status rides on *whiteness and thread count*, not color. Fine-ancient-linen thread counts vary wildly across the literature; treat any specific figure (e.g. "finer than anything modern mills make") as false — modern cambric exceeds ancient fineness (to-verify exact comparisons; Vogelsang-Eastwood 1993 is the standard route).
- Minor fibers as plot seams: nettle as prehistoric bast/silk-surrogate (Barber 1991); camelid hierarchy vicuña > alpaca > llama (vicuña reserved for Inca royalty — commonly stated; verify against the chronicles before canonizing); sea-silk (*Pinna nobilis* byssus) is real, but identifying Biblical *byssus* with it is contested — mainstream reading is simply fine linen.
- Calibration: claimed silk at Harappan sites (wild *Antheraea*?) is contested scholarship (to-verify via I. Good's publications before citing).

## 3. Dyes: chemistry, prestige, trade

| Dye | Source | Chromophore | Class / fastness | Origin & trade | Prestige note |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Tyrian purple | *Bolinus brandaris*, *Hexaplex trunculus*, *Stramonita haemastoma* (hypobranchial gland) | 6,6′-dibromoindigotin (Friedländer 1909) | vat; fast | E. Mediterranean; tens of shells per gram of dye (popular counts of "thousands per gram" — to-verify) | the Roman imperial monopoly; apex of Diocletian's 301 CE price schedule — quote exact figures only from a critical edition |
| "tekhelet" blue | *H. trunculus*, processed toward blue | indigotin + brominated analogs | vat | Levant | Biblical blue-thread identification is a live, contested debate (Spanier 1987) |
| indigo | *Indigofera tinctoria* etc. | indigotin | vat; fast | India → world; Bengal plantations; industrial synthesis 1897 (structure Baeyer 1883) | banned as "the devil's dye" by woad-protecting states (Balfour-Paul); dates/edicts vary by state — verify per polity |
| woad | *Isatis tinctoria* | indigotin (same molecule) | vat; fast | Europe (Thuringia, Toulouse) | Europe's blue before indigo; Caesar's *BGall.* 5.14 says Britons stain with *vitrum* — read as woad, but paint-vs-tattoo is contested |
| madder | *Rubia tinctorum* root | alizarin + purpurin | mordant (alum=red, iron=purple-black); fast | W. Asia → Europe (Zeeland, Alsace) | **Turkey red** (oil-alum mordant process) was the technological summit; Europe replicated it only in the 18th c. (Vale of Leven, Scotland); synthetic alizarin 1868–69 (Graebe & Liebermann; Perkin in parallel) killed the fields |
| kermes | *Kermes vermilio* scale insect on oak | kermesic acid | mordant; fast | Mediterranean | "dyed **in the grain**" → *ingrain*; *crimson* < Arabic *qirmiz* (< Sanskrit *kṛmi-ja* "worm-made", standard etymology) |
| cochineal | *Dactylopius coccus* on *Opuntia* | carminic acid | mordant; fast | Mesoamerica/Andes; Spanish monopoly via Veracruz (Greenfield 2005; Phipps 2010); Canary Islands still harvest | far stronger red per weight than kermes (avoid quoting exact multiples); "second-most-valuable New World export" is a popular claim — verify before canonizing |
| safflower | *Carthamus tinctorius* | carthamin (pink) + fugitive yellow | direct; pink fast only with care | Egypt → India → Japan (*beni*, luxury Edo cosmetics) | the dye-as-makeup seam |
| saffron | *Crocus sativus* stigmas | crocetin/crocins | direct; fades | Mediterranean/Iran | costliest dye-spice; double-use makes it an economy, not a color |
| weld | *Reseda luteola* | luteolin | mordant; very fast | Europe | the canonical fast yellow; woad+weld overdye = the medieval *stable* green (Pastoureau 2001) |
| sappan / brazilwood | *Caesalpinia* spp. | brazilin | mordant; fugitive | SE Asia; Brazil | the wood **named the country** (*brasa* "ember") — the strongest toponym template in the corpus |
| logwood | *Haematoxylum campechianum* | hematoxylin | mordant; fugitive→fast with mordant | Yucatán; British logging | blacks/purples for commoners; later a histology stain |
| walnut / oak galls | tannins (+ iron) | gallotannates | direct/mordant; fast | worldwide | the poor's black; same chemistry as iron-gall ink — a culture's ink and black dye may share a supply chain |

- **Alum is the trade's salt**: near-universal mordant, geographically concentrated (Egyptian oases; the papal Tolfa monopoly from 1462 — commonly cited date). Whoever holds alum holds every mordant color.
- **The purple case study** (the template for designing a monopoly dye): biology (tiny gland per shell) → chemistry isolated only in 1909 (Friedländer) → processing nuances shift *H. trunculus* from violet toward blue (mechanisms still debated) → law (toga praetexta's purple border, senatorial *latus clavus* vs equestrian *angustus clavus* — stripes *woven in*, not sewn on; Byzantine *porphyra* birth-room → *Porphyrogenitus* "born in the purple") → language (*purpureus* → "purple prose", Horace *Ars Poetica* 15–16; for most of history purple meant a *rank*, not a hue — couple to myth-symbol/color-symbolism.md) → rupture (Pope Paul II moves cardinals to kermes scarlet, 1464, commonly cited).
- **The synthetic rupture is an era-marker lever**: mauveine 1856 (Perkin; "magenta" 1859, after the battle), alizarin 1868–69, indigo 1897. On the near side, the entire prestige geography of color (madder fields, Bengal indigo, cochineal fleets, murex fisheries) collapses into chemistry. A world without alchemical dyers keeps the old geography; a world with them has none.

## 4. Loom types and their cloth signatures

| Loom | Era / region | Tension | Practical width | Forensic signature in the cloth |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| ground loom (horizontal, staked beams) | Neolithic–pharaonic Egypt, hot climates | pegs/beams | ~weaver's reach | narrow panels; both selvedges finished alike; flat, even tension |
| warp-weighted loom | Neolithic Europe, Aegean, Norse north (Hoffmann 1964) | clay/stone loomweights on warps | ~1–1.5 m | woven top-down from a **starting border**; selvedges often carry doubled, weight-bearing warps; loomweights in pits are the archaeological tell |
| two-beam vertical | Rome onward; Coptic Egypt; high-warp tapestry | both beams | ~2 m | no starting border; symmetric ends; widens cloth |
| backstrap (continuous warp) | Mesoamerica, SE Asia, Andes variant | weaver's body via strap | ~armspan (40–70 cm) | selvedges finer than the field; one-piece panels; loom dismantles into cords; Classic Maya ceramics depict women at them |
| Andean staked vertical | Andes, precontact | fixed bars/stakes | small-to-medium | **complementary-warp** patterns identical on both faces; geometric rank-coded designs; no spinning wheel, no treadle in the entire precontact hemisphere |
| treadle loom, multi-shaft | Han China (tomb models/tiles, e.g. Chengdu finds; to-verify details) → Europe by 11th c. | treadles/cams | long lengths, wide | regular repeat structures (2/2 twills); weft density regularity; enables the male urban guild era (Barber 1994) |
| drawloom (pattern) | China (*jin*), Sasanian Persia, Byzantium, Italy 13th c. | drawboy selects warps | wide | figural repeats — Sasanian-style **pearl-roundel silks**; patterned silk as diplomatic currency |
| broadloom + flying shuttle (Kay 1733) → power loom (Cartwright 1785) | industrial Europe | mechanical | beyond armspan | machine-even threads, near-zero width variation |

- Forensics usable in prose: **cloth width = loom width**; selvedge construction; presence of a starting border; **S vs Z spin direction** (spindle-rotation handedness clusters by region and period — the cheapest single forensic, per Barber/Wild); thread regularity (handspun slubs vs machine evenness); weave structure (tabby vs twill vs compound).
- **Width breeds architecture** (Burnham 1973, *Cut My Cote*): pre-modern garments are assemblies of rectangles because looms do not waste cloth — Doric chiton and Roman tunica (seamed rectangles with woven *clavi*), Egyptian kalasiris, huipil (three backstrap panels), kimono (standard bolt *tan*, c. 36 cm × 12–13 m; figures vary by period, to-verify), toga (one long length, draped not cut). Curved tailoring — set-in sleeves, gores, gussets — appears when cloth is *cheap* (or wool is elastic enough to cheat): treat "tailored culture" vs "draped culture" as a textile-economy readout, not an aesthetic choice.

## 5. Finishing: the expensive half

- **Fulling** (Roman *fullonica*; Flohr 2013 catalogs dozens at Pompeii/Ostia): scour in stale urine and fuller's earth → tread/stomp (the *saltus fullonicus*) → rinse → raise nap with teasels (*Dipsacus fullonum*) → **shear/crop** the nap level with heavy shears on a shearman's board → stretch on **tenterhooks** → press. Medieval north: water-powered fulling mills spread 12th–13th c. (England's "first" at Temple Newsham 1185 is commonly cited; to-verify).
- Finishing can cost as much as weaving and defines the **grade words**: broadcloth (heavily fulled, sheared, "broad" only after finishing), kersey (Kersey, Suffolk), frieze (napped coarse wool), flannel, stuffs, and the 16th-c. English "new draperies" (light worsteds, bays/says).
- Guild geography as worldbuilding: Florence split **Arte della Lana** (production) from **Arte della Calimala** (import + refine/finish — the luxury brand was the *finisher's*, not the weaver's); England's wool export ran through the Company of the Staple at Calais (1363). Mechanical nap-raising (gig mill) drew shearmen's riots in England (commonly cited late 16th–17th c.) — technology-displacement riots are prepackaged plot.
- The stink trades (retting, fulling, tanning-adjacent) sit downstream of euphemism and taboo — couple to linguistics/politeness-taboo-euphemism.md.

## 6. How textile tech bounds what a costume vocabulary can name

| Gate | Word classes it generates | Real exemplars |
|---|---|---|
| fiber | prep verbs; occupational nouns/surnames; gender-marked trades; abstract metaphors | Webster (fem. *-ster* weaver), Walker/Tucker (fullers), Sherman (shearman), Dyer, Draper, Mercer, Throwster (silk-throwing); *spinster*, *distaff side*; PIE \**teks-* "weave" → *text, textile, technology* (Watkins 2011) |
| dye | fused color-cloth words; toponymic fabrics; trade loan chains | *scarlet* = a fine wool cloth before it is a color (OED); *crimson/carmine* < *qirmiz*; Slavic \**čьrvь* "worm" → *czerwony/červonyj* "red"; *porphyry*, *ingrain*; damask (Damascus), muslin (Mosul), calico (Calicut), denim (*de Nîmes*), cambric (Cambrai), jersey, cashmere, worsted, tulle, angora; *milliner* (Milan); Arabic *quṭn* → *cotton, algodón, algodão*; Greek *Sēres* "silk-people" (China); *satin* commonly derived from Arabic *zaytūnī* "of Zayton/Quanzhou" (etymology debated); *chintz* < Hindi *chīṇṭ* |
| loom | measure words (= cloth width); garment-architecture nouns; forensic nouns | ell/ulna, cloth-yard, *tan* bolt; panel, gore, gusset, *clavus*; selvedge, starting border, S/Z twist |
| finish | grade nouns; idioms; guild/office words | broadcloth, kersey, frieze, flannel, stuff; *on tenterhooks*, *dyed-in-the-wool*, *cut from the same cloth*, *coarse* (of people) |
| (cross-gate) sumptuary law | status nouns; taboo compounds | 1363 English statute tiers fabric by rank and Elizabethan apparel proclamations (Hunt 1996); Tokugawa *ken'yakurei* (silk upward, cotton/hemp for commoners); Ming–Qing *buzi* rank badges — civil birds vs military beasts (Cammann 1952); Roman *clavus* grades; Levitical *shaʿatnez* wool-linen ban (Lev 19:19; Deut 22:11; etymology unknown — a gift: a taboo word with no recoverable derivation is realistic); Inca vicuña reservation (commonly stated; verify against chronicles) |

- The binding rule stated linguistically: a culture cannot have a *basic* costume term for a cloth or color its four gates cannot make. Dye technology does **not** change Berlin & Kay stage order (that critique belongs to linguistics/color-terminology.md) — it changes the **focal exemplars**: a "red" prototyped on kermes scarlet is a different red from one prototyped on madder brick, and the prestige codes build on whichever the elite controls.
- False-etymology trap list (do not port these as real, but all are realistic folk etymologies your fictional philologists could invent): *woman* ≠ "weaver-man" (OE *wīfmann*, "woman-person" — the weaving folk etymology is modern); "loom large" is unrelated to the weaving loom (separate Middle English lexemes — standard lexicography, verify OED s.v.); "gauze from Gaza" is a doubtful medieval story; "seersucker" < Persian *shīr-o-shakar* "milk-and-sugar" is popular but shaky.

## 7. Worldbuilding application checklist

1. Fix the fiber suite from climate; name 4–5 prep verbs; assign each stage a gender/estate.
2. Fix the dye palette: 3–4 fast colors + 2 fugitive; designate one **monopoly dye** and write its supply chain (organism → processing → route → tax).
3. Fix the loom set; derive garment architecture from maximum width (§4 rule); name the measure word after the loom's output.
4. Fix finishing capacity; build a 3-rung grade ladder with proper names (broadcloth-class → kersey-class → frieze-class).
5. Derive the lexicon: 3 toponymic cloth names (port-towns on the dye routes); 5 occupational surnames; 1 fused color-cloth word; 2 idioms from the finish trade; 1 taboo compound (a *shatnez*-class ban whose etymology is already lost).
6. Write the sumptuary statute in the culture's own tier vocabulary (Hunt 1996 template); penalties payable in cloth.
7. Add the ritual layer: priest-fiber purity rule (linen-in-Egypt template), sacred fiber (hemp-in-Japan template), and one rank-costume badge system (*buzi* template) whose iconography encodes office.
8. Write one forensic paragraph — a cloth scrap described as an in-world archaeologist would see it (width, selvedge, spin direction, dyes) — so the past you implied is checkable inside the fiction.
9. Contrast drill: a steppe wool culture (worsted combed, madder/kermes reds as rank, warp-weighted or treadle, trousers = tailoring from felt-and-surplus) vs a monsoon cotton culture (printed mordant cottons, blue vat as the sacred color, backstrap panels, wrapped/rectangle dress) should generate non-overlapping lexicons from the same framework — if they don't, a gate is being ignored.
