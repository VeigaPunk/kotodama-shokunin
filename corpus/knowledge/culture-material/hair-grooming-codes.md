## 1. State and institutional control of hair

- Qing regent Dorgon's renewed July 1645 decree ordered that all Chinese men must shave their heads and begin growing the queue within 10 days or face execution, and Manchu soldiers enforced it with hired barbers (S833)
- Peter the Great's decree of 16 January 1705 taxed beards: Russians who paid received copper tokens issued for paying the beard tax in the time of Peter the Great, inscribed "money taken" (S834)
- Samurai dress codes bound the male head: the chonmage topknot began as hair tied and tucked into a ponytail, the origin of the "mage", before warriors shaved the crown (sakayaki) to keep the kabuto cool (S835)
- Pitt's 1795 hair-powder duty required that anyone who wished to use hair powder had to obtain an annual certificate from their local Justice of the Peace and to pay a stamp duty of one guinea (S844)
- Missouri law as challenged in Niang v. Carroll required hair braiders to obtain a license to practice either cosmetology or barbering before they could braid African-style hair for pay (S843)
- The mid-Victorian beard revival was argued in health terms: an 1853 Household Words essay held that no better summer shield or winter covering against the sun or storm can be provided than a beard (S848)

## 2. Faith and ritual grooming codes

- Buddhist monks and nuns shave the head as one of the key indicators of renunciation showing that a person has given up ordinary life and will live outside of social conventions (S846)
- The Catholic rite of tonsure received a baptized and confirmed Christian into the clerical order by the shearing of his hair and the investment with the surplice (S836)
- Initiated Khalsa Sikhs keep kesh, uncut hair, which the BBC's religion guide says has been regarded throughout history as a symbol both of holiness and strength (S837)
- Manusmriti 2.35 with Medhatithi's commentary prescribes that the Tonsure-ceremony of all twice-born children should be performed, according to law, in the first or third year (S838)
- Numbers 6:5 binds the Nazirite for the vow's whole term: no razor shall pass over his head, and the growing hair marks consecration until the release offering (S839)
- Rastafari dreadlocks rest on the Nazarite rule, quoting Numbers that he shall be holy and shall let the locks of the hair of his head grow; the length of the locks measures time in the faith (S840)
- The hadith of the five fitrah practices commands circumcision, shaving the pubic hair, cutting the moustaches short, clipping the nails, and depilating the hair of the armpits (S852)
- Observant Jewish men grow pe'ot, the side-locks worn by Jewish men, especially those of Poland and Russia, kept from Leviticus 19:27's ban on rounding the head's corners (S853)

## 3. The wig and hair trades

- The British Museum's 18th-dynasty human-hair wig was built on a plaited mesh further secured with an application of a "setting" mixture made of two-thirds beeswax and one-third imported conifer resin (S845)
- Colonial Williamsburg's wigmaker joined barbering, hair-dressing and peruke-making services in one shop; apprenticeship to a master barber and perukemaker was the normal, in fact the only, way for a boy to learn the trade (S855)
- Pehr Kalm's 1748 account of England reported that farm-servants, clodhoppers, day-labourers, and all labouring-folk went about their duties with peruques on the head, and very few wore only their own hair (S856)
- The barber's pole survives the barber-surgeons' bloodletting: its colours represent the bandages, clean and bloodstained, wound around the staff grasped by those about to be bled from the arm (S847)
- Victorian mourning jewellery was made from human hair because hair keeps for a long time; professional hair artists worked the hair of the deceased into brooches, bracelets and wreaths (S849)

## 4. Hair politics and identity

- The 1920s bob was read as a liberation: new short haircuts announced the wearers' break from tradition, boosted the hairdressing industry, and sent women to barbers when hairdressers refused (S850)
- Cambridge's Museum of Archaeology and Anthropology traced the Afro comb across 6,000 years of culture, politics and identity, from Predynastic Egypt to the twentieth-century black-fist pick (S851)
- Nineteenth-century physicians feared plica polonica, matted hair thought to be the effect of a spell cast by witch or a punishment from God, and believed cutting it would kill the patient (S854)

## 5. Tapu, rank and the dressed head

- For Māori, the head is the most tapu (sacred) part of the body, so ornaments worn on or near the head, like kapeu and kuru, take on its tapu state (S841)
- High-status Māori men oiled, braided and dressed the hair upon the head in elaborate topknots, enhanced with heru combs and the feathers of the now-extinct hūia, as an outward expression of mana (S842)
