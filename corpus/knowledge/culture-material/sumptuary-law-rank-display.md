# Sumptuary Law and Rank Display — Legal Dress Codes, Their Assays, and the Language of Regulation

## 1. What sumptuary law is — and the two readings worth having

- **Definition**: statutes, proclamations, and codified edicts restricting consumption by rank, gender, or religion — dress is the dominant target, but the same genre also regulates food, funerals, weddings, carriages, horses, livery, games. Hunt (1996) is the standard single-author history and his thesis is the useful one: these are not primarily "anti-luxury morals," they are **governance** — tying visible display to visible degree, protecting the fiscal/military base, disciplining gender.
- **Five motives recur, and reading motive from the text is a craft skill**: (1) hierarchy maintenance (make degree readable); (2) protectionism (the English cap act exists to sell caps, not virtue — read the preamble); (3) moral/gender discipline (Florence policed women's trousseaus; Rome's lex Oppia); (4) monopoly (imperial color/fiber); (5) ritual control (Lateran IV 1215 canon 68 orders Jews and Saracens to be marked by dress — sumptuary logic serving religious marking, not luxury control).
- **Calibration**: "sumptuary laws always failed" is itself too strong. Price-graded regimes in market economies (England, Italy) were reissued again and again — each reenactment is evidence of weak enforcement, or of ongoing political usefulness; both readings are on the table. But dye-grade and badge regimes with administrative assay (Tang/Ming China) ran for centuries as routine bureaucracy. Failure tracks assay technology, not human vice.

## 2. The regulated object — a six-axis typology

| Axis | What the law fixes | Real exemplars |
|---|---|---|
| color (dye-grade) | who may wear which dye grade | Roman purpura grades; Tang robe colors; Ming imperial yellow; Spanish princely black (Pastoureau 2009); Lateran IV distinct-dress marking |
| fiber/material | which cloth, not which look | Mexica cotton vs. maguey; Edo commoner hemp/cotton limits; English "cloth of gold" and silk bans |
| price | value per ell/yard, keyed to income | 1363 English statute (cloth ≤ 2s for laborers); 1574 Elizabethan proclamation grades cloth by price |
| trim/embellishment | fur, lace, jewels, trains, pearls | ermine/sable reserved to high nobility; Florentine train-length and pearl limits (Hughes 1983) |
| garment-type/badge | named items, insignia, weapons | Roman toga types; Tang fish-pouch tally; Ming buzi badges; Edo two swords; English woolen cap |
| quantity/occasion | how many, and when | gowns-per-year limits; trousseau allowed on the wedding day; funeral and festival provisions |

- **The regulated person is a seventh axis, and it does the political work**: by estate/degree, by gender (women pinned to fathers'/husbands' rank — the "wives problem," §6), by religion (Lateran IV), by office (China), by status-category (casta, §7).
- **Who enforces is an eighth axis — the inspectorate table**:

| System | Enforcement officer | Modus |
|---|---|---|
| Rome | aediles (market/morals police) | street inspection of dress and carriage |
| Florence (14th c.) | Ufficiali delle donne — women's officials (office established 1333; some accounts give 1330/early 1330s) | powers of entry at weddings/trousseaus; Hughes 1983; *The Right to Dress* (CUP 2019) |
| England | churchwardens, "searchers," aulnagers (cloth measurers) | parish presentments; cloth assay at the trade level |
| Tang/Ming China | robed bureaucracy itself; code courts | dress is self-displaying rank; violation surfaces at ritual/procedural moments |
| Edo Japan | bakufu and domain officialdom — town magistrates and their neighborhood agents (no evidence found of a dress-specific inspectorate) | episodic crackdowns; markets self-police under threat |
| Casta New Spain | corregidores, ecclesiastical courts, notaries | dress read as evidence in litigation (§7) |

- **Craft rule**: real laws almost always regulate a *measurable surrogate* — dye grade, price, width, material, badge — never "dignity" in the abstract. If your fictional statute names no assay, it is decorative.
- **Craft rule 2**: match the inspectorate to the assay. A price schedule needs market inspectors; a dye-grade regime needs chemists; a badge regime needs only the personnel files; an evidence regime needs a courtroom. A regime whose officers cannot assay its sign collapses into the evidence mode or dies.

## 3. Rome: purple and the clavus — status as stripe width and dye grade

- **The toga is a legal garment**: citizens wear it, the non-citizen and the infamis may not. Augustus required the toga in the forum (Suetonius, *Aug.* 40). Sub-types are rank-coded: **toga praetexta** (purple border — curule magistrates, freeborn children), **toga picta/triumphalis** (all-purple with gold — triumphators), **toga candida** (chalked white — whence *candidate*), **trabea** (a purple-striped toga; equestrian and kingly by association — *trabeati* as a name for equites — and Servius lists three kinds (all-purple for the gods, purple-and-white royal, purple-and-saffron for augurs); the standard caveat holds that sources disagree on its cut and on later usage (Smith 1875, s.v. *toga*).
- **The tunica carries the legal stripe**: **latus clavus** (broad purple stripe = senator), **angustus clavus** (narrow = eques). The regulated unit is *width* — an assayable measure, exactly like cloth price in England.
- **Purple is a dye schedule, not a hue**: murex secretion at enormous cost per gram; Pliny (NH 9) grades the shades; Diocletian's Edict (301 CE) lists purpura grades at wildly different prices — a legal price schedule for the status color itself. Reinhold (1970) traces purple's drift into imperial monopoly; "born in the purple" (*porphyrogenitos*) is the fossil of that monopoly.
- **lex Oppia (215–195 BCE)**: wartime limits on women's ornaments and carriages; Livy 34 preserves the repeal debate — Cato's slippery-slope of hierarchy versus Valerius' counter — the classic primary text for sumptuary *rhetoric*, usable verbatim as a fictional senate scene.
- **Craft note**: define a status color by *process* (an organic molecule, a number of dips, a dye-house license), not by hue. Then enforcement means assaying dye — inspectors sniff, dissolve, or burn a thread — and counterfeiting dye becomes the world's forgery trade.

## 4. China: Tang and Ming — rank as administrable color and badge

- **Tang robe colors = rank** (purple = 3rd rank and above; crimson (*zhū*) = 4th–5th; green = 6th–7th; blue-green (*qīng*) = 8th–9th — the full ladder is standardly dated to the 674 (Shangyuan 1) regulation under Gaozong, after Zhenguan-era edicts had put purple at 3rd rank and above, with later edicts shifting details): yellow reserved to the emperor — but exclusivity is a Tang-era development, not an ancient invariant: an early Wude order had reserved the imperial red-yellow, and Gaozong's 668 (Zongzhang 1) edict barred officials and commoners from yellow altogether; commoners thereafter undyed white. Schafer (1963) gives the material-culture texture behind the code.
- **Accessory privileges stack on top**: the **fish-pouch** (*yudai*) — the standard formulation is gold for the 3rd rank and above, silver for the 5th and above (《新唐書·車服志》: 三品以上飾以金，五品以上飾以銀; the tally itself was issued from the 5th rank up, formalized under Gaozong) — was simultaneously display and a palace-entry credential, the tally being presented at the palace gate; belt-plate materials graded (jade/gold/silver). Display doubles as a *pass*: the garment carries a permission.
- **Legal register**: the Tang Code punishes unlawful assumption of official dress and imperial things, scaled by the rank of the thing assumed, under heads of "great irreverence" (Johnson's translation gives the article voice; the code's article-opener "諸" — "in all cases of..." — is the canonical Chinese legislative formula, directly liftable into a conlang's legal register).
- **Ming**: Hongwu-era codification adds the **buzi** rank badge — a square panel, civil bird front-and-back, military beast; imperial yellow monopoly tightened; commoner silk restricted in principle (**Mingshi** juan 67, 1370: commoners may wear 綢/絹/素紗 (plain silk and gauze) but not 金繡/錦綺/紵絲/綾羅 (gold-work, brocades, damasks), and never yellow — with the fiber bans eroding across the dynasty as commercial silk spread); the Great Ming Code (Jiang trans. 2005) supplies sanctions. Qing continues the system and adds **hat-button finials** graded gem-to-metal (ruby, coral, sapphire, lapis, crystal, shell, gold, silver, 1st→9th) and codifies dress pictorially in the *Huangchao liqi tushi* (1759).
- Civil buzi ladder (standard list; the minor rungs differ by dynasty — Ming: 8th oriole, 9th quail; Qing: 8th quail, 9th paradise flycatcher, the oriole dropped): 1 crane · 2 golden pheasant · 3 peacock · 4 wild goose · 5 silver pheasant · 6 egret · 7 mandarin duck · 8 quail/oriole · 9 paradise flycatcher. Military beasts: qilin, lion, leopard, tiger, bear, leopard-cat (6–7), rhinoceros, seahorse.
- **Craft note**: badge systems are machine-readable rank for *examination* empires — status attaches to the job and the garment, is revocable, and must be legible at corridor distance. When officials are appointed rather than born, sumptuary law migrates from "who you are" to "what you hold."

## 5. Edo Japan: status outfit and the hidden-luxury dialect

- **The samurai's legal markers are the two swords and silk entitlement**; townspeople (chōnin) were pushed toward hemp/cotton and plain cloth by recurring bakufu and domain edicts (no single unified dress code — edict streams under the buke shohatto umbrella; dated examples to cite: the 1683 material bans on crepe, twill, and figured silk for townsmen, the 1719 "List of Prohibitions Concerning Clothing for Edo Townsmen," the Tenpō-era 1842 silk ban — always cite a decree with its year). Enforcement was episodic; compliance partial — Hanley (1997) shows everyday consumption above the legal floor; Shively (1964–65) is the standard survey of the edicts.
- **The famous consequence is the fashion dialect the bans created**: luxury migrated to the *underside* — linings (*ura*), inner collars, the back of the obi. Kuki (1930) builds the aesthetic of *iki* on exactly this: subdued surface, rich concealment. Dalby (1993) documents the sumptuary-creativity loop in kimono practice. Kabuki actors, the era's trendsetters, were repeat targets of anti-gaudiness bans (an early dated case: the 1636 jailing of the Saruwaka-za manager and an actor for overly sumptuous costumes; the Tenpō reforms again banned gaudy display in 1842 — Shively, "Bakufu Versus Kabuki").
- **Calibration**: the tidy "four classes" (shi-nō-kō-shō) is an ideological simplification — the actual mibun order included courtiers, clergy, and outcast groups, and varied locally (Howell 2005). Do not build a fictional status system on the schoolbook pyramid; build it on the edict streams and the leaks.
- **Craft note (the strongest move in this file)**: write the ban, then design the *dialect it produces*. Sign migration patterns: surface→lining, color→weave technique, material→workmanship, garment→trim. Fashion history is the patch notes of the law.

## 6. England: price-schedule statutes, the cap act, and the wives problem

- **1363 (37 Edw. III) Statute of Apparel**: a full estate ladder from ploughmen (blanket/russet, cloth ≤ 2s the ell, and — unusually — *required* to wear it) up through esquires and gentlemen (no cloth of gold, silk, or stones below a land threshold) to lords. Enforcement assigned to churchwardens and "searchers"; generally judged ineffective (Baldwin 1926) — and reissued for a century, which is its own data point.
- **The English drafting trick is price-per-ell**: because fashion mutates faster than Parliament, the statute grades by *cloth value*, not by named fabric — a mutation-proof schedule. And it indexes display to *revenue* (annual income/land value): a legal yield-curve for ostentation.
- **1571 Cap Act (13 Eliz. I c.19)**: every male 6+ to wear a woolen cap on Sundays and holidays (listed exemptions: lords, gentlemen, substantial landholders, heads of corporations), fine 3s. 4d. This is a protectionist sumptuary — the stated aim is the cappers' trade. Read motive from the preamble, not from the object.
- **1574 Elizabethan proclamation on apparel**: grades silks, cloth of gold, pearls, furs down the ladder — and grades *women through men*: "wives of..." rungs descend below the male degrees (the fur ladder is explicit and liftable: sables reserved to duchesses, marquises, and countesses and above, then black genets and lucernes, then foins/grey genets/budge — ermine is *not* named in this proclamation; Hughes & Larkin, *Tudor Royal Proclamations* 2, no. 601). The **wives problem**: a woman's legal rank is her husband's, so rich merchants' wives overshoot the law's grid — an artifact of the law's grammar, not the society's.
- **Enforcement economy**: forfeiture of the garment itself, with fines and forfeitures commonly shared with informers (Baldwin 1926, 270–71: the cap provision 8 Eliz. c.11 §5 forfeits 10s per offending item — one moiety to the Queen, the other to whoever sues, the suing cappers being the natural informants; per-statute details vary); aulnagers inspected cloth. The informer's moiety creates a standing surveillance class — tailors and dyers become de facto inspectors.
- **Language**: "no person under the degree of...", "shall not wear nor take upon them to wear", "over and above his degree or estate." The degree lexicon (lord, knight, esquire, gentleman, yeoman, serving-man) is the statute's real payload — every fiction with a rank system needs this vocabulary written out, because the law is where the language pins it down (couples sociolinguistics-registers.md).

## 7. Casta: dress as evidence, not license — colonial Spanish America

- **Calibration first**: there was no tidy legal dress ladder for castas. "Casta" categories in paintings were an elite art genre's labels; lived identity was **calidad** — fluid, reputation-based, read from dress, occupation, language, and honor (Katzew 2004; Carrera 2003; Martínez 2008; Rappaport 2014). The rigid-pyramid version of the casta system is a retrospective overreading; treat it as such.
- **What *was* regulated**: repeated royal orders restricted indigenous people from wearing "Castilian clothing" and from carrying the Spanish elite package — arms and horses above all — keeping tribute-paying Indians visibly Indian (the *Recopilación* (1680) has no dedicated dress title: its related laws sit in Libro VI, título 1, "De los indios," leyes XXXI/XXXIII, on weapons and horses, while dress provisions are scattered cédulas. Indigenous dress litigation is documented in 505 petitions from 277 towns, 1575–1693, Archivo General de la Nación — Schroer 2022). Indigenous nobles (caciques) kept Spanish dress and the mounted elite package (horses, swords) as privilege. And Spanish court prestige ran on **black** — expensive dye chemistry, princely restraint (Pastoureau 2009) — a case where the status color is "absence."
- **The deep layer**: Mexica sumptuary law reserved cotton for nobles, dressed commoners in maguey fiber, graded cloaks, sandals, and jewelry by rank (Clendinnen 1991); the death penalty for a commoner in cotton or in an over-long mantle is documented in the "Laws of Motecuhzoma Ilhuicamina" (Durán, trans. Heyden 1994, ch. 25, 207–209; Clendinnen 1991, 41). Colonial law partly overlaid, partly perpetuated this fiber politics.
- **The paradox worth stealing**: casta paintings dress their "mixed" subjects in fashionable imported silks — display outruns the taxonomy the panels claim to certify (Katzew). The picture classifies; the clothing refuses.
- **Craft note**: use the dress-as-evidence mode when your state lacks reach to license clothing. Then costume is *testimony*: courts, priests, and patrons read your clothes, and the trial is about what your outfit proves. Identity litigation replaces the inspector.

## 8. The language of regulation — a drafting-pattern field guide

1. **The degree formula** (permissive-by-exclusion): "no person under the degree of X shall wear Y" — hierarchy encoded as descending permission; the degree list is the payload.
2. **The monopoly clause**: "none but the emperor may wear..." — color/fiber reserved, breach edging toward treason.
3. **The assay clause**: the ban names a measurable property — stripe width, cloth price, dye grade, badge, fur provenance. Unassayable bans die; assayable ones run for centuries (compare §3/§4 with §6).
4. **The forfeiture-plus-informer clause**: "the garment shall be forfeited, part to the informer" — crowd-sourced enforcement, and a blackmail economy (Baldwin 1926; Hunt 1996).
5. **The occasion exemption**: "except on the day of her wedding" — Italian statutes licensed the trousseau ritually; display allowed, but only in ritual time (couples ritual-liminality.md).
6. **The women-through-men clause**: "wives of X may wear Y" — grammatically pins women to male degree; a legal-grammar artifact with plot consequences (§6).
7. **The intent preamble**: "whereas outrageous and excessive apparel..." — the moral frame; protective, hierarchical, or disciplinary motive is readable from it, and often the preamble contradicts the schedule.
8. **The renewal**: the same ban reissued across reigns/decades (England, Edo) — in-world, a ruler's repeated edict is a chronic weakness marker; historians of your world should read reenactment as data.
9. **The bureaucratic performative** (Chinese register): article-openers "in all cases of..." (諸), "unlawfully assumes," graduated punishments rod→exile; code voice assumes an inspectorate, not a mob.
10. **The marking command** (ecclesiastical register): Lateran IV c.68's "distinguish themselves by their dress" — sumptuary logic aimed at *othering*, not luxury; pairs with badge systems as its secular cousin.

## 9. Calibration — contested and oversimplified readings

- **"Tokugawa four-class system"** — schoolbook shorthand; real mibun order more complex and locally variable (Howell 2005). Use edicts and leaks, not the pyramid.
- **"Casta system as rigid racial law"** — overshoot; calidad was fluid and situational, and casta paintings are genre images, not statutes (Katzew; Carrera; Martínez; Rappaport).
- **"Sumptuary law = Puritan moralism"** — the texts themselves are mostly protectionist, fiscal, and hierarchical; morals supply the preamble, the schedule does the governing (Hunt 1996).
- **"Purple is a hue"** — in Rome it is a dye grade with a price schedule (Reinhold; Edict of Diocletian); legal purple is assayed, not seen. Same caution for Ming "yellow."
- **"Yellow was always the emperor's color in China"** — exclusivity matured over the Tang and later (the usual anchor is Gaozong's 668 (Zongzhang 1) edict barring officials and commoners from yellow, after an earlier Wude-era reservation of the imperial red-yellow); do not project Ming monopoly back to antiquity.
- **"Sumptuary law always failed"** — assayable-sign regimes (Tang/Ming) were durable administration; failure is characteristic of price-graded regimes facing market fashion (§1).

## 10. Applying it to a fictional culture — build sequence

1. **Pick the scarce axis** (a dye molecule, a fiber, metallic thread, a guild monopoly on fulling). The law regulates scarcity; everything else is theater.
2. **Choose the assay**: measurable (width, weight, price) beats visible (color) beats testimonial (who says he saw it). If nothing is assayable, the law only exists at trial — the casta mode.
3. **Draft the statute in degree formula** and generate the degree lexicon as a real table (title × gender × office). This is the kotodama work: the law is where the language's status terms get pinned down.
4. **Build the enforcement economy**: who profits from informing? Forfeiture splits buy you a surveillance class of tailors, dyers, and secondhand-clothes dealers. Their guild politics is your next chapter.
5. **Write the loophole dialect in advance**: each ban spawns a technique — linings, detachable trains, look-alike weaves, leased garments, wedding-day exemptions. Show the patch notes; never show a law that worked as intended.
6. **Plant one fossil law**: an archaic ban on a now-common material (elders remember when it was rare) — instant era texture and a dating tool for your world's history.
7. **Choose the enforcement mode**: license mode (inspectorate, Tang/Edo flavor — dress as pass) or evidence mode (courtroom, casta flavor — dress as testimony). Mixed regimes produce the best trials.
8. **Handoff to lore-shokunin**: deliver four artifacts — the code table (who may wear what), the assay, the sanction schedule, and the black-market price spread between the legal floor and what people actually wear. That spread is where the fiction's pulse is.
