## 1. Frame: war gear is a text with three layers

- Armor does three jobs at once: **stop blades** (materials engineering), **identify** (who is on my side, and who commands), and **speak** (rank, lineage, patron deity, era). Worldbuilders usually write only the first layer; the other two are where the kotodama lane lives, because identification and speech are *language problems wearing metal*.
- Design order mirrors the corpus: pick the **construction system** first (§2–3 — it fixes weight, cost, who can afford it), then the **identification stack** (§4 — it fixes battlefield legibility and administration), then the **nomenclature and ritual layer** (§5–6 — it fixes what the gear *says*). A culture's war gear is legible to its enemies too: heraldry is a shared grammar, which is why borderlands spawn counterfeit arms and false colors — a plot engine.

## 2. Construction typology: five systems and the hybrids

| System | Structure | Weight (approx.) | Real exemplars | Fiction hook |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Lamellar | small plates laced to each other into rows, then rows laced together; no backing garment needed | 10–30 kg whole harness by era | Japanese kozane armor (hon-kozane rows); Byzantine klibanion; steppe lamellar (Mongol) | lacing rots → armor maintenance economies; each culture's lacing pattern is a fingerprint |
| Laminar | horizontal strips/hoops laced or riveted, each overlapping the one below (big strips, not small plates) | varies | Roman lorica segmentata (name is post-antique, §7); Nanbokuchō-era big-plate dō | a "modernized" laminar cuirass reads as an arms-race era stamp |
| Scale | plates sewn to a backing garment, overlapping upward like shingles | light–medium | lorica squamata; Scythian/steppe scale; Japanese kawara-scale inserts | sewn-to-cloth = tailors repair it, not smiths; class split between fitters |
| Mail | interlocked riveted/punched rings; drapes and cuts slash, blunts thrusts poorly | hauberk ~10 kg | Celtic Europe (earliest finds 3rd c. BCE) → lorica hamata → European hauberk → Islamic and Indian zirah | the export-proof technology: adoption across Eurasia and Africa; mail *noise* as battle prose ("ring-mail" in sagas) |
| Rigid plate | large shaped sheets, articulated with rivets/straps; peak craft c. 1420–1560 | field harness ~20–25 kg distributed (Williams 2003) | "white harness" c. 1420; Milanese Missaglia and Augsburg Helmschmied schools; Greenwich armor; munitions "Almain rivet" exports | proof-testing: armor sold "of proof" with pistol-shot dent left as certification mark — metallurgy as receipt |
| Hybrids | coat-of-plates/brigandine (plates riveted *inside* cloth), jack-of-plate, Japanese kikko hexagonal-plate sewn forms | cheap and common | 13th-c. coat-of-plates; Iberian brigandine worn by kings and archers alike | the *democratic* armor: the full-status spectrum from brigandine to plate is a wardrobe of rank |

- The substrate is always textile: gambeson/padded jackets under and often *instead of* metal. A worldbuilder who forgets padding economies (linen/flock supply, who quilts, who launders field jackets) forgets the army's largest textile industry.
- Calibration: figures above are order-of-magnitude from the standard literature (Williams 2003; Blair 1958; Bottomley & Hopson 1988); surviving-harness weights vary widely by set and reconstruction. No rigorous head-to-head test program exists for Japanese vs European armor — anyone claiming a verdict ("samurai steel beats plate") is past the evidence (§7).

## 3. The Japanese lineage: one system, four eras

| Era | Form | Worn by | Diagnostic features |
|---|---|---|---|
| Kofun–Nara (pre-10th c.) | tankō (lame plate cuirass), keikō (lamellar) | continental-style infantry | direct Chinese/Korean borrowing; the import generation |
| Heian–Kamakura (10th–14th c.) | **ō-yoroi** "great armor" | elite mounted archers | box-shaped lamellar cuirass with right-side opening shielded by the waki-itate panel; huge flat sode shoulder shields tied by kawa-haide cords; tsurubashiri smooth chest panel so the bowstring glides; agemaki bowknot at the back; kusazuri skirt-plates; total weight ~30 kg (estimates vary) |
| Kamakura–Nanbokuchō (13th–14th c.) | **dō-maru**, **haramaki** | attendants, then everyone as armies grow | dō-maru wraps and fastens at the right side; haramaki is a belly-wrap closing at the *back* — originally the cheaper, informal cut, later battle-standard as infantry scales |
| Sengoku (15th–16th c.) | **tosei-gusoku** "modern armor," tatami-do, nanban-dō | mass ashigaru + daimyo | folding/rollable armor; import European cuirasses recut into nanban-dō; yukinoshita-dō (Sendai school, hinged horizontal plates); russet-iron and black-lacquer finishes; mon now stamped everywhere |
| Edo (17th–19th c.) | parade armor | status display | bright edomono vs austere katajiro collecting categories; armor as heirloom and theater; ends with the 1876 Haitōrei |

- **Lacing is the status surface.** Odoshi-ge cords bind true small plates (hon-kozane — expensive, dense labor) vs kiritsuke-kozane (wide plates *painted* to imitate lacing — the veneer option). Armors are *named* by lacing scheme — "Akaito-odoshi" (red-thread-laced) — and surviving named heirlooms cluster in shrine treasuries (Ōyamazumi-jinja, Kasuga Taisha). Lacing also *fails*: rain, rot, and worn cords were the maintenance bill of lamellar armies.
- **Kabuto nomenclature is a typology you can copy**: hoshi-kabuto (protruding rivets), suji-kabuto (raised ribs), harikake (lacquered papier-mâché over armature — parade lightness), tatami-kabuto (folding), and the Sengoku burst of kawari-kabuto "eccentric helmets" (eboshi-cap shapes, momonari "peach" bowls, huge maedate front crests: kuwagata antler-mounts, Takeda's Fudō Myōō face (attribution debated), Date Masamune's gilt crescent). The neck-guard is shikoro; the folded turn-backs (fukigaeshi) carry the mon — a helmet that signs itself.
- Apply to fiction: give your armor tradition a *craft lineage* (schools, master-signatures, dated styles), a *lacing/veneer economy* (real vs fake construction as class marker), and a *shrine/depository trail* so named heirlooms have provenance like swords (§5).

## 4. Identification: heraldry as grammar, the sengoku stack as signage

- **European heraldry is a written language with a grammar.** Arms are hereditary pictorial property; **blazon** is the closed register that generates them — tinctures (metals or/argent; colours gules/azure/sable/purpure; furs ermine/vair), fixed attitudes (passant, rampant), and the rule of no metal-on-metal or colour-on-colour (furs and the arms of Jerusalem — gold on white — breach it famously; Fox-Davies 1909). Armorial seals spread from the 1130s; the Le Mans enamel of Geoffrey Plantagenet (d. 1151) is the earliest surviving depiction of arms borne in person (Wagner 1956). The **surcoat** worn over mail is literally where "coat of arms" comes from.
- The European stack splits by law, not battle: **arms** (hereditary, court-registrable), **badges/cognizances** (personal-political: Richard III's white boar, Edward IV's suns), **liveries** (colored cloth and collars distributed to followers — allegiance worn as uniform), and **crests** (helm-top marks, tournament-centered). Degradation works the stack in reverse: Sir Andrew Harcla (1323) had his spurs hacked from his heels, sword and belt broken, arms reversed — knighthood un-installed as ceremony (attested in 14th-c. chronicles; formula details vary by account).
- **The sengoku stack is signage, not law.** Japan never built a blazon court; it built a *flag system* (Turnbull 2002; Kyūan 1632):

| Layer | Item | Carrier | Function |
|---|---|---|---|
| Personal device | **mon** (crest) | helmet fukigaeshi, chest/back of dō, jingasa | identity, but *loose*: several unrelated houses share mons, lords grant their mon to retainers, individuals bear multiple mons at once |
| Unit cloth | **nobori** (vertical banners) | whole companies | mass legibility of a thousand-man unit; the battlefield's Typography |
| Individual | **sashimono** (small back-banner) | ashigaru on foot, some riders | person-level ID inside the unit |
| Command | **uma-jirushi** / ō-uma-jirushi (horse insignia) | daimyo HQ | the headquarters is wherever the great insignia is — a *command location beacon* cataloged in Kyūan's 1632 compendium |
| Messenger | **horo** (framed cloak) | tsukai-ban couriers | marks the courier as protected person (its arrow-deflecting fame is folkloric — flag it) |
| Voice | command fan (gunpai), baton (saihai), conch (horagai), drums; night challenge-and-response passwords (**aikotoba** 合言葉, "match-word" — the challenge-and-response is the exchange form, not the attested term) | officers | audible/verbal layer of the stack |
| Color policy | unit colors, e.g. the Ii "akazonae" red-armored tradition (from Takeda style) | formations | color as brand (couple myth-symbol/color-symbolism) |

- Comparative lesson: Europe = *one hereditary pictorial code, grammatically strict*; Japan = *proliferating field signage, legally loose*. Both are real equilibria. A fictional culture should pick its position on that axis and then discover its failure modes: Europe's failure is usurpation and counterfeit arms (hence heralds' courts); Japan's is misidentification and decapitation of command — kill the great insignia-bearer and the army is blind.
- **Honest legibility:** Conlan (2003) shows 14th-c. Japanese warriors carried little fixed insignia at all — identification by face, armor quality, and shout; the banner-saturated field is a Sengoku development. Battlefields are *less* legible than movies show: friendly fire, false colors, and mud-smothered devices are the attested norm, and a world that admits this writes better fog-of-war.

## 5. Weapon nomenclature: names, signatures, and the gift economy of blades

- **Japan industrialized blade biography.** Smiths sign the tang (nakago) — the **mei** is authorship law, and unsigned (mumei) blades are *attributed* by appraisers. Signature placement follows wear mode: a **tachi** (worn edge-down) and an **uchigatana** (worn edge-up, the "katana" configuration) put the mei on opposite tang sides — the blade's *script* encodes its *dress code*, the single best kotodama datum in this file. The **gokaden** (five traditions: Yamashiro, Yamato, Bizen, Sōshū, Mino) are smith lineages functioning like dialect areas (Robinson 1961; Kapp et al. 1987, 2002).
- **Famous blades are named entities with provenance.** The Edo canon of the **tenka-goken** ("five swords under heaven") — Dōjigiri Yasutsuna, Mikazuki Munechika, Ōdenta Mitsuyo, Onimaru Kunitsuna, Juzumaru Tsunetsugu — reads like a registry of relics. Blades *accumulate* names: the Minamoto heirloom Higekiri ("Beard-cutter") carries a chain of renamings after its kills (Tomokiri → Higekiri → Onikiri, in Heike-period narrative sources — semi-legendary, flag it); a sword's name is its curriculum vitae, and renaming it is a court-of-record act.
- **Connoisseurship is institutional:** Edo appraisal ran on oshigata (ink-rubbings of tangs), hakogaki (box inscriptions certifying attribution, the Hon'ami appraiser lineage's instrument), cutting-test records — saidan-mei inscribed on tangs ("cut through two bodies"), certified by the Yamada Asaemon tester office — and registers like the Kyōhō meibutsu-chō tracking famous objects through owner after owner. Your world needs this: *an appraisal guild, a file format, and a register* — that's what turns weapons into named lore.
- **Europe named swords but federalized them into story and regalia.** Literary: Joyeuse, Durendal, Excalibur (chansons de geste and chronicle romance). Institutional: Curtana, the coronation "Sword of Mercy" with blunted point (attested in English regalia from the 13th c.; its edifying legend is later), and the French coronation sword still called "Joyeuse" (a real 13th-c. artifact wearing a legendary identity). Modern scholarship rebuilt nomenclature as *typology*: Oakeshott's blade types (X–XXII) and Petersen's Viking hilt letters — invented, stable, citable names. Do the same in-world: give your smiths' guild a typology and its Oakeshott.
- **Sword-giving rituals encode majority, office, and alliance:**

| Rite | Culture | Mechanic | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arms-taking | Germanic | receiving shield/spear (sword later) = full legal person, majority rite | Tacitus, Germania 13 |
| Accolade/dubbing | Latin Europe | belt and sword given by lord; flat-of-sword blow is the later normalized image | Wagner 1956; Blair 1958 |
| Genpuku | Japan | boy receives first real sword **and adult name** in one rite — name and blade change together | standard rite-of-passage literature (Friday 2004) |
| Kenpei presentations | Japan | famous swords offered to emperor, shogun, shrines; meibutsu as diplomatic gift | Kyōhō meibutsu-chō; Robinson 1961 |
| Katanagari | Japan (1588) | Hideyoshi's disarmament edict strips peasants of weapons — who may *own* a blade becomes state law | Tenshō 16 edict |
| Daishō convention → Haitōrei | Tokugawa Japan | the paired big-small swords as shizoku status wear → abolished by the 1876 Sword Abolition Edict | Haitōrei, 1876 |

- The arc is the point: **sword = person = status**, from Germanic majority to Japanese two-sword badge to its 1876 abolition. Disarmament edicts are era stamps sharper than any battle date; couple era/.

## 6. How gear encodes rank and myth

- **Sumptuary law is rank written on the body**: England's Statute of Apparel (1363) assigns cloth grades to classes; Edo codes regulate fabric, colors, and ornament by status; the daishō convention marks the sword-wearing estate. The rule for fiction: *every sumptuary law creates a black market and a sumptuary trial as plot*.
- **Myth sits where the body meets death** — helmet, chest, and the flag above the head: maedate with Fudō Myōō or the crescent; crosses of crusading orders; bonji (Siddham seed-syllables) lacquered onto armor surfaces as apotropaic *worn speech*; the Sanada **Rokumonsen** (six coins = fare for the river of the dead) — the funerary reading is the popular interpretation and the clan's *intent* is unproven (flag it). Myth-crests work because they are legible *at a distance* — theology compressed to a glyph.
- **The anti-rite is as codified as the rite**: spurs hacked, sword and belt broken, arms reversed (Harcla, 1323); the post-1868 stripping of samurai rank via sword ban. To degrade is to un-write the person's gear-language in public — the negative space proves the language existed.
- Apply to fiction: pick 3–5 myth-motifs and assign each a *carrier* (helm crest, chest plate, back-flag, blade inscription) and a *legibility distance* (divine at 100 paces, secret at arm's length); then write who is allowed to mount which motif where, and what the counterfeit version is.

## 7. Calibration: the pop-framework shelf (do not ship these to the bible)

- **"Studded leather armor"** as a medieval standard is a fantasy-gaming/Victorian invention; documented leather armors are cuir bouilli (boiled-leather cuirasses), the jack-of-plate, and Japanese leather/kusari inserts. Blair (1958) catalogs what actually existed.
- **D&D armor taxonomy** — "banded mail," "splint mail," "chainmail" — is largely gaming vocabulary, not medieval: mail *is* chain armor (the word suffices); "banded mail" has no real attestation; real splint defenses exist (Japanese splinted limb armor, Eastern European splints) but never as the game's category. Oakeshott/Blair typologies are the citable replacements.
- **Katana myths:** folding a blade compensates for heterogeneous bloomery steel; it does not create magic. "Folded a thousand times" (folded N times doubles layers exponentially, not linearly), "katana cuts machine-gun barrels," and the Muramasa curse / Masamune-and-leaves contest are Edo-period kaidan and later moral tales — folklore, not metallurgy; Tokugawa dread of Muramasa blades is largely retrospective legend (flag as folklore in any use).
- **Armor immobility myths:** well-fitted plate harness (~20–25 kg, spread over the body) allowed running and mounting (Williams 2003; Capwell 2015's mounted work in the English harness tradition); "knights crane-lifted onto horses" is cinema. Agincourt's mud was real but situational.
- **"Lorica segmentata"** is a post-antique naming convention, not what Romans called it (Bishop & Coulston 2006). Antique names are usually lost; be honest about which names are ancient and which are scholarly.
- **Nagashino (1575) "three-rank volley fire"**: the tidy drill image is a later standardization — the rotating-volley account is absent from 『信長公記』 and enters via the Edo-period 『甫庵信長記』, canonized as fact by Meiji-era textbooks; the degree of coordinated volleys at Nagashino is contested (Fujimoto 1980; Nawa 1998; Hirayama 2014) — do not use it as a proof-point for Japanese gun doctrine without hedging.
- **Bushidō as eternal samurai code** — including "the sword is the soul of the samurai" — is substantially a Meiji-era and later construction; Nitobe (1899) packaged it for Western readers, Benesch (2014) documents the invention. Use in-world bushidō ideology as a *dated ideology*, not as ancient ethnography.
- **Samurai-vs-knight comparisons** in both directions (superior/reduced) outrun the evidence: Williams's penetration tests are European-only; there is no controlled comparative program. If a bible text needs a verdict, it needs a canon decision, not a citation.

## 8. Kotodama hooks: the word-spirit layer

- **The signature rule that reads the wearer** (couple writing-systems): blade script flips with dress code (tachi-mei vs katana-mei). Extend it: in-world blades whose *inscription placement* tells you how they were carried, and court cases decided by reading a tang.
- **Name-as-CV** (couple naming-systems): blades renamed at each great deed, with the renaming witnessed and recorded; duels fought over the *right to rename*; a sword refuse to answer to an unworthy name.
- **Worn speech** (couple writing-systems + ritual-liminality): bonji seed-syllables and sutra invocations lacquered on armor — apotropaic grammar on the body; a helmet inscription that must be renewed or re-inked before campaign.
- **Closed registers** (couple sociolinguistics-registers): blazon and mon as constrained generative codes (the sibling file animal-symbolism §7 already fields blazon-as-sociolect); add the camp password register — at night, the wrong answer to "who goes there" is lethal, so give passwords their own politeness grammar and a code-switch for friendly vs hostile challenge.
- **Performative renaming of people** (couple ritual-liminality): genpuku pairs the first sword with the adult name — write one rite that issues both, so that *being armed* and *being named* are the same legal event.
- **Slogan biography** (couple semantics-pragmatics): "the sword is the soul" is a modern export; build the in-world slogan and then write *its* biography — who coined it, which reform school adopted it, which edict it was printed under. A motto with a birthdate is calibration built into canon.
- **Contact etymologies** (couple language-contact via linguistics/): weapons named for arrival points (tanegashima after the island of first Portuguese contact, 1543) — name the foreign weapon after the port, and the name fossilizes first contact.

## 9. Build order for a fictional culture

1. Choose the **construction system** (§2) and derive its cost curve: who wears what at age 20, what the maintenance economy (lacing! cloth! smiths!) looks like in peace.
2. Place it on the **lineage table** (§3): name the ancestor form, the modernization crisis, and the parade/degeneration form; stamp each with era dates.
3. Pick the **identification equilibrium** (§4): strict hereditary code (Europe-side) or proliferating field signage (Japan-side) — then write its failure mode (usurpation courts vs command-decapitation) and its honest fog (Conlan-style misidentification).
4. Build the **identification stack** as a table like §4's: one item per layer (personal, unit, individual, command, messenger, voice, color), each with a carrier garment and a legibility distance.
5. Institute the **appraisal complex** (§5): smith signatures, attribution guild, rubbings/registries, cutting-test or proof-test records, and a register of famous objects with owner chains.
6. Write the **name law**: blade naming, renaming-on-deed, the signature/wear-direction rule, and one regalia sword wearing a legendary identity (the Curtana move).
7. Attach the **rites**: majority arms-giving (§5 table), the paired sword+name threshold, the presentation gift, and the degradation anti-rite.
8. Load **myth onto death-facing surfaces** (§6) with 3–5 motifs, assigned carriers, and a counterfeit economy; then run sumptuary law over the whole wardrobe.
9. Audit against §7 before handoff; every folklore-derived flourish must enter the bible labeled as folklore-with-a-date.

## 10. Canon boundary

Reference knowledge and method only; which weapons a fictional culture sanctifies, which crest a house may bear, and which disarmament law ends its era are lore-shokunin's canon decisions. Hand off: the construction typology (§2), the lineage table (§3), the identification-stack template (§4), the name-and-appraisal complex (§5), the rite/degradation pair (§5–6), and the calibration shelf (§7) as options with flags attached. Nothing on §7's shelf is load-bearing for canon.
