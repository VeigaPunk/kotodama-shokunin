## 1. Word order: six orders, their companions, and their limits

- Basic constituent order is the most reader-visible typological choice. WALS 2013 counts (Dryer 2013, 81A, sample of ~1,377 languages with a dominant order): SOV ≈ 41%, SVO ≈ 35%, VSO ≈ 7%, VOS ≈ 2%, OVS < 1% (Hixkaryana is the textbook case, Derbyshire's analyses; Warao claims are contested), OSV essentially unattested as dominant; ~14% of languages have no dominant order. Treat proportions, not laws — counts shift with the sample.
- Greenberg's 1963 universals are statistical correlations, not laws. The load-bearing ones for worldbuilding:
  - U1: subject precedes object in almost every dominant order (all six alternatives exist, but SOV+SVO dominate).
  - U2: preposition ↔ genitive follows head noun; postposition ↔ genitive precedes.
  - U3: VSO languages are (nearly) always prepositional.
  - U5: SOV languages have genitive-before-noun almost without exception.
  - U17: VSO languages have noun-adjective order overwhelmingly.
  - Adjective position is the weakest correlation (Gen-U17 tension exists); numeral position and adposition position are stronger.
- "Harmonic" languages order heads and dependents consistently (VO+prep+N-G+N-Adj); "disharmonic" mixtures (German: SVO with postposition-compatible NP order; Persian) are common and fine — but give a fictional disharmony a history (contact, substratum, documented change), because real disharmony usually has one.
- Trade-off that matters for prose: free word order languages are usually case-marking languages (Latin, Russian, Japanese); fixed-order languages lose case (English, Mandarin). A language cannot usually have both total word-order freedom and zero morphology (Comrie 1989).
- Calibration: Greenberg's sample was 30 languages, non-random; Dryer 1992 refined the correlations (order tendencies attach to individual dependent types, not whole "OV/VO packages"); Dunn et al. 2011 showed in Nature that word-order correlations are largely lineage-specific (family inheritance beats universal pressure in their Indo-European/Austronesian/Bantu/Uto-Aztecan samples). So: tendencies good for texture, not "laws of nature" a character could discover.
- Apply: pick one dominant order, then run the harmonic sweep below; word order alone sets prose texture — verb-final suits suspense and legal/technical build-ups (the payoff lands last), verb-initial suits chronicle/report register, object-initial is nearly alien and demands a stated discourse rule (e.g., "the affected party is named first" as a legal-cultural maxim).
- Exemplar table (all rows are attested real languages; use as plausibility anchors, not as templates to copy whole):

| order | attested exemplars | usual companions | register/texture notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| SOV | Japanese, Turkish, Korean, Hindi-Urdu, Tamil, Quechua, Navajo, Amharic, Classical Latin prose | postpositions, genitive-noun, rich case, verb-final auxiliaries | closure lands last; epigrammatic endings; chainable nominalizations |
| SVO | English, Mandarin, Indonesian, Swahili, Vietnamese, colloquial Arabic | prepositions, noun-genitive (tendency), little case | "transparent" baseline for readers; drifts toward fixed order |
| VSO | Irish, Welsh, Classical Arabic, Māori, Tongan, Biblical Hebrew | prepositions, rich agreement, particles | chronicle/report register; verb-first gives each clause a headline |
| VOS | Malagasy, Mayan languages (Tzotzil, Ixil), Nias, Fijian | prepositions, voice/trigger systems | afterthought feel; usually needs a discourse trigger for the fronted verb |
| OVS | Hixkaryana (Carib, Brazil — Derbyshire's analyses); Warao claims contested | heavy case marking; discourse-driven fronting | affected-party-first ideology; alienating by design |
| OSV | no well-documented dominant case | — | avoid without explicit motivation; flag any use as exotic |

## 2. Alignment: who counts as the same person

- Three argument roles: S (intransitive subject: "she slept"), A (transitive agent: "she cut it"), P (patient: "it got cut"). Alignment decides which roles share marking — the single most consequential grammar choice after word order.
- Core systems:
  - Nominative-accusative: S=A (nominative), P distinct (accusative). Latin, Russian, Turkish, Japanese, Quechua. The statistical majority; English retains a fossil of it (I/me, he/him).
  - Ergative-absolutive: S=P (absolutive), A distinct (ergative). Basque, Inuktitut, Dyirbal, Sumerian, Abkhaz, Mayan languages. Ergativity correlates with nothing about cultural "complexity" — see §5.
  - Tripartite: S, A, P all distinct. Rare; attested in some Australian languages (e.g., Yingkarta, Wangkumara — Dixon 1994). Reads as maximal formalism; fits ritual-legal registers.
  - Split-S / fluid-S (active-stative): intransitive subjects split by volition (Lakhota, Guaraní, Choctaw; Merlan 1985). In fluid-S systems the speaker *chooses* per utterance ("she fell [unwittingly]" vs "[deliberately]") — grammar itself becomes testimony about intent; ideal for cultures where agency is morally charged.
  - Direct-inverse (Algonquian obviation, Plains Cree; cf. Mapudungun): a person hierarchy (1/2 > 3 > obviative) ranks participants; "inverse" marking flips the expected role without changing case. Bakes a social/animacy hierarchy into every clause.
  - Austronesian / Philippine symmetrical voice: the "trigger" (topic) is agreed with by the verb and the voice affix selects it; Actor and Patient voice are roughly co-equal (Tagalog, Malagasy VOS, robust in Formosan languages; reduced two-voice system in Indonesian). Schachter 1976 debated whether the trigger is subject or topic; Riesberg 2014 shows symmetricality is a continuum — western Austronesian systems are less symmetric than Formosan ones. Good fit for consensus/court cultures where "who the clause is about" is negotiable.
- Splits are where the realism lives (Dixon 1994):
  - Aspect split: Hindi-Urdu, Georgian, Tibetan mark ergative only in perfective/past — grammar remembers completed actions as agent-patient transactions.
  - Person split (Silverstein 1976 hierarchy: 1st/2nd > 3rd > proper name > human > animate > inanimate): ergative marking starts low on the hierarchy; Hindi-Urdu 1st/2nd pronouns drop the ergative *ne* in the perfective. A world can grade alignment by species, caste, or divine rank via this same hierarchy.
  - Discourse basis: Du Bois 1987 (Sacapultec Maya) — A arguments are overwhelmingly new lexical NPs, S/P overwhelmingly given; ergative alignment mirrors information flow. Hopper & Thompson 1980's transitivity parameters (volition, agency, affectedness…) explain why high-transitivity clauses get more overt marking.
- Syntactic vs morphological ergativity (Dixon 1994): Dyirbal is ergative not just in case but in its ellipsis pivot (omitted argument tracks S/P); English's pivot tracks S/A. This changes how narratives chain actions — an ergative-pivot language writes "he cut it and fell" with a different referent sustained than an accusative language would. Use pivot choice to control who stays "on stage" through a scene.
- Apply: alignment is invisible to readers until the in-world text is quoted — then it becomes the strongest authenticity signal. An ergative culture's idioms will lean patient-ward ("it was suffered", "under the blade it went"); a fluid-S culture's legal formulas will interrogate volition; a symmetrical-voice culture's etiquette will ask "who is this clause about?" before "who did it?".

## 3. Relative clauses and embedding

- Keenan & Comrie 1977, Noun Phrase Accessibility Hierarchy: SU > DO > IO > OBL > GEN > OCOMP. It predicts (and roughly predicts acquisition and processing order) which positions a language can relativize; empirically, languages relativize a continuous initial segment of the hierarchy. A fictional language that relativizes only subjects, or everything including comparatives, sits at real points on this scale.
- Strategy table (Andrews 2007):
  - Postnominal gap (English, French): flexible, prosaic; supports long relative chains.
  - Prenominal gap (Japanese, Korean, Turkish, Chinese): whole clause before the noun; favors compact attributive strings, tends to restrict relativization to high hierarchy positions; gives prose a "left-branching" breathlessness.
  - Correlative (Hindi-Urdu *jo…so*, Sanskrit *yat…tat*; Lipták 2009): paired parallel clauses — inherently epic, gnomic, proverb-friendly; a correlative language's wisdom literature writes itself in couplets.
  - Internally-headed (Navajo, Lakhota — Williamson 1987; an analysis of Japanese also exists): the head noun sits inside the relative clause; produces a "this very thing that just happened" immediacy — strong fit for oral performance and testimony.
  - Circumnominal / gapped-with-case (Basque, Turkish): the noun is wrapped; formal, solemn register.
  - Pronoun-retention / resumptive (colloquial Hebrew, Arabic): forgiving of heavy embedding; suits spoken registers and low-literacy worlds.
- Center-embedding ceiling: Karlsson 2007 (*Journal of Linguistics* 43) — even in edited written corpora, sentences with two levels of center-embedding are rare and three are effectively unattested. Any in-world prose should respect this or be stylized as explicitly artificial (spells, divine speech, contract boilerplate).
- Cross-clause tracking, high value for oral cultures:
  - Switch-reference (Stirling 1993): verbs mark whether the next clause has the same or different subject — Muskogean, Kiowa-Tanoan, Panoan, Papuan, some Australian. Epic chant with SS/DS marking makes every scene transition an explicit hand-off.
  - Logophoric pronouns (Culy 1994; Donno So/Dogon, Ewe, Gokana): a dedicated pronoun for "the person whose speech or thought is being reported" — disambiguates reported speech elegantly; a culture with logophors polishes its art of quotation and gossip to a legal sheen.
- Apply: choose the relative-clause strategy with the *genre* of the culture, not at random: correlative for liturgical/epic; prenominal for bureaucratic compactness; internally-headed for testimony-driven (evidential, see §4) cultures. Pair with the word order: prenominal RCs are at home in OV languages, postnominal in VO.

## 4. What syntax betrays about culture

- Evidentials — grammar that forces speakers to say *how they know*:
  - Willett 1988's distribution: the recurrent split is direct vs indirect, with indirect dividing into reported (hearsay) vs inferred; some languages add quotative, assumed, or secondhand.
  - Aikhenvald 2004 (and Aikhenvald & Dixon 2003): grammatical evidential systems run 2–5 terms; Tariana has five (visual, nonvisual, inferred, assumed, reported); Turkish contrasts witnessed -DI with hearsay/inferred -mIş; Cuzco Quechua -mi/-s(i)/-chá (direct/report/conjecture — Faller 2002). Fleck 2007 documents Matses, where evidential choice is so fine-grained (including temporal distance of the evidence) that a wrong choice is treated as lying.
  - Sizing table (system size vs. what it does to narrative):

| system | real model | what the culture's information economy looks like |
|---|---|---|
| none (lexical only) | English, Mandarin | attribution optional, stylistic; "according to X" hedges; deniability easy |
| 2-term witnessed/hearsay | Turkish -DI / -mIş | gossip and journalism grammaticalized; hearsay marked even in formal writing |
| 3-term + inference | Cuzco Quechua -mi/-si/-chá (Faller 2002) | deduction is a marked speech act; conjecture visibly separated from report |
| 5-term | Tariana; Tuyuca (Aikhenvald 2004) | source-tracking civilization; misuse invites accusations of lying; expertise = knowing how you know |
| evidential × tense | Matses (Fleck 2007) | precision economy: *when* the evidence was gathered is as obligatory as *what* was seen |
  - Cultural consequences a worldbuilder can bank on: testimony norms (an evidential language's law courts demand source-marked statements), rumor genres with distinct grammar, prophecy as grammatically marked reported speech, and social sanction for evidential misuse (documented in Aikhenvald's Tariana material). Languages *without* grammatical evidentials still mark evidence — but lexically and optionally, which itself expresses a cultural stance on attribution.
  - Mirativity (DeLancey 1997): grammatical marking of *unexpected* information, often fused with evidentials. A culture's threshold of astonishment is literally in its morphology.
- Social deixis in syntax: Japanese referent honorifics + humble forms (Shibatani 1990), Korean addressee levels and subject/object honorifics (Sohn 1999), Javanese ngoko–krama speech levels (Errington 1988). Grammar encodes rank in every utterance; pair these systems with court costume, caste, or guild hierarchies — and with the alignment hierarchy tricks from §2 (person splits) for a coherent status ecology.
- Information structure: in free-order languages, position codes given-before-new and topic-comment (Prague-school functional sentence perspective; Croft 2003 gives the typological framing). Verb-initial and object-initial orders in fiction should be justified by a discourse rule of this kind, or they read as random.
- Syntax records contact history — linguistic areas (Sprachbund):
  - South Asia (Emeneau 1956; Masica 1976): SOV + postpositions, retroflexes, absolutive converbs, dative-experiencer subjects, echo-words spread across Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, and Munda — unrelated families converging under centuries of shared civilization.
  - Balkans (Joseph 1983): loss of infinitive, postposed article, volitional future, clitic doubling across Greek, Albanian, Romanian, Bulgarian.
  - Mesoamerica (Campbell, Kaufman & Smith-Stark 1986): vigesimal numerals, relational body-part locatives, head-marking patterns shared across Mesoamerican families.
  - Implication: a syntax feature map is a map of old empires, trade roads, and mission zones. Give neighboring fictional families shared "areal" quirks at former imperial seams, and disharmonic exceptions at borders.
- Syntax records time: order and alignment drift. English went from case-rich, OV-leaning Old English to analytic VO (van Gelderen 2006); Li & Thompson 1974 argue for SVO→SOV pressure in Chinese; Persian and Tibetan drifted into aspect-split ergativity. Practical tool: date in-world text strata by their syntax — a classical scripture keeps verb-final order while the modern vernacular has shifted, the way modern English readers feel Shakespeare's syntax as "old".

## 5. Calibration — contested and discredited frameworks (say these plainly)

- Strong linguistic relativity ("language determines thought", pop-Whorf) is discredited. Weak relativity has solid support in space (Levinson 2003) and number (Frank et al. 2008, Pirahã), while color shows strong universal constraints (Berlin & Kay 1969). Boroditsky 2001 (Mandarin time metaphors) failed direct replication attempts (January & Kako 2007). Treat syntax-thought links as plausible at the level of habitual attention, not destiny.
- "Ergative languages are primitive" is 19th-century philology residue and false: ergative languages include Basque (industrial European society), Inuktitut, Sumerian (the earliest writing). No alignment type tracks cultural complexity.
- The "Eskimo snow words" claim is a debunked anecdote (Pullum 1991); the general "no word for X" trope about untranslatability is unreliable in both directions.
- Everett 2005's Pirahã claims (no recursion, no number words) ignited a real controversy; Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues 2009 (*Language* 85) rebutted much of it and Everett 2009 replied there — status: unresolved. Do not build "a language without recursion" lore on this case without flagging the dispute.
- Greenberg's universals: real tendencies, many exceptions, small biased original sample, and (Dunn et al. 2011) lineage effects that make "universal pressures" claims shaky. Use them as plausibility scaffolding, not as in-world natural law.
- Claims that SOV is "the human default" draw on emerging sign languages and homesign (e.g., Nicaraguan Sign Language research); suggestive, interpretively contested — don't assert it flat.
- Known open problem in our own lane: whether "Austronesian alignment" is voice, case, or information-structure (Schachter 1976 vs subjecthood analyses) is still live; worldbuild Philippine-type systems from Riesberg 2014's continuum framing, not from a single-source dogma.

## 6. Fictional-culture consistency checklist

1. Dominant word order chosen + harmonic sweep: adposition side, genitive side, adjective side (weakest link — may deviate), numeral side, adverb positions.
2. Case/agency: if order is free, case marking exists; if morphology is thin, order is rigid. No both-neither language without a stated history.
3. Alignment chosen + any split trigger (aspect, person/hierarchy, clause type, or fluid volition) + ellipsis pivot (who stays on stage when arguments drop).
4. Relative-clause strategy matched to word order and to the culture's dominant genre (epic/liturgical/testimonial/bureaucratic); center-embedding kept within human limits (Karlsson 2007).
5. Evidential system sized to the culture's information economy (2-term gossip world vs 5-term source-tracking civilization; Fleck 2007 as the maximal case); mirativity marking the culture's astonishment threshold.
6. Social deixis: honorific registers aligned with costume/court systems (handoff to costume entries); person hierarchy consistent with alignment splits.
7. Areal layer: 1–2 shared "Sprachbund" quirks with neighboring languages marking past contact; archaic order preserved in scripture/law for era contrast.
