## 1. The Parry–Lord engine: composition by formula and theme

- Parry's formula: "a group of words which is regularly employed under the same metrical conditions to express a given essential idea" (Parry 1928, trans. in The Making of Homeric Verse). Formulas are metrical tools, not memorized "stock phrases": the singer assembles verse in real time — composition in performance (Lord 1960) — from a three-level toolkit: **formula** (line-phrase level) → **theme / type-scene** (multi-line episode) → **story-pattern** (whole plot, e.g. the return song).
- Evidence base: the 1933–35 Yugoslav field recordings. Guslari sang thousands of lines without script and without verbatim repetition — the same song re-performed differs in wording almost line by line while keeping the story, scene order, and diction (Lord 1960). Benchmark: Avdo Medđedović's The Wedding of Smailagić Meho, 12,323 lines in one performance — Lord's candidate for a Homer-class singer.
- Economy and extension (Parry): one essential idea ↔ one formula per metrical slot; new proper names are folded in by analogy. Demonstration: Achilles' epithets fill every hexameter position with near-zero overlap.
- Learning: hearing → scaffolded practice with a master → full performance. Formulas are never memorized as lists; they sediment through use (Lord 1960).
- Foley's addendum, **traditional referentiality** (Foley 1991): a formula's meaning is the tradition, not the occasion — "swift-footed Achilles" is not a report on speed but an index to a whole character-concept. Oral diction is metonymic.

## 2. Meter is mnemonic engineering (comparative data)

| Tradition | Verse form | Language-fit logic | Performer & frame | Scale (approx.) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Greek epic | dactylic hexameter (—UU / ——) | quantitative, needs vowel-length contrast | rhapsōdoi, festival contests | Iliad ≈15,700 lines; Odyssey ≈12,100 |
| Old English | alliterative long line, 2 half-lines, 4 lifts, alliteration on stressed onsets (Sievers 1885 types A–E) | stress accent + consonant clusters | scop at hall | Beowulf 3,182 lines |
| South Slavic | deseterac: 10 syllables, caesura after 4th | syllable-count + fixed break | guslar with gusle, coffeehouse/court | Meho 12,323 lines |
| Finnic | Kalevala meter: trochaic tetrameter, broken lines, parallelism, no end-rhyme (Kuusi 1977) | trochaic stress, open syllables | runo-singer at weddings/rites | Kalevala (compiled by Lönnrot 1835/1849) 22,795 verses |
| Sanskrit epic | anuṣṭubh śloka: 2×16 syllables, caesura | syllable-count | sūta at court/sacrifice | Mahābhārata ≈100,000 ślokas |
| Vedic (Rigveda) | various meters + engineered recitation: pada/krama/jaṭā/ghana vikṛtis (Staal 1961; Howard 1986) | self-checking recitation permutations | ritual specialists | claims of near-zero variance across millennia |
| Arabic | qaṣīda: monorhyme, nasīb–raḥīl–madīḥ movement (Nicholson 1907); rajaz ("the poets' donkey") for improvisation | rhyme-friendly finals | poet at assembly/court | qaṣīda ≈60–100 lines; mu'allaqat canonized |
| Mande | chanted praise-epic | speech-rhythm + instrumental accompaniment (kora, balafon) | jeli (griot), hereditary | Sunjata versions, thousands of lines (Conrad 2004) |
| Old Norse | eddic fornyrðislag (alliterative couplets); skaldic dróttkvætt: 8-line stanza, internal rhyme, dense syntax | alliteration + rhyme tolerance | skald at court; eddic wisdom duels | skaldic corpus indexed verse-by-verse (Skaldic Project) |
| Middle High German | Nibelungenstrophe: 4 long lines, rhymed couplets, feminine ending on each 4th half-line (shared with the Kürenberg stanza) | rhyme | recited/mauzed at court | Nibelungenlied ≈2,370 strophes, 39 âventiuren |

- Why verse at all (Rubin 1995): rhyme and rhythm are retrieval cues; imagery is a content cue; thematic organization (type-scenes) is a structure cue. Serial order is the weak link in human memory, and each meter is a different fix for it. Children's counting rhymes show centuries of near-verbatim stability with drift at the edges; Rubin re-analyzed the Yugoslav epic materials with exactly these tools.
- Do not treat the numbers as precise: line counts are edition-dependent, and "formula density" percentages depend entirely on how a formula is defined (see Calibration).

## 3. Epithets and praise-names: the formula lexicon

- Parry's Homeric tables: for each major figure, a small epithet set distributed across metrical positions (dios Akhilleús / díogenēs Akhilleús / pódas ōkús Akhilleús …), deployed regardless of immediate aptness — "swift-footed" while Achilles sits. Aptness-independence is the tell of traditional (vs ornamental) diction (Parry 1971).
- Independent invention, Japanese: makura-kotoba ("pillow words"), fixed ~5-mora epithets bound to particular nouns — chihayaburu kami "mighty-swift gods", shirotae no tsuki "white-cloth moon" (Miner, Odagiri & Morrell 1985; Keene 1993). A metrical-position system with no Greek-style meter: proof the mechanism is cognitive, not hexameter-specific.
- Praise poetry as social formula: Yoruba oríkì (Barber 1991) and Nguni izibongo (Opland 1983). A person's praise-name is compressed genealogy + history + political claim — the epithet system promoted to a social institution; praise verses are edited and contested as circumstances change.
- Corrections: Hainsworth (1968) — real Homeric diction is more flexible than "economy" predicts (formulas are modified mid-line; economy is a tendency). Benson (1966) — literate poets reproduce formulaic style easily; density proves technique, not orality.
- Build order for a fictional culture: (1) fix the meter; (2) tabulate its metrical slots; (3) assign each major character, lineage, land, and ship a slot-filling epithet set; (4) decide what each epithet asserts (descent, homeland, deed, curse); (5) track violations — the "wrong" epithet at the wrong moment is how tradition shows through, and a ready-made plot device.

## 4. Type-scenes, ring composition, narrative engines

- Type-scene (Arend 1933, typische Szene; Lord's "theme"): a recurring episode with fixed beat-order and variable slots. Core inventory: arming (garments → armor → weapons → mount/chariot, each item named and described), arrival (host sees guest → questioning → hospitality), feast, sacrifice, omen-taking, messenger speech, journey/underworld, lament, duel. Six to twelve cards cover most epic narrative.
- Ring composition: episodes mirror around a center, A B C … C′ B′ A′ (Whitman 1958 for Homer; Leyerle 1967's "interlace" for Beowulf). Memory-friendly architecture: symmetry tells the singer where the story is.
- Ballad technique: incremental repetition — a scene replays with accumulating variation (Child 1882–98); laconic jump-cuts; dialogue carries the plot. The catchphrase "leaping and lingering" describes this style (attribution uncertain — to-verify).
- Rule of three: three attempts, three days, three brothers — the near-universal pacing device; combine with rings for scaffolding.
- In medias res (Horace, Ars Poetica) is the performance default: start mid-action; the audience already owns the backstory, so "flashbacks" are reminders, not reveals.
- Apply: write each type-scene as a card (fixed beats / variable slots / meter landmarks). A standard episode is a card re-instantiated; a "novel" event is a card used off-label — which is exactly how singers generate surprise inside tradition, and how you can too.

## 5. Kennings, heiti, and myth-locked diction

- Kenning anatomy: base-word + determinant in a possessive link, naming a thing by riddle-compression — "whale-road" = sea (OE hronrād, The Seafarer); "Odin's mead" = poetry (Mead-of-Poetry myth, Skáldskaparmál); "Fróði's flour" = gold (the Grotti mill myth); "Hildr's play" = battle. **Heiti** = plain poetic synonyms without the riddle step; **þulur** = canonical synonym lists. Snorri's Skáldskaparmál (c. 1220) is the genre's own textbook and teaches kennings through the myths they encode; Meissner (1921) is the classic typology; the Skaldic Project database gives attested instances.
- Decoder-ring effect: parsing a kenning requires knowing the myth — poetic diction is an in-group credential. Snorri nonetheless insists poetry be "opened", i.e. intelligible to the trained. Design lever: couple every kenning family to a myth file entry, so the register silently indexes the world's mythology.
- Japanese toolkit (convergent invention): makura-kotoba (fixed epithets), kakekotoba (pivot words — one word carries two senses across the line), utamakura (poem-places: place names dense with allusive history) — punning compression rather than riddle compression (Keene 1993).
- Hebrew parallelism (Lowth 1753): synonymous / antithetic / synthetic half-line pairs — the formula device of psalm and proverb verse. Works in any language and is the cheapest oral register device to retrofit into a conlang.
- Finnic parallelism: statement then restatement with lexical substitution — same trick, different tradition (Kuusi 1977).
- Boundary: a **kenning** is institutionalized (conventional, teachable, reusable); a **riddle** is occasional (one-off, competitive, answer closed until solved). Same cognitive machinery — metaphor by allusion — differently licensed.

## 6. Proverbs: the culture's compressed hard drive

- Paremiology: proverb = fixed text with a situation-indexing use (Taylor 1931). Permyakov (1979) models proverbs as signs of situation-classes; the Russian school's "paremiological minimum" — the few hundred proverbs any competent adult commands — is a practical worldbuilding budget.
- Meta-proverb (Yoruba): "Proverbs are the horses of speech; when speech is lost in talk, we ride proverbs to track it down." Proverbs are the sanctioned way to say the unsayable — indirect reproach, counsel to superiors, negotiation cover.
- Content classes worth seeding: law and procedure (evidence, inheritance), agronomy and season, etiquette and hierarchy, the famous defeat or famine (historical allusion), the trickster precedent ("even the tortoise…").
- Proverbs are jurisprudence before codes: in African customary courts, proverb-quoting is argumentation proper (Finnegan 1970). Norse parallel: the lawspeaker recited a third of the law each assembly from the Law Rock (Íslendingabók / Grágás tradition) — law as performable text.
- Wisdom literature is the literate edge of the same system: Sumerian Instructions of Shuruppak, Egyptian sebayt (Ptahhotep; Lichtheim 1973), biblical Proverbs, Old English Maxims, Hávamál. Proverbs are the first oral genre a culture anthologizes once writing arrives.
- Build: 100–300 proverbs per culture; tag each with situation-class + ethos value + optional historical allusion. Let 3–5 proverbs reference the same lost war — proverb clusters are how a culture remembers trauma.

## 7. Riddles: taxonomy exams under a license to play

- Riddle = description whose referent must be recovered (Taylor 1951); riddle-tale plots are indexed in the international folktale catalog (Uther 2004). The Exeter Book preserves ~90 Old English riddles, several about writing itself (the book-consuming moth/worm) — a literacy-era fossil.
- Serious frames: Vedic brahmodya — riddle-debate as ritual and philosophical contest (Ṛgveda 1.164 is the classic riddle hymn); Norse wisdom duels (Vafþrúðnismál: Odin wagers his head); African riddle sessions as encyclopedic schooling for children (Finnegan 1970).
- Riddle content = cultural taxonomy: flora, fauna, weather, body, craft tools, domestic technology. A riddle corpus is your semantic-fields inventory made playable.
- Mythic riddles (sphinx-type) gate passage and sovereignty: riddling as trial is a plot engine that works in any era.
- One-line summary: **proverb** — meaning closed, use flexible; **kenning** — referent closed, use conventional; **riddle** — referent open until solved, use occasional.

## 8. Designing an oral-formulaic register ("the singer's speech")

Diction checklist — each item independently implementable:

1. Meter locked to the phonology (decision table below).
2. Formula lexicon: epithet sets per character; slot tables per meter position; expect drift and misuse (Hainsworth 1968).
3. Type-scene cards: 6–12 episodes, fixed beats + variable slots (Arend 1933).
4. Additive parataxis: "and X, and Y, and Z" chains; few subordinate clauses (Ong 1982's additive/aggregative tendencies — heuristic; see Calibration).
5. Epithetic reference over pronouns: characters re-named at each mention — an aid to listeners who cannot re-read.
6. Repetition with variation; parallelism pairs (Lowth 1753); ring-shaped episodes; thrice-structure.
7. Quotative frames: invocation openings (muse, ancestors, hero's name); "as the ancients say"; first-person performer intrusions.
8. Archaism layer: frozen inflections, obsolete weapon-names, superseded place-forms — the register lags the spoken language by centuries (couples historical-linguistics).
9. Taboo alternates in performance: euphemism sets for death, plague, and enemies' names (couples politeness-taboo).
10. Performer licensing: hereditary guild (jeli), apprenticeship path, competitive forms (Scottish flyting; Lokasenna-style senna), festival/court/wake frames, instrument as register key.
11. Audience interactivity: vocatives to the crowd, riddle breaks, applause-able formula returns.
12. Media extensions: tonal languages can ride drums (African talking drums; Carrington 1949) and whistled registers (Busnel & Classe 1976) — a "broadcast layer" for the same formulas.

Meter decision table:

| If the conlang has… | Natural verse engine | Real-world proof |
|---|---|---|
| vowel-length contrast, loose stress | quantitative meter (hexameter-like) | Greek, Vedic |
| initial stress + heavy onsets | alliterative long line (Sievers types A–E) | Old English, Old Norse |
| simple CV syllables, fixed syllabification | syllable-count + caesura | deseterac (South Slavic), śloka (Sanskrit) |
| trochaic stress, rhyme-poor poetics | trochaic tetrameter + parallelism | Finnic Kalevala meter |
| lexical tone | tonal counterpoint; drum/whistle speech | Yoruba dùndún (Carrington 1949) |
| rhyme-friendly final syllables | monorhyme qaṣīda or rhymed stanza | Arabic; Nibelungenlied |

- Enregisterment (Agha 2007): the register only becomes "the old speech" through metadiscourse — performance introductions, court etiquette about quoting singers, satire of bad poets, school scenes. Name the register in-world, or it will read as generic "archaic talk" (couples sociolinguistics-registers).
- Worked micro-example: a steppe horse-clan language with initial stress and no rhyme culture gets an alliterative long line; eight type-scene cards; ~60 epithet slots; kennings keyed to a sky-myth (horses = "wind's children", war = "the arrows' rain-debt" — each kenning cites a myth entry); 150 proverbs, twelve of them about the burned river-fort; two hereditary singer lineages licensed at the autumn assembly; a drum layer that transmits muster-orders in formula cadences across the steppe.

## 9. Textualization: what writing does to the tradition

- Dictation phase: a sung tradition written down at dictation speed runs longer than normal performance — Parry and Lord's "oral dictated text" hypothesis for the Homeric poems (Lord 1960).
- Crystallization: Nagy (1996) models Homer as a performance tradition that stabilized over generations (e.g. via Panathenaic rhapsodic contests), against "single genius poet" readings. The Homeric Question (opened by Wolf 1795) remains genuinely open — do not canonize either side.
- Canonical memorization: Vedic-style vikṛti recitations (pada/krama/jaṭā/ghana; Staal 1961; Howard 1986) make a canon orally self-checking — a temple can out-remember an archive; documents burn, recitations are distributed.
- Compilation: the Kalevala (Lönnrot 1835/1849) is field-collected material edited into one national epic — a 19th-century artifact, not an ancient recording. Perfect in-world plot: a state philologist "writes the epic" out of collected variants.
- Fossilization: after writing, the register survives as deliberate archaism — later written epics imitate oral style (Benson 1966), epithets decay into clichés, and the sung and read varieties split like H and L (couples writing-systems, sociolinguistics-registers).
- Decide, per culture, what each "epic text" is (Foley 2002): live performance, oral composition, dictated transcript, or literate imitation of oral style. The answer determines what a variant manuscript means in your plot.

## 10. Calibration: contested, discredited, commonly botched

- The "Great Divide" (oral minds vs literate minds; Havelock 1963; Ong 1982's popular form) is rejected in its strong version (Finnegan 1977; Goody & Watt 1963; Street 1984). Use Ong's psychodynamics list as a drafting checklist, never as cognitive law.
- Formula-density counts do not prove oral composition (Benson 1966); density is definition-dependent, and Magoun's (1953) extension of the test to Old English overreached what the test can show.
- Parry's "economy" is a tendency, not a law (Hainsworth 1968).
- South Slavic epic is one analogy, not a universal template (Finnegan 1977); Lord's oral/written dichotomy was sharper than the ethnography.
- "Oral cultures memorize word-perfectly": false in general — every re-performance drifts (Lord 1960). Near-perfection requires engineered systems (Vedic vikṛtis, recitation schools) or short ritual texts.
- "One genius poet" vs "communal composition" is unresolved in every famous case — the Homeric Question (Wolf 1795 → Nagy 1996), Beowulf, and Manas authorship are all live debates.
- Line counts and density figures circulating in worldbuilding sources are approximations from editions and fieldwork; the 12,323-line Meho figure is Lord's own count (Lord 1960).
- Orality ≠ ahistorical: praise poetry and griot repertoires are active historical argument (Barber 1991; Conrad 2004). "Pre-literate societies have no history" is the oldest error in this area.
- Do not model "pure oral epic" on the Kalevala: it is Lönnrot's compilation, a different kind of artifact.

## 11. Canon boundary

Reference knowledge and method only. Which singers exist, what the register is called, which myths the kennings cite, which war the proverbs remember — canon decisions owned by lore-shokunin. Hand off slot tables, type-scene cards, and proverb/riddle inventories as era-anchored options, and flag every to-verify item before it approaches the bible.
