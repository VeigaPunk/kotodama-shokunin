## 2. Located evidence and limits of analogy

The Lancaster overview describes Polari as mainly a lexicon and includes backslang among its inputs; it glosses backslang as saying a word as if spelled backwards. Its spelling discussion begins, “Polari is a more recent spelling.” [S952]

The publisher's book description states, “Polari is a secret form of language mainly used by homosexual men in London and other cities during the twentieth century”. This is a description of the book's subject, not evidence that the entire book has been read. [S953]

The Harman capture's lexicon title begins, “Here I set before thee good Reader, the leud lousey language of these lewtering Luskes”. The hostile wording belongs to that framing voice; it is not this reference's description of speakers. [S954]

Messili's verlan discussion uses the phrase “argot à la fois d’exclusion et de reconnaissance” in paragraph 8. Treat this as a located characterization of verlan in that article, not a universal definition of every secret register. [S955]

Design heuristic: place each analogy in its own evidence compartment. A publisher's sentence can motivate a question about audience and place; it cannot supply an unattested vocabulary or a scene transcript. A hostile early printed description can motivate a question about the observer's interests; it cannot be silently converted into an insider's self-description. A discussion of one reversal practice can motivate a formal experiment; it does not license merging all reversal practices into a single historical lineage.

Research limit: a direct transmission chain connecting all the named practices is `(to-verify)`. A historical origin, a first inventor, an exact speaker population, or a complete inventory for any proposed analogue is `(to-verify)` unless separately supported by one of the admitted captures. Do not fill these gaps with attractive etymologies. For the construction exercise, use invented tokens and label them fictional rather than supplying counterfeit historical vocabulary.

Handoff to lore: carry forward the evidence compartments and their limits alongside the design, so an analogy cannot acquire more authority during adaptation.

## 3. In-group concealment: choose a bounded threat model

Design heuristic: begin with one utterance and two listeners. Write the message in ordinary language, then identify the exact information to hide: a person's identity, an evaluation, a meeting place, a transaction, or the fact that a proposal has been made. Choose only the information needed by the scene. Do not start by replacing every noun in the setting.

For a fictional market encounter, suppose the ordinary message is “the meeting has moved to the west stair.” The private version may conceal only the location while leaving the change of plan audible. That is a different design from concealing whether any meeting exists. Write these as separate candidate solutions. Neither candidate is a claim about a real market, city, or community.

Specify the outsider's resources in the scene: can they hear the full exchange, ask for repetition, compare two conversations, read a note, recruit a fluent speaker, or observe where the participants go? Choose the resources deliberately rather than granting the code whatever protection the plot needs. Record the point at which concealment fails. A useful design result may be that the register buys a short delay but cannot withstand repeated exposure.

Then specify a legitimate cost. A novice may hesitate, a friend may misunderstand an obsolete term, or a location word may have two locally plausible readings. These are possible fictional constraints, not empirical claims about secret speech. Select a cost that follows from the chosen rule and the scene's information, not from an assumption that marginalized speakers are unreliable.

Separate recognition from concealment in the test. In one version, let the outsider understand the literal message but miss the invitation to intimacy. In another, let them recognize membership without decoding the location. Keep the different outcomes visible in the design record instead of collapsing them into a single “secret” score.

Handoff to lore: offer the threat model, the revealed information, and the failure boundary; lore chooses whether the encounter and its social stakes belong in canon.

## 4. Anti-language: design the conversational relationship

The Lancaster overview permits classing Polari as a language variety, sociolect, or anti-language; it does not require this worksheet to choose a single label for every use. [S952]

Design heuristic: for the proposed anti-language, test social structure maintained through conversation rather than merely counting substituted words. Draft a three-turn exchange: an opening that proposes a relationship, a response that accepts or challenges it, and a closing that determines what happens next. The test is a construction technique, not a new claim about the theory already owned by the adjacent anthropology entry.

Make two versions with identical vocabulary. In the first, an experienced speaker responds warmly to a novice's awkward usage; in the second, the same words receive an evasive reply. Specify the gesture, timing, correction, or subsequent action that differentiates the outcomes. This isolates the social decision from a lexical password. If the relationship is indistinguishable in both versions, revise the conversational design before inventing more terms.

Next draft a disagreement among insiders. Give both speakers competence in the register but different judgments about whether it should be used in front of a particular listener. Avoid making fluency automatically settle trust, authority, friendship, or ethical standing. In this fictional exercise, competent speech is one observable action, not a universal certificate of belonging.

For continuity, keep a scene ledger containing speaker, addressee, overhearer, intended relationship, literal proposition, and observable response. Do not import the sacred access hierarchy from the adjacent synthesis owner. This ledger tracks the exchange itself: who offered solidarity, who declined it, and what later action makes the interpretation legible to a reader.

An ethnographic claim that a particular historical group's conversations produced a particular social structure is `(to-verify)` here. The admitted material is not permission to invent an interview, participant observation, or transcript. Distinguish the proposed scene from evidence even when it feels persuasive.

Handoff to lore: deliver the contrasting conversations and the unresolved social interpretation, leaving the world's institutions and histories for lore-shokunin.

## 5. Backslang and reversal games: define the operation

Messili describes verlan in paragraph 9 as a language game involving reversal of a word's syllables, then presents inversion, added sound, and deletion possibilities in the following examples. [S955]

Design heuristic: do not equate the syllable operation in that description with the spelling reversal gloss in the Lancaster overview. Keep the comparison narrow: the overview explicitly glosses backslang through backwards spelling. [S952]

For a fictional reversal game, first choose the representation. Letter reversal, sound-segment reversal, and syllable-order reversal are separate proposed systems. Write an invented input as both an orthographic string and a segmented pronunciation before manipulating it. A designer may choose a simple notation such as `ta-mi` solely for this exercise; it is not an attested source example.

Candidate A reverses the two syllables, producing `mi-ta`. Candidate B reverses the four letters of `tami`, producing `imat`. These are fictional outputs under two stipulated rules. Do not say that the outputs are historically used words. If the story requires the sound of Candidate B, specify its pronunciation rather than assuming a reader will infer it from the spelling.

Build a test set with a one-syllable input, two equal syllables, unequal syllable lengths, a consonant cluster, a borrowed name, and a phrase. For every input, record whether the rule applies, leaves it unchanged, rejects it, or requires a declared repair. This is a design test set, not evidence that real games universally handle those cases in any one way.

Choose a single repair policy before generating the lexicon. For example, a fictional rule could insert a fixed vowel only when the output violates the invented base language's stated sound constraints. If the base language's phonotactics have not been designed, mark that dependency unresolved instead of asserting that an output is impossible. Prefer a visible exception list to an invisible change of algorithm.

Keep both an encoder and a decoder specification. If two inputs converge on one output, identify the ambiguity and decide whether context, a clarification phrase, or an intentionally playful misunderstanding handles it. Do not promise reversibility when the proposed deletion rule discards information.

Handoff to lore: submit the chosen operation, rejected alternatives, test pairs, and ambiguity policy; lore decides which fictional speakers adopt the game.

## 6. Ludling and Pig Latin style word play: a bounded prototype

The history, distribution, and standard rule inventory of Pig Latin are `(to-verify)` under this entry's admitted source set. Here “Pig Latin style” names the requested design surface of learnable word play, not a sourced historical account. A universal definition of ludling is likewise `(to-verify)` here; the worksheet uses the term only as an explicit label for its proposed rule-governed exercise.

Design heuristic: create a toy rule independent of the reversal prototype. Let the invented classroom exercise append `-va` to each two-syllable practice token, while leaving punctuation outside the operation. This is fictional and deliberately limited; it is not presented as the rule of Pig Latin, verlan, Polari, or any real ludling. Use invented practice tokens rather than appropriating a source's vocabulary to make the exercise look authentic.

Define the teaching sequence as a design artifact. Give three worked items, three unworked items, one deliberately ambiguous item, and a way for a learner to ask for help. Write down what counts as a correct answer under the stipulated rule. If the game later extends to other word shapes, version the rule and retain the original exercise so the story does not retrospectively change what the learner knew.

For a scene test, compare a playful exchange with a private planning exchange. The same transform can be proposed for both, but the fictional participants need not assign it the same stakes. Decide whether laughter, correction, annoyance, or refusal is the intended response. Do not infer that an easy rule is useless or that a complicated rule must be secret.

Add a no-operation case. A name, a warning, or a quoted instruction might remain in ordinary speech by the designer's choice. State the boundary in the fictional specification. An exception introduced solely to rescue a later plot must be visible as a revision, not disguised as a rule that supposedly existed all along.

Handoff to lore: provide the practice sheet and its fictional status; do not hand over an invented rule as recovered cultural history.

## 7. Argot and cant: build a useful lexical slice

The Lancaster overview reports a core vocabulary alongside a larger fringe and variation in individuals' knowledge; this supports asking about uneven repertoire without assuming one exhaustive shared dictionary. [S952]

Design heuristic: choose one activity for the first lexical slice. For a fictional repair crew, this might be assessing whether an object can be trusted until morning. Invent terms for distinctions that matter in that activity, then write ordinary paraphrases. The purpose is not to claim that repair crews historically have a particular cant; it is to keep the invented choices useful in dialogue.

For each proposed term, record an ordinary paraphrase, a worked utterance, a speaker who avoids it, and an ambiguity that could matter. Separate referential content from an evaluative undertone chosen for the story. A term may convey “temporarily usable” while the speaker's delivery expresses pride or contempt. Do not force the dictionary gloss to carry every social meaning.

Keep the prototype small enough to test in a complete scene. Draft the scene first in ordinary language, then substitute only where the lexical choice contributes to the intended concealment, affiliation, or word play. Read the result against the ordinary version. If the private version is merely less intelligible with no dramatic or social difference, reduce or revise the substitutions.

Plan knowledge as individual rather than uniform. In the fictional worksheet, one speaker can know a term passively, another can use it confidently, and a third can reject it as old-fashioned. These are proposed characterization options, not measured distributions. Keep each person's inventory stable across the tested scenes unless the narrative supplies a learning event.

Treat a hostile dictionary as a witness with a voice. The Harman title's derogatory characterization is visible in the capture, so borrowing its hostility as neutral narration would erase the distinction between recorder and recorded. [S954]

Handoff to lore: send the lexical slice, ordinary paraphrases, and speaker-specific variants; the crew's actual existence, values, and status remain lore decisions.

## 8. Leakage, change, and identity without a universal lifecycle

Messili's paragraph 12 discusses re-encoding when verlan words circulate into other social layers; that is the article's account of the case, not proof that every private repertoire must renew at a fixed rate. [S955]

Design heuristic: write a leakage event only if it serves the chosen setting. Choose one leaked item, who notices, and whether anything changes. The group might replace it, retain it for affection, use it ironically, or stop relying on it for concealment. These are alternate fictional branches; do not turn them into a mandatory lifecycle.

Preserve two separate questions in each branch: can outsiders understand the term, and do insiders still value saying it? This keeps a recognition marker available even after its referential content is public. If the plot needs a replacement, identify who proposes it and how another speaker can learn or refuse it. Avoid an omniscient collective that updates every speaker overnight.

For continuity across generations or regions in a fictional world, keep variant records rather than a single corrected form. Annotate each form with the scenes in which it is used, not invented census figures. A designer can leave the distribution undecided and flag the missing evidence instead of manufacturing a precise dialect map.

Do not claim that real concealment always produces resistance, or that play always implies safety. Those universal social propositions are `(to-verify)` here. In the proposed world, specify the actual stakes through action and consequence, allowing characters to disagree about what their register means.

Handoff to lore: supply alternative change events and their consequences; select no canonical chronology on this reference page.

## 9. Delivery procedure and source hygiene

Design heuristic: deliver a compact packet containing the ordinary scene, the transformed scene, the operation specification, the lexical slice, the learner exercise, and the outsider comprehension test. Include a rejection log: which attractive rules were discarded because they contradicted another rule, obscured the scene, or required an unsupported analogue? Retain only choices that meet the intended scene's needs without silently changing the constraints.

Mark every historical comparison with its source and every invented utterance as fictional. Do not attach a citation to a fictional token as though the token occurred in the source. Do not conflate a source author's analytical description, a quoted hostile observer, and the designer's proposal. These are three distinct voices in the packet.
