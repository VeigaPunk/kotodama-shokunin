## 1. Scope: the contest this entry owns, and the four things it defers

This entry owns the **word-contest** — the occasion on which people fight with words in front of an audience that decides who won. It owns four things inside that surface: the inventory of contest genres and how they differ; the formula grammar of the three moves the contest is made of (the boast, the taunt, the riposte); the rules that keep the contest a contest rather than a quarrel or a war; and the design procedure for building a fictional word-contest whose stakes stay tied to its contestants, its judges and its occasions.

It explicitly does **not** own the following, each of which is owned elsewhere and is named here only as a neighbour:

- **The formulaic composition machinery** — the formula, the type-scene, the epithet, the meter, the performer-licensing inventory, and the theory of oral composition. That is `linguistics/orality-formulaic-language.md`, which already lists the Scottish flyting and the Lokasenna-style senna as two words inside one licensing-checklist item. This entry treats those forms as **contest genres with rules** and adds no new theory of how oral verbal art is composed or remembered (design, not sourced).
- **The binding-speech machinery** — what makes a spoken word hold, who executes it, the enforcement ledger, the curse physics, the release taxonomy. That is `myth-symbol/binding-speech-complex.md`, and the slot anatomy of the binding utterance is `linguistics/oath-blessing-curse-formulae.md`. A satire with legal force is a **curse** and belongs there; this entry owns only the licensed contest that a satire may happen inside, and defers the cause grammar and the sanction (design, not sourced).
- **The politeness and taboo machinery** — the face-work model, the taboo domains, the euphemism ladder, the rules that forbid saying. That is `linguistics/politeness-taboo-euphemism.md`. A ritual insult is the **sanctioned inverse** of a taboo — a licence to say what is normally unsayable, and only on the occasion and inside the frame — and this entry owns the licence, not the prohibition (design, not sourced).
- **Canon, and the method of research.** What is true in any world is lore-shokunin's; how a source is found, graded and admitted is chousa's. This entry is reference craft and generative method only (design, not sourced).

Read this file as craft for building invented contests. It reports what eight opened sources say about word-contests in their own traditions and converts those reports into a building procedure; it settles nothing about any world, people or law.

## 2. The verbal duel: contestants, occasion and frame

A **verbal duel** is a contest fought in words. The definition is not that people argue; it is that the arguing has been **converted into a form**: there are contestants, there is a frame, there is an audience, and there is something at stake that the audience can adjudicate (design, not sourced).

The broadest definition on record in the opened material is the one this entry starts from, and it is worth quoting in full because each of its terms is a design slot. The genre is defined as "a genre of argumentative language that entails exchanges between two persons, parties," (S985) and the exchange is one in which the parties "challenge each other to a performative display of verbal skillfulness in front of" an audience (S985). Four things are decided by that account and a designer must decide all four (design, not sourced):

1. **Two or more parties.** The contest is between parties, not between a speaker and a world. A monologue that insults an absent third party is not a duel (design, not sourced).
2. **A challenge.** The parties *challenge each other*; the challenge is an act, and the design says who is entitled to issue it (design, not sourced).
3. **A performative display of verbal skillfulness.** The thing on display is **skill at language**, which is why a duel can be lost by a speaker who was right (design, not sourced).
4. **An audience.** The display is made *in front of* an audience, and the audience is the term's judge (design, not sourced).

The definition in the opened material is deliberately wide, and the width is a design gift: the survey reports that "Verbal duels can be highly structured or more free-flowing; some traditions use rhymes," (S985) and that they may be sung or spoken, improvised or memorized. Three things follow for a designer (design, not sourced):

- **A duel need not use insults.** The form can be a praise competition, a demonstration of craft, or a formal exchange about a matter, and a designer who writes a duel as an insult-exchange has chosen one option and called it the genre (inference).
- **A duel is not a rite of passage.** It is performed by adults as well as by children, and by women as well as by men, so the age-and-sex limits that a reader may assume are a local rule of a given tradition and not part of the form (inference).
- **A duel is not a catharsis.** The reading that a duel is a release of aggression is described in the opened material as a common view that the same scholarship criticises: "A common view of verbal duels posits that they are a cathartic expression of aggression" (S985). A designer who wants a duel to carry a plot needs it to do social work, not to discharge anger (inference).

Three things separate a duel from a quarrel, and a designer has to decide all three before writing a line of it (design, not sourced):

1. **The frame is announced.** A duel opens with a formula that says a contest is beginning — the challenge, the call-and-response, the naming of the sides, the setting of the stake. A quarrel has no such formula; it starts wherever it starts.
2. **The audience is present and positioned.** The audience is not a witness to the fight; it is one of the three parts of the machine. It hears, it reacts, and it decides.
3. **The stake is external to the insult.** What is won is not pride but something the culture can name — a payment, a marriage, a year's exemption, a reputation, a post. The insult itself is the means; the stake is the point.

The design consequence of the third point is the one that keeps a duel from being a mood: **an insult with no stake is a quarrel, and a quarrel with no frame is a brawl** (design, not sourced).

The frame is also what makes a contest repeatable. A form that can be opened is a form that can be scheduled, staged, judged and remembered, which is the difference between a custom and an incident (design, not sourced).

## 3. Flyting and senna: two word-contest forms

The two genres the sources use most often are the Scottish **flyting** and the Norse **senna**. They are worth putting side by side because they differ in exactly the variable that a designer has to choose (design, not sourced).

The Scottish flyting is described, in the edition this entry opens, as a competitive contest between two speakers over the piling-on of abuse: "The flyting was a verbal competition in which the participants vied in heaping abuse of" (S988) "all sorts on each other, some of it quite gross." (S988). The same source calls it at least in part a literary game, which is the phrase a designer should keep: a game has rules, and rules are what separate the form from an exchange of abuse (design, not sourced).

The genre has a purpose that the scholarship on it states directly, and the statement is the design-relevant part: "the Scottish genre of flyting, whose main" (S989) "purpose was to humiliate the opponent" (S989). The form's aim is to *humiliate* — which is a stake, and a cultural one: the loser is made small in front of the audience, and that is what the audience came for (design, not sourced).

The Norse senna is the same act inside a feast rather than on a stage. The account of the poem that this entry opens says of it: "The exchange or contest of insults was dear to the" (S987) Norse heart. The poem's own frame — Loki enters the hall, the company falls silent, he asks for a place and a seat at the feast and is answered, and the exchange of insults follows — is the *senna* in performance: "Then Loki went into the hall, but when they who were there saw who had entered, they were all silent." (S987). The contest is at a table, with the assembly as the audience (design, not sourced).

The Arabic tradition supplies the same structure in a different key, and the survey of the form names it: "Soon these 'Flytings' (_Naqá’iḍ_) were recited" (S992) "everywhere, and each poet had thousands of enthusiastic partisans who" (S992) maintained their superiority to the rival. The word-contest is a **recited** art in that tradition, performed before partisans who attach themselves to one poet or the other, and that partisanship is the audience-adjudication mechanism in its simplest form: the crowd's side is the verdict (design, not sourced).

The forms bracket the design space. A flyting is a **scheduled contest with a purpose and a public**; a senna is a **staged exchange inside a feast, with the assembly as the audience and the outcome a matter of who has the last word**; an Arabic *naqāʾid* contest is a **recited performance before a partisan crowd**; and a Basque *bertsolaritza* contest is a **judged championship with rules, a panel and prizes** (design, not sourced).

What all of them share, and what a designer may not separate from them, is that the insult is **directed at the other contestant in front of witnesses**. Remove the witnesses and the same sentence becomes an assault; that is the difference between these forms and a quarrel (design, not sourced).

## 4. Ritual insult across traditions

A **ritual insult** is an utterance whose whole point is to be an insult, and whose safety depends on the frame around it. The frame has four parts, and a designer who omits any one of them produces a real insult rather than a ritual one (design, not sourced):

- **The licence** — who is licensed to insult whom, on what occasion.
- **The occasion** — the date, the festival, the gathering, the licensed space.
- **The form** — the fixed shapes the insult takes, so the hearer can tell a set piece from a personal attack.
- **The release** — the ritual that ends the contest and returns the participants to ordinary speech.

The opened material separates this from the duel deliberately, and the separation is the most useful thing in it for a designer. The critique of the field that this entry opens puts the point as follows: "it is important to avoid conflating verbal duels with" (S985) "ritual insults, since these are substantially different, albeit overlapping, genres." (S985). The consequence for design is that a **ritual insult may have no opponent at all** — no exchange, no riposte, no audience verdict — and a designer who wants a contest must add the opponent, the turns and the adjudication, because the ritual-insult frame supplies none of them (design, not sourced).

What makes the insult safe is **who is allowed to answer, and how** — and the traditions disagree. In the Turkish *ashik* contest the duty is to answer with wit and never with anger: "an angry poet would literally be seen as the cause of the offense, not the victim of it" (S985). In the Ewe *haló* the reverse holds: "In the haló the offended party is expected to answer" (S985), even with physical violence. The design lesson is that a ritual insult needs an explicit **answer rule** — answer in kind, answer by force, or do not answer — and a designer who leaves it open has an insult with no ritual in it (design, not sourced).

The generalisation this entry draws from these cases — and marks as **inference**, because the sources do not state it as a general law — is that the four parts of the frame are what separate a ritual insult from a personal attack, and that the traditions differ mostly in how expensive each part is to run (inference).

## 5. Song duels: the contest carried by a tune

A **song duel** runs the same contest in a sung form: the taunt and the riposte ride on a shared melody, and the audience judges the singing as well as the words. The change is not cosmetic (design, not sourced).

The tradition this entry opens describes the song duel as a **legal instrument**: "The song duels are juridical instruments insofar as they do serve" (S986) to settle disputes and restore normal relations between estranged members of the community. The same account notes that the contests are announced and occur on festive occasions, and that stealth, cunning and ambush are no part of them (S986). The form is widespread: "The occurrence of the song-duel complex all down the west coast" (S986) of Alaska and into the Aleutian Islands is the source's evidence for how basic the form is among the Eskimos.

What is being contested there is standing in a dispute, and the source states the mechanism plainly: the singer delivers the traditional patterns "with such finesse as to delight the audience to enthusiastic applause" (S986), and the most heartily applauded singer is the winner; the win carries no restitution, only prestige, and "Among the East Greenlanders song duels may be carried on for" (S986) years, for the fun of it. The verdict is the audience's applause, not an officer's ruling — the song duel is the purest case of audience adjudication in this entry's set.

Two things follow for a designer, and they are the reason the song duel is worth its own section (design, not sourced):

1. **The form is a substitute for the enforcement.** The contest settles the matter itself; there is no court behind it and no executor afterwards. That is why the contest must have a judge *inside* it — the judge is the mechanism by which the dispute ends (design, not sourced).
2. **The occasion is the safety.** The announcement and the festive setting are what make the insult licensable, and both are structural parts of the form rather than decorations (design, not sourced).

Three further design questions a song duel forces that a spoken duel does not (design, not sourced):

- **Who carries the melody?** A contest with two contestants needs either two singers or a chorus that sings the frame. A chorus that sings the frame is a *third* voice, and the culture that has one has a chorus (design, not sourced).
- **What is improvised and what is fixed?** In a sung form the tune is fixed and the words can be improvised; in a spoken form it is often the reverse. A design that fixes both is a recitation and has lost the contest (design, not sourced).
- **Who tunes it?** Someone has to start, and the person who starts has decided the pace of the whole contest (design, not sourced).

## 6. The boast formula: claim, warrant and wager

The **boast formula** is the opening move of most word-contests: the speaker asserts a claim about their own worth, their own deeds or their own line, and the assertion is deliberately larger than the facts. The audience's task is to test it (design, not sourced).

The grammar of a boast proposed here has four slots — the framing that marks the utterance as a contest move, the claim, the warrant offered for it, and the stake the speaker puts on it. These are this entry's own working slots for the contest move; they are not the slot set of the binding utterance, which `linguistics/oath-blessing-curse-formulae.md` teaches separately (performative verb, witness invocation, assertion, sanction clause, self-imprecation, uptake), and a designer who wants a boast to bind should take that file's slots rather than these (design, not sourced).

The opened material shows the boast doing two jobs at once, which is why the slot order matters. The claim is made **about the speaker's own group**, and it is made in a form that the hearers can check: the survey describes one sung contest form in which the poets exchange stanzas on a theme usually proposed by the audience (S985). Three consequences follow (design, not sourced):

1. **The warrant is part of the boast, not a separate move.** A claim with no warrant is a puff; a warrant with no claim is a boast's own opposite. The formula needs both, in that order.
2. **The audience supplies the occasion, and therefore the test.** If the audience proposes the theme, the claim is already shaped to be answerable, and the riposte is invited by the boast itself.
3. **The boast is the opening bid, not the whole contest.** A contest that ends after the boasts has never been a contest; the boasting is what the taunts and ripostes are about.

The design point the boast formula forces is **calibration**: a boast that could literally be true is not a boast, and a boast whose claim is so outrageous that no audience would entertain it is a joke. The usable range is narrow, and a culture's sense of what is *plausibly* overclaimed is a real register (design, not sourced).

## 7. Audience adjudication: judges, uptake and outcome

**Audience adjudication** is the part of the machine that decides the outcome, and it is where most designed word-contests go wrong: they have contestants, they have insults, and they have no way to say who won (design, not sourced).

The opened material puts the audience at the centre of the performance rather than at its edge, and says so in the strongest terms available: "Furthermore, in verbal duels, the audience is fundamental in deciding what is or is not an insult," (S985) and "the performance between poets and audience is therefore the key." (S985). Two design consequences (design, not sourced):

- **The audience's taste is a rule of the contest.** The duelers adapt to what the audience wants to hear, which means the audience is not a passive consumer of a fixed form but a party to the form's content.
- **Authority is drawn from the audience, not only from the words.** The duelers' standing comes from pleasing the listeners, so the outcome of the contest is partly about reputation and not only about who spoke better.

Two institutionalised forms carry adjudication in the opened material, and they are different solutions to the same problem (design, not sourced).

The **judged panel** solves it by office. The Basque championship that this entry opens is described with the panel as a named institution of the contest, together with the rules and the prizes: "the Consistory, and thus the first proper championship of bertsolaris arose, with its rules, judges" (S990) and prizes, and in the contests the verdict is public — "championships, when the judges announced their verdict, choosing Xalbador to participate in the" (S990) final round, after which the audience booed the decision at length. The design lesson is that a contest with prizes needs a **body** that can be recorded as having judged, that the body has to be constituted before the contest, not improvised at the end of it, and that a constituted panel can still be overruled in the hall's opinion (design, not sourced).

The **named adjudicator** solves it by role. The Filipino debate-poetry form this entry opens is described with its own official, the *lakandiwa*, and the genre's frame is stated in the same sentence: a "joust between two poets with an overseeing judge or emcee-adjudicator that evolved in part from the duplo," (S991) and a genre that is "Balagtasan is an indigenous form of competitive Filipino debate poetry in the Tagalog language" (S991). The design lesson is that a contest with a moderator has a **voice** that can interrupt, summarise and rule, and that the presence of such a voice changes what the contestants are willing to say (design, not sourced).

The adjudication step has three parts, and a designer should specify all three (design, not sourced):

1. **The signal** — the visible thing that marks a hit: the crowd's noise, a judge's hand, a dropped token, a repeated line.
2. **The tally** — how the signal becomes a score: a count, a best-of-n, a single verdict, a withdrawal.
3. **The award** — what the score pays: a prize in coin, a right, a rank, a marriage.

The three are separable, and a design that has one without the others fails in a specific way. A signal with no tally is theatre: the crowd roars and nothing changes. A tally with no award is a scoreboard nobody reads. An award with no signal is a decision the audience cannot witness, and the audience is what makes the contest worth having (design, not sourced).

## 9. The rules that keep an insult ritual rather than real

This is the section the entry exists for. A ritual insult is **safe because of the rules around it**, and the rules have to be stated explicitly, because they are what a reader checks when the scene turns (design, not sourced).

Six rules, each with the failure it prevents (design, not sourced):

1. **The licence rule.** Say who may insult whom. A culture with no licence rule has no ritual insult, because any insult is then a personal attack and every insult is then a real one.
2. **The occasion rule.** Say when the insult may happen. An insult licensed only at the festival is safe; the same words on a Tuesday are not the same words.
3. **The form rule.** Say what the insult must look like. A fixed form — a set of taunts, a metre, a sequence, a stock of formulae — makes the insult legible as a set piece and auditable as one.
4. **The target rule.** Say what may not be named. Some things are off the list in every contest, and the list is usually where a culture's real values are written down.
5. **The release rule.** Say how the contest ends. A closing formula, a host's thanks, a change of tune, a drink, a move to another game: without a release, the frame leaks.
6. **The consequence rule.** Say what the insult does *not* do. The audience may laugh; the insult does not make the target a criminal, it does not change the target's legal status, and it does not survive the frame.

The consequence rule is the one most often left implicit, and it is the one that decides whether the reader believes the scene (design, not sourced).

## 10. Error patterns: contests that stop being contests

The rules above make these failures checkable, because each failure is a missing or broken rule (design, not sourced):

**1. The insult with no frame.** An insult spoken outside any licensed occasion reads as an attack, and the reader will treat it as one. The test: ask where this was said and who was listening. If the answer is *anywhere, anyone*, there is no ritual here.

**2. The contest with no judge.** A contest whose outcome nobody announces is a scene, not a contest, and the stakes never get paid. The test: ask who says the words that end it.

**3. The ritual that slips into real violence.** The moment the insult is meant to produce a wound rather than a laugh, the frame is over. The test: ask whether the target could be injured by the words, and whether anyone would stop it. If nobody would, the words are a weapon.

**4. The contest that cannot end.** A contest with no release rule runs until someone is exhausted or someone is hurt. The test: ask what physically happens when the contest is over.

**5. The boast that outgrows the audience.** When the claim is so large that the audience has no way to test it, the contest becomes a recitation. The test: ask what a listener could have said to check the claim.

**6. The audience that is only decoration.** A crowd that laughs but never decides is a chorus in the literary sense, and the contest has no stakes. The test: ask what the crowd's noise changes.

**7. The licence that leaks.** An insult licensed for one pair of people — a peer, a guest, a comic partner — and used on anyone else is a violation, and the reader will feel it as one. The test: ask who could make this insult and who could not.

**8. The insult that names the undefendable.** Every contest has something off the list: kin, the dead, the sacred, the physically marked, the guest. The test: ask what is on the list, and whether the scene respects it.

**9. The contest with stakes that do not exist.** A prize the culture does not value, or a payment nobody makes, makes the whole frame decorative. The test: ask who pays and what they get.

**10. The audience that never forgives.** A contest whose audience is drawn from the target's own household, or from a rival group, is not safe because the licence says so. The test: ask who is in the room and what they would like to happen.

## 11. Design procedure: a fictional word-contest

This is the operating procedure, and it is a design instrument and nothing else (design, not sourced).

**Before writing a single line (design, not sourced):**

1. Fix the contest genre: scheduled contest, feast exchange, sung duel, quick-answer game, or licensed performance of insult.
2. Fix the **frame**: the opening formula, the closing formula, and what happens between them.
3. Fix the **licence rule**: who may insult whom, and under what authority.
4. Fix the **occasion**: the calendar slot, the place, the sponsor, the duration.
5. Fix the **adjudication**: the signal, the tally, the award.
6. Fix the **stakes**: what is won, who pays, and whether the payment is in money, rank, right or marriage.
7. Fix the **target list**: what may not be named, and who enforces it.
8. Fix the **release**: the act that ends the contest and returns ordinary speech.

**While drafting (design, not sourced):**

9. Give the boast formula a plausible claim and a real warrant; the claim must be testable and the test must be visible.
10. Make the taunt specific to the occasion and to what the contestants know about each other; a generic taunt is a joke, and a joke is not a contest move.
11. Write the riposte as a counter-move that uses the opponent's own claim; a riposte that changes subject loses the round and the reader will not know why.
12. Keep the turns even. A contest in which one side has four moves and the other has two reads as a beating, not a duel.
13. Use the fixed formulae to make the contest legible — the audience must be able to tell a set piece from a personal remark.
14. Write the audience into the scene: what they do at each hit, and how the scene ends for them.

**Before the design is declared done (design, not sourced):**

15. Check every scene against the error patterns of §10 — in particular the insult with no frame, the contest with no judge, and the ritual that slips into real violence.
16. Check that the contest's stakes are tied to named contestants, named judges and a named occasion; a contest whose stakes float free of all three cannot be used in a scene.
17. Check that no design decision here restates a neighbouring entry: the composition machinery belongs to orality-formulaic-language.md, the binding side to oath-blessing-curse-formulae.md and binding-speech-complex.md, the taboo side to politeness-taboo-euphemism.md, the contest-procedure side to culture-material/games-play-sport-contests.md.
18. Check that the entry has not decided any canon: the names, the offices, the festival, the deity and the history belong to the lore layer.
19. Record the non-canon status and the handoff.

**A worked micro-example (NON-CANON).** Two dyeing households on a terraced hillside meet in a word-duel **twice a year**, at the vat-yard, on the morning the season's cloth is judged. The frame is fixed: the cloth-judge names the two contestants, the contestants speak the challenge themselves, and each side gets three turns. The adjudicator is the cloth-judge, the signal is a bolt of cloth lifted by the buyers' agent, and the tally is best of three; the award is the first place in the buyers' queue for the season. The boast formula claims something for the speaker's own household — *our blue reached the river-mouth markets* — and must be followed by a warrant naming a bolt, a year and a buyer. A taunt must name something the other household really did, and the riposte must answer with a counter-claim of its own. The release is the cloth-judge's call of the first bolt, which ends the contest and opens the market. The forbidden targets are painted on the yard wall: the dead, the spring that feeds the vats, and the buyers. The audience is the buyers' agents and the other households, and the licence covers judging day only, between households paired for the year. Everything else about these people — language, offices, belief and history — is canon and belongs to the lore layer (design, not sourced).

## 12. Limits, calibration and the handoff to the lore world bible

**How far the sources reach.** Eight opened sources ground this entry, and each grounds only the passage captured from it. None surveys word-contests worldwide, so no part of this entry is a universal grammar of the duel (design, not sourced).

**Calibration.** A practice reported for one tradition stays a report about that tradition here and becomes a *design option* only for invented ones. The frame of §4, the rules of §9 and the procedure of §11 are this entry's apparatus, not a finding that real contests share them (design, not sourced). The catharsis reading of word-contests is recorded as a view the opened critique argues against, not as a fact (S985). The Inuit, Basque and Filipino material each describe one tradition's contest and are not generalised beyond it (design, not sourced).

**The relationship-versus-fact line.** A ritual insult is a genre, not a report of what anyone said. The corpus's existing calibration on the Arabic invective mode applies here as a rule for the whole entry: do not turn a recorded insult into an ethnographic fact, and do not turn a described practice into a universal (design, not sourced).

**Canon and rights stay outside.** Nothing here says what happens in any fictional world, and nothing here rules on whether a real community's contest form may be borrowed; those questions belong to the canon layer and, for borrowing, to a rights process (design, not sourced).

**What lore-shokunin receives.** A surface, not canon: the genre table (§8), the boast and taunt working slots (§6), the answer and licence rules (§4, §9), the failure modes (§10) and the build procedure (§11). The world bible names the people, the festival, the judges, the prizes and the language, and decides whether any contest in the fiction is lawful or sacred (design, not sourced).

## 13. Inference ledger

The statements below are readings this entry draws from the opened sources but which the sources do not state in that form. They are marked *inference* here and in the body, and a reader who rejects them loses no cited claim (design, not sourced).

- That the four parts of the frame (licence, occasion, form, release) are what separate a ritual insult from a personal attack (inference).
- That a duel need not use insults, and that an insult-exchange is one option rather than the genre (inference).
- That the safest way to model a word-contest for fiction is to fix the adjudication before the dialogue (inference).
- That the consequence rule — what the insult does *not* do — is the rule a reader checks first, and therefore the rule to write first (inference).
- That a contest with no award is not a contest with a small prize but a different genre (a display) (inference).
- That the folk reading of word-contests as cathartic release of aggression is a twentieth-century scholarly framing rather than a description of the forms themselves (inference from the critique the opened material levels at that framing).
