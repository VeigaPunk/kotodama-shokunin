## 1. Core-vocabulary lists: the floor, not the house

| List | Size | Origin | Built for | Trust as "universal" |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Swadesh | 215 → 200 → 100 (1950/1952/1955) | lexicostatistics | dating via retention rates | medium; Swadesh himself culled culture-bound items |
| Dolgopolsky | 15 (1964) | long-range comparison | most stable meanings | high within the stable-core idea; exact 15 varies by reproduction |
| Yakhontov | 35 and 65 | Moscow school lexicostatistics | stricter dating | high; publication details to-verify, list widely used via Starostin school |
| ASJP-40 | 40 (Holman et al. 2008) | automated comparison | wide-coverage classification | medium; deliberately minimal |
| Leipzig–Jakarta | 100 (WOLD 2009) | borrowability test over 41 languages | meanings most resistant to borrowing | high for borrowability, not for universality |

- The Swadesh-100 is the working floor for a conlang: every item must be renderable, even by periphrasis (kill = make-dead; small-child for baby; water-offspring for fish). This forces a derivation strategy early (Rosenfelder 2010 formalizes this drill).
- Concepticon (List et al. 2016) maps every historical list onto stable concept IDs — use it instead of hand-copying lists; CLICS³ (Rzymski et al. 2020) shows which concept pairs colexify across ~3000 languages (check before merging two meanings into one word).
- NSM's ~65 semantic primes (Wierzbicka 1996, 2014) are a different, complementary floor: the words a culture allegedly cannot avoid using for explication. Treat as a design constraint for ritual/legal registers, not as gospel.

## 2. What the floor is for — and where it breaks

- Use 1 — coverage check: the 100 items expose holes in morphology before prose does.
- Use 2 — stability tiers: core resists replacement, periphery borrows first. This is the backbone of contact realism.
- Use 3 — budget: a lexicon of 1,500–3,000 derived from ~100 roots plus productive morphology reads as a natural language; a lexicon of 1,500 flat coinages reads as a naming language.
- Limits, stated plainly:
  - Lists are not culture-free. 'ice', 'snow', 'sea', 'to swim', 'bark (n.)' have no dedicated word in many languages; tropical and landlocked cultures express them by phrase or not at all. For any given list item, "no word here" is a legitimate, informative design decision.
  - Glottochronology is discredited as a clock. Lees 1953 fixed a constant retention rate (~0.81–0.86 per millennium); Bergsland & Vogt 1962 destroyed the constant with documented rates in Icelandic, Norwegian Riksmål, and Armenian. Use stability tiers as a ranking, never as dates.
  - The universalism debate (Swadesh's assumption of uniform semantic atoms) is unsettled; Wierzbicka's primes are her school's answer, not consensus.
- Design rule: the floor proves your grammar can say anything; the fields (below) prove your culture notices something.

## 3. Field engineering: Trier, salience, colexification, change routes

- Fields, not bags of words. Trier 1931 showed Middle High German wîs 'wise/knowledge (courtly)' splitting into wîs 'manner/way' + kunst 'knowledge', with kunst then sliding to 'craft/cunning' when wizzen 'knowledge' arose. One field restructured across roughly a century, pushing neighbors as it moved. Lesson for era-work: vocabularies change by neighbor pressure within fields; log the push, not just the innovation.
- Salience drives granularity. Conklin 1954: Hanunóo has four basic color terms but roughly 1,800 named plant categories, and the color terms classify rice-growth stages. Lexicon maps cultural attention, not perception capacity.
- Colexification is evidence. Before merging sun/moon, fire/wood, day/sun in one lexeme, check CLICS³ for whether real languages colexify that pair; before splitting river kinds finer than English does, check that some language really does.
- Change routes to log per coinage (Campbell 1998): metaphor, metonymy, synecdoche, narrowing, broadening, pejoration, amelioration. Field-typical routes:
  - body → spatial (head of the mountain, mouth of the river) and body → instrument
  - subsistence → money (Latin pecūnia 'wealth' < pecū 'cattle'; salary < salarium 'salt-money')
  - weather → emotion (stormy, sunny) and weather → calendar (season terms)
  - religion → taboo strata and euphemism cycling (see §4 Religion)
- Borrowability strata (Thomason & Kaufman 1988 for intensity; WOLD/Tadmor et al. 2010 for item level). High-borrow fields: religion, war/ranks, dress, cuisine, trade/measures, administration. Resistant: core body parts, basic kin, low numerals, pronouns. Core leaks under intense contact — in the WOLD 41-language sample even basic meanings get borrowed, and the single most-borrowed meaning was a pronoun ('she'). Normans split English subsistence vocabulary by class: live ox/calf/sheep/pig/deer (Saxon farmers) vs. beef/veal/mutton/pork/venison (French table) (Baugh & Cable 2002) — a stratal split is cheap, checkable era evidence.

## 4. Field-by-field briefs

### Body
- Partition varies with culture, not anatomy: English hand/arm vs. Russian ruká (arm+hand); Japanese te covers hand and forearm. Limb and head partitions are near-universal; internal-organ vocabularies are where cultures diverge.
- Soul-organs: organs assigned mental/moral function differ (heart in Egyptian and many cultures, liver in Mesopotamian and Classical Chinese medicine, hara 'belly' as seat of intent in Japanese usage). Body-part metaphor is the standard source for spatial vocabulary (Andersen 1978; Enfield, Majid & van Staden 2006).
- Worldbuilding use: give the body field one anomalous partition + one soul-organ doctrine; derive the spatial system from it, not from English prepositions.

### Kinship
- The six reference types (Murdock 1949 labels, still standard): Eskimo/lineal (English: nuclear family highlighted, cousins merged); Hawaiian/generational (all same-sex same-generation merged); Iroquois/bifurcate-merging (parallel cousins = siblings, cross-cousins distinct); Crow (matrilineal skewing) and Omaha (patrilineal skewing) as mirror images; Sudanese/descriptive (every collateral distinct; characteristic of bureaucratic states — Latin, Chinese).
- Lounsbury 1964 reduces skewing types to rewrite rules — port these as generation rules for your conlexicon. Sibling-term types correlate with patterns of cross-sex interaction and division of labor (Nerlove & Romney 1967).
- Registers: Dyirbal has guwal (everyday) vs. jalnguy (avoidance register used near taboo relatives), with a smaller, one-to-one-mapped lexicon (Dixon 1972). Teknonymy and name-avoidance generate epithet systems.
- Worldbuilding use: the kin field is the cheapest single place to display descent + marriage rules; choose the type first from social structure, then coin the terms.

### Weather
- The "N words for snow" inflation is the founding case study of lexical pop-ethnology: Boas 1911 noted a few roots; the count grew to ~100 through an error chain documented by Martin 1986 and Pullum 1991. Reality: Central Alaskan Yup'ik has on the order of 15 snow-related roots, but productive suffixation yields hundreds of derived lexemes (Woodbury, per Martin 1986).
- Worldbuilding use: copy the mechanism, not the number. Polysynthetic/derivation-heavy languages manufacture weather words on demand; analytic languages do not. If your culture has 20 weather nouns, justify each one (rain-by-duration, rain-by-wetness, wind-by-direction-of-travel) or cut them.

### Landscape
- Languages cut terrain at different joints (watercourse words are the classic scatter: creek/brook/stream/run vs. finer single systems); ethnotopography/ethnophysiography studies this (Burenhult & Levinson 2008, venue to-verify).
- Toponymic strata date settlement layers: suffix chains (English -chester/-caster/-cester; Slavic -grad/-gorod) work like geological strata in era-work.
- Worldbuilding use: decide which terrain differences carry subsistence weight (pasture types, flood stages, pass routes) and lexicalize only those; reuse body/spatial metaphors from the body field.

### Subsistence
- Folk-biological ranks are stable cross-culturally: life-form → generic → specific/varietal, with generic-level salience and inventories clustering in the hundreds (Berlin, Breedlove & Raven 1974; Berlin 1992). A temple state with 12 plant names is a red flag; a swamp culture with 400 fish terms is not.
- Pastoral wealth vocabulary (herd-age, herd-color, herd-condition terms) indexes property systems; reconstructable PIE horse/wheel/pastoral vocabulary mapped against archaeology is the model case (Anthony 2007; wheel family *kʷekʷlo-).
- Worldbuilding use: couple the subsistence field to calendar (harvest terms → month names) and to number words (tallies precede numerals).

### War
- War lexicon indexes force organization: levy societies have few rank terms; standing armies borrow rank vocabularies wholesale (English from French; Meiji Japanese from European languages — specific donors to-verify). Weapons and armor are high-borrow and therefore era markers (a "helmet" word that is a loan from a rival culture says who made your armor).
- Chariot/horse vocabularies spreading with the technology itself (Anthony 2007) is the reusable pattern: the field's loan stratum maps the technology's diffusion map.

### Religion
- Taboo and replacement: euphemism treadmills cycle within eras (Pinker 2007; Allan & Burridge 2006) — deity/illness/death terms are replaced and re-replaced, so ritual registers stratify by age. Bear 'honey-eater' style taboo replacements are commonly cited across Slavic/Finnic material (classic example; specific etymologies to-verify).
- Honorific stratification: Javanese ngoko/madya/krama speech levels (Geertz 1960) and politeness theory (Brown & Levinson 1987) supply the design for deity/royalty registers.
- Evidentiality: grammaticalized information-source marking (Aikhenvald 2004; Tuyuca's five evidentials, Barnes 1984). For myth-work: scripture and hearsay default to reported/non-visual evidentials; a religion whose miracles are narrated in the visual evidential is making a grammatical claim.
- Worldbuilding use: pick two of {name-taboo, speech levels, evidential grammar, euphemism cycle} and let them generate the ritual lexicon mechanically.

## 5. Cultural vocabulary as worldview evidence — calibrated

- Baseline: Sapir 1929 (language habits condition the "real world") and Whorf 1956 (Hopi time). Malotki 1983's Hopi Time rebutted the Hopi claim with hundreds of attested temporal expressions; Gumperz & Levinson 1996 re-established weak, domain-specific relativity as the defensible position.
- Verdict table:

| Claim type | Status | Usable in-world? |
|---|---|---|
| "Word count = degree of attention" | supported (salience→granularity, Conklin 1954) | yes, as inference from lexicon to cultural concern |
| "No word → cannot think it" | discredited as strong claim; lexicon absence is weak evidence (periphrasis fills gaps) | no |
| Color categories constrained by biology, modulated by language | supported both ways (Berlin & Kay 1969; WCS 2009; Winawer et al. 2007 Russian siniy/goluboy; vs. Saunders & van Brakel 1997; Kay & Kempton 1984) | yes, with both halves stated |
| Absolute-frame spatial language → constant orientation | supported (Levinson 2003, Guugu Yimithirr) | yes |
| Spatial-language time mapping | supported (Boroditsky & Gaby 2010, Kuuk Thaayorre) | yes |
| Number words as cognitive technology, not cognitive limit | supported (Gordon 2004 vs. Frank et al. 2008 — read both) | yes |
| Pirahã "grammar without recursion/numbers" | contested (Everett 2005 vs. Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues 2009) | cite the dispute if used at all |

- Lane rule: lexical elaboration is evidence of cultural attention; grammaticalized categories (evidentials, classifiers, obligatory honorifics) are stronger evidence than any word count; absence of a word proves nothing. Never let a character or document claim a language "has no way to say X" — that sentence is Malotki bait.

## 6. Decision table and worked method

| Culture profile | Deepen (3–4 fields) | Shallow is fine |
|---|---|---|
| riverine horticulture | subsistence, landscape, weather (flood stages), kin (lineage land) | war, color |
| steppe pastoral | subsistence (herd wealth), kin (clan), war (raid), landscape (pasture) | sea/coast terms (note the gap) |
| temple/ritual state | religion (register, evidentials), kin (Sudanese), body (soul-organs) | weather specifics |
| mercantile archipelago | trade/measures, weather, kin by marriage, war (escort) | inland fauna |
| conquest composite | war + costume strata from donor, subsistence split by class (Norman model) | — |

- Method: (1) write the Swadesh-100 floor and the derivation/periphrasis strategy for each item; (2) choose fields from the table; (3) coin depth terms only via logged routes (§3) from existing roots; (4) add dated loan strata with donor cultures checked against borrowability; (5) run the calibration pass — no snow-hoax counts, no absence-of-word claims, etymologies logged; (6) hand the resulting reference entries to lore-shokunin as material, not canon. Iterate field depth one axis at a time so improvement is attributable.

## 7. Calibration checklist

- Every coinage has a route (metaphor/metonymy/narrowing/loan) logged, or it is not done.
- Field sizes justified by culture profile, not by flex ("100 words for X" is a bug in your own notes).
- No glottochronological dates anywhere; stability language only ("core", "periphery").
- Kinship type chosen before kinship terms; evidential defaults chosen before scripture quotes.
- Unverifiable in-world etymologies marked to-verify in the lexicon itself.
- Worldview claims ride on grammaticalized categories first, elaboration second, absence never.
