## 1. The register property: a surrogate carries a spoken language on another channel

**Master claim.** A speech surrogate substitutes the utterances of one spoken language with other sounds, systematically and in the acoustic modality — what Sebeok and Umiker-Sebeok called "a true substitutive system, 2) a first-order rather than a second-order system, and 3) in the acoustic modality" (S134, quoting their 1976 definition; the 1976 volume itself was not opened this round). James's rendering: speech surrogacy is "systematically replacing utterances in 2) a spoken language with 3) other sounds" (S134). A whistled language is "secondary speech codes based on whistles anchored to, and derived from, a spoken language" (S135): Silbo Gomero (Spanish, La Gomera, Canary Islands), Mazatec and Chinantec (Oaxaca, Mexico) and the Surui (Rondônia, Brazil) each ride one spoken language (S135). Status: **B** for the systems the sources describe; the test column is the lane's design frame (**H**).

Stern's 1957 split sorts surrogates by what the signal encodes: phonological "abridgment" systems, where the carrier reduces the spoken language's own sounds, against "lexical ideograph" systems, where the signal "arbitrarily signify words, phrases, or perhaps concepts" with no connection to the phonology of the spoken language (S134, reporting Stern's terms; the page reference the source prints is unresolved `(to-verify)`). The abridging case is the one this entry owns: whistled speech is an abridgment of speech that encodes the essential phonology (S136), tied to the spoken language by acoustic iconicity (S135).

| Test | Pass when | Fails as (owner) |
|---|---|---|
| **Carries speech** | the signal replaces utterances of a spoken language with other sounds, systematically, in the acoustic modality (S134) | a signal code: a bugle call or a fixed drum signal has "no systematic connection to language", and Morse code refers "to an alphabet rather than to speech itself" (S134) |
| **Anchored in phonology** | the carrier reduces and re-shapes the spoken language's own sounds — abridgment plus acoustic iconicity (S136; S135) | a lexical-ideographic code: the signal fixes word or concept meanings without the spoken phonology (S134) |
| **Not self-standing** | the surrogate stays anchored to, and derived from, one spoken language (S135) | a sign language, which is "self-standing lexically, grammatically, and phonologically" (S135); the manual modality's register test stays at `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:14` |
| **Carries words, not a performance** | the signal carries words and propositions of the spoken language (S134) | music — the instrument register stays at `music-instrument-lexicon.md:98`; the drum and whistle channels at `phonetics-phonology.md:197` |
| **Secrecy in the channel** | the carrier hides the message by changing channel, not the vocabulary (S135: whistled speech is used "to speak in secret from foreigners") | a secret language — anti-languages and registers stay at `sociolinguistics-registers.md:84` |

## 3. Typology: channel, encoding and reach

| Channel | What is encoded | Typical reach | Example language and community (cited) | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Whistled speech, non-tonal language | Vowel quality as whistle pitch level; consonants in pitch modulations and in the amplitude envelope; no lexical tone to carry (S135) | Intelligible to about 2 km in good conditions; 10 km reported for La Gomera (S135; S137) | Spanish (Silbo Gomero, La Gomera, Canary Islands) (S135); the channel list is already owned at `corpus/knowledge/linguistics/phonetics-phonology.md:197-199` | Pitch is spent on vowels, so the whistled lexicon must dodge whistled homophones; the register is fitted to the language and the livelihood that needs it (S138); detail in §4 |

**Boundary: a code is not a surrogate.** Bugle calls, bells and fixed hunting-horn calls are
signal codes: a closed, taught repertoire with no tie to the speech it replaces; French
hunting-horn signals were named hunt events kept as an oral memory aid
(S142). Arbitrariness is not the test: enphrased phonemic messages also
become conventional (S140); bugle and bell cases stay (to-verify).

## 4. Whistled speech

A whistled register re-encodes the local spoken language instead of listing fixed messages, and what survives depends on whether that language is tonal; the ecology-to-register coupling and the drum-and-whistle music links stay with `phonetics-phonology.md:197-199`, `music-instrument-lexicon.md:98-100`, `orality-formulaic-language.md:114,:124` and `sound-symbolism.md:121` (S143; S144).

| System (language, community, place) | What the whistle keeps | What it drops | Distance and terrain |
|---|---|---|---|
| Silbo Gomero (Spanish of La Gomera, Canary Islands; herders and farmers, taught in school since 1999) | vowel quality as whistle pitch (F2 to H1) and consonant place as two loci above and below the vowel range; voiced and voiceless as continuous and interrupted whistle (S144; S185) | most of the Spanish inventory: the classroom model has two vowel slots (contested — Meyer's measurements give four statistically distinct bands) and /o/ merges with /u/ (S144; S143) | the island's ravines: messages carried over 2 to 3 km, heard as far as 8 km (S185; S146) |
| Whistled Turkish (Kuşköy, Görele valley, Black Sea; village and summer high-plateau herders) | eight vowels as eight pitch bands in four groups, disambiguated by vowel harmony; consonants as frequency and amplitude modulations, silence for stops (S147; S146) | band overlap between vowels, and the occlusive /p/ whistled "very similar to [k]" (S147) | Black Sea mountains: the whistle takes over where speech fails, from about 40 to 100 m depending on the terrain; valleys carry it kilometres (S148; S149) |
| Mazatec (Oaxaca highlands; four recorded whistled varieties: Tenango, Eloxochitlan de Flores Magon, Huautla, San Mateo) | the tonal system itself: registers and lexically, morphologically and syntactically significant glides (S144; S150) | no transposition of consonants or vowels; only suprasegmental traits reach the whistle (S143; S144) | Oaxaca mountain valleys: the same ease and intelligibility as speech, at close quarters and at a distance; distance figure (to-verify) (S150; S149) |
| Hmong (White Hmong; South-East Asia and its diaspora in Laos, Thailand, French Guiana) | tone melody: the whistle follows the spoken F0 contour, not the formants (S144) | nearly all vowel and consonant distinctions; only class-level alternation survives (S144) | hill and mountain country of the Hmong dispersal; distance (to-verify) (S135) |
| Chinantec (Sochiapam Chinantec, San Pedro Sochiápam, Oaxaca) | lexical tone: a rich tonal system for lexical load holds intelligibility even in unpredictable sentences (S135) | discourse length: whistled sentences tend to be simple, single-clause utterances, with equally simple conversational repairs (S135) | San Pedro Sochiápam, Oaxaca (S151; S155); distance (to-verify); an archive deposit records spoken and whistled Chinantec elicited from children in San Pedro Sochiápam (S151); the age at which the register is learned is (to-verify) |
| Siberian Yupik (not tonal) | a balance: the formant pull and stress intonation both count, so it sits between the segmental and tonal strategies (S143; S147) | the clean pitch scale a purely segmental whistle would give (S143) | Arctic; the corpus was collected with bilabial whistling in 2006; distance (to-verify) (S143) |

Decide the register before the lexicon: which tier the whistle carries — pitch standing for vowels (non-tonal), tone (tonal), or both (intermediate) — and which redundancy in the language will repair the loss (S143; S148). Status **B** for the attested systems, **H** for this rule (`../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`).
Typical failure (**H**): a whistle carrying only fixed signals such as "come here" or "danger" and no re-encoding of speech — that is a signal code, while a whistled language is an adjunct to speech rather than a replacement for it (S143; S150); the second failure is a tonal world given a segmental whistle, which drops exactly the contrasts that language exists to carry.

## 5. Encoding and ambiguity

A surrogate channel is a lossy channel, so the first decision is which features of the spoken language ride it and which are dropped; status codes: **B** for the attested measurements below, **H** for the design frame (`../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`).

| Feature the designer assigns | What rides the channel | What is lost | Repair, and the typical failure |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tone** | one instrument pitch per tonal level: in the Bora *manguaré* system of the northwest Amazon the talking mode uses two pitches, one on each drum (S139) | finer tonal structure — two notes cannot render downstep, as the Akan atumpan pair shows (`../civilizations/akan.md:58`), and for tonal bases the surrogate strips away segmental features to keep the tonal ones (S134) | stock phrases: a short word that would come out a drum homophone is replaced by a longer conventional expression (S139), as with the Kele people of the Stanleyville area of the Congo, whose 'manioc' is always drummed as 'the manioc which remains in the fallow ground' (S152); the failure is one melodic shape per event, which leaves no way to tell which message was meant (`../civilizations/yoruba.md:46-47`) |
| **Length** | pause length between beats; four rhythmic units match vowel-to-vowel intervals with different consonant counts and vowel lengths (S139) | the syllable as the unit — aligning beats with syllables, mora or vowel length alone gives inconsistent results (S139) | longer stereotyped phrases, whose extra length is what makes them identifiable (S152); the failure is a fixed beat-per-letter cipher, which no source documents and the lane forbids inventing (`../civilizations/fon-dahomey.md:46`) |
| **Rhythm** | the beat sequence and its timing, plus the conventional rhythmically distinct markers that separate words (S139) | segmental detail, dropped as the signal simplifies (S134) | framing and markers: messages conventionally carry an opening signal and a closing signal (S134); the failure is free composition, so no two drummers read the same sequence alike (**H**) |
| **Vowel quality** | in whistled speech the whistle's frequency stands in for the vowel's second formant, and vowels sit at different frequency levels with consonants as modulations around them (S153; S154) | the voice's complex spectrum: a whistle carries its high power in one band, 800 to 4000 Hz (S154) | a small fixed set of vowel-bearing forms whose contour carries the word (**H**); the failure is designing more vowel contrasts than the channel can hold (**H**) |
| **Consonant transitions** | in whistled speech, as frequency and amplitude modulations of the steadier vowel frequencies (S154); on the drum, only as a count of consonants inside a vowel-to-vowel interval (S139) | consonant place and manner, so drum homophones multiply (S139) | long epithets and conventional elaborations — enphrasing, the strategy that operates on roteness and elaborateness at once (S134); the failure is expecting the listener to recover the intended consonant from the melody alone (**H**) |

Four repairs, in the order a designer should apply them: stock phrases and long epithets, since the elaborateness of the Kele drum names is what differentiates the tonal patterns without ambiguity (S152; S134); repetition, because the drum form is much longer "because of the repetition necessary to make the meaning clear" (S152); restriction to a few topics, since context is "an important tool for combatting the problem of ambiguity, since it allows listeners to use context to their advantage" (S134); and call-and-answer repair: in the whistled register of San Pedro Sochiapam Chinantec, Oaxaca, the open repair format is favoured over the restricted one, which Sicoli attributes to a reduced semiotic carrying capacity of the whistled channel (S155), and the attested failures were repaired with a limited set of standard questions and interjections (S134). The couplings that place a surrogate in its world stay with `phonetics-phonology.md:197-199` and `../culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md:98-100`; the formula broadcast layer with `orality-formulaic-language.md:114` and `orality-formulaic-language.md:124`; the music row with `sound-symbolism.md:121`. Test the result the lane's own way: compare phrases that share a tonal pattern, add conventional expansions or contextual cues, and see whether trained listeners recover the intended message (`../civilizations/fon-dahomey.md:45`).
Rate cost: the channel carries less than the voice — whistled languages "encode less information from which to identify the intended speech sounds than voiced speech" (S153) — and no surrogate channel in these sources is measured in bits per second, so the yardstick is the spoken one, 39.15 bits/s on average across 17 languages (S156); the designer buys the loss back with redundancy and with a trained listener, since in Busnel's whistled Turkish experiments isolated words were recognized at about 70% against 80–90% for common whistled sentences (S154).

## 6. Ecology and function

| Condition that favours a surrogate | Cited case | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Mountain, ravine and dense-forest terrain | Silbo Gomero, La Gomera (inscribed 2009): messages carried "over a distance of up to five kilometres" on an island cut by "a network of very deep ravines" between "population clusters" (S184); whistled languages are "mostly used in mountains or dense forests" (S158) | Set the reach budget before the phonology: the distance decides how many contrasts the channel can carry |
| Dispersed settlement | Turkish Black Sea villages, whose houses are "remote and inaccessible from one another" (S159); UNESCO ties the practice to terrain that "required the local population to find an alternative way to communicate across long distances" (S163) | Map the settlement scatter first; the channel exists exactly where face-to-face contact fails |
| Herding and farming at distance | Turkish whistling now survives in "a few words spoken by shepherds" (S159); Banda-Linda drums reach people "working their farms" (S162) | Give the channel a working vocabulary (weather, livestock, harvest) before any elegance; the register link itself is owned by phonetics-phonology.md:197-199 |
| Hunting | Drum messages organise "assistance after a hunting accident" and carry instructions when "someone gets lost in the bush" (S160, S162) | Write the emergency set as fixed formulae; a free-text channel fails under panic |
| Courtship and private errands | Kuşköy whistled "akşama bize gel" ("come to our place this evening"), whistled by a girl (S161); routine courtship use (to-verify) | Separate public calls from private ones: the same channel that reaches a village also exposes the sender |
| Announcements and alarms | Turkish whistling carries "warning and SOS or calling for help", for example "There is a forest fire!" (S159) | Fix the alarm set as stock phrases and decide who may send them |
| Ritual and praise | Banda-Linda drums announce "the investiture of a chief" and "the consecration of new drums" (S160) | Tie the surrogate to one rite and one prestige genre; praise poetry itself stays with orality-formulaic-language.md:124 |
| Secrecy from outsiders | Drum tone codes keep their "mysteriousness" for listeners "unacquainted with it" (S162) | Decide the audience: outsiders barred means a restricted code and restricted bearers; outsiders welcome means a small public code |

The typical failure is a surrogate designed for the wrong distance: built for the village it breaks in the ravine, built for the ravine it dies when the settlement concentrates, as the Turkish whistled language, with mobile phones now one of its main threats, is "about to lose its communication function" (S159).

## 7. Who may speak: social control of the channel

A surrogate is never a neutral wire: in every attested case below the channel is an office, a rule or a school, and the design consequence column is what a designer must fix (design frame, **H**).

| control | attested case | design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| court and royal office | Yorùbá (south-west Nigeria) drumming families carry the hereditary title *baale* or *aare onilu* (chief drummer) in patronage with *oba* (sacred king) families (S164); in Ghana, one master drummer's biography says "by seventeen he was appointed Royal Court Drummer to the high chief of the Aburi-Akuapim region" (S165), and Aburi court drummers are described as people "who fiercely resist change in repertoire and performance practice yet accept it in the realm of performance context" (S166) | make the channel an office with a title, a patron and a duty of announcement; an unnamed skill has nobody to answer for it, and a court posting can be lost |
| historian and praise poet | the same Yorùbá offices keep the ruler's lineage history and the praise names of the ruler and his ancestors, delivered in the *oriki* genre, greet visitors at the palace gates and direct the ruler's processions (S164); the Àyàn lineage of Ọ̀yọ́ heralds the king's arrival and titles its masters *Otun onilu Alaafin* (S167) | bind the surrogate to a text corpus — genealogies, praise names — and decide who owns that text; praise that nobody may revise is propaganda, not memory |
| gendered or age-restricted use | in Yorùbá drum poetry "no woman is found and even there has not been women apprentice for drumming", while recent formal training has produced women drummers (S168); the Àyàn line is patrilineal, though female master drummers now exist (S167); in Kuşköy (Turkey) a woman still learned the whistle language from her grandparents (S169) | fix the default speaker set; a rule that bars half the speakers is a dated design choice, and lifting it is a historical event, not a correction |
| secrecy and eavesdropping | in Yorùbá practice only initiates may beat the *ìpese* ensemble and only initiates understand it (S168); the older Yorùbá drums carry restrictions the dùndún does not (S164); in Kuşköy "The only thing you never whistle is your love talk" because "you'll get caught" (S169) | design an audience ladder — initiates, elders, office, public — and a content taboo list; since an overheard surrogate is a plot instrument, choose inaudibility deliberately rather than by accident |
| training and apprenticeship | Yorùbá drumming is "a family profession, which requires skill and perfection that cannot be attained without proper tutelage" (S170); an Àyàn novice works up the whole dùndún drum family before "graduating" to *Ìyáàlù Dùndún* (S167); La Gomera mandated the Gomeran whistle in primary and secondary schools in 1999 and requires accredited teachers (S171) | choose the transmission mode — household, guild, school or state — because that choice decides whether the channel outlives one generation |

The drum-and-whistle register couplings stay with `phonetics-phonology.md:197-199`, `../culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md:98-100`, `orality-formulaic-language.md:114,:124` and `sound-symbolism.md:121`; this section owns who holds the channel, not what the channel encodes.

5. Test one full message end-to-end against the §10 calibration bank before expanding the vocabulary. *Artifact: one verified message and its gloss.*

## 9. NON-CANON worked micro-example

**NON-CANON.** The Coldwater ravine culture, its language (Coldwater, tonal, spoken by herding households that keep goats and sheep above the tree line), its places, its phrases and every number below are invented; nothing here describes a real people, and canon belongs to lore-shokunin. Walked through the §8 decision table: channel — two, a two-note whistle and a hide drum, because the ravine walls carry pitch further than they carry rhythm (§6; design frame, **H**); encoding — the whistle keeps the language's lexical tone and vowel length and reduces consonants to transitions, which a tonal language can afford because pitch level characterizes the syllable (S172; §4), while the drum carries pause length and a closed set of stock phrases, because four rhythmic units ride the pauses and tone alone separates few words (S139; §5, §5); stock phrases — 24 fixed herding formulas, one drum pattern each, drawn from the oral formula banks (`orality-formulaic-language.md:114`, `:124`); who may speak it — whistling is open to every adult of the herding households, drumming is licensed to the herd-criers and is the channel outsiders must not overhear (§7; performer licensing stays at `orality-formulaic-language.md:112`); decline driver — the new valley road brings a lorry line and a school that teaches the valley's trade language, so the whistle stops being learned and only the drum formulas survive as ritual (§8). The failure to design around is a drum that carries only a closed list of calls, which would be a signal code rather than a surrogate (§1, §3). The tone-and-drum couplings stay with `phonetics-phonology.md:197-199`, instrument naming with `../culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md:98-100`, and channel iconicity with `sound-symbolism.md:121`.

| "the goats are up at the north spring" | whistle: three notes, level, rising, level; the mid tone of the second word is the only cue that separates it from its neighbour |
| "the herd moves to the upper pasture tonight" | drum: six beats with a long pause before the fourth, the pause pattern that marks the stock phrase; no melody and no pitch |
| "how many sheep are left?" | whistle: rising, then falling, the last note held twice as long, the contour that asks for a count |

## 10. Calibration bank

Codes per `../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39` (R rejected, C contested, B bounded, H heuristic, T to-verify).

| Claim | Status | What to say instead |
|---|---|---|
| A drum or whistle code is a separate language | R | it is a surrogate register of a named spoken language — Meyer 2021: whistled speech "does not replace but instead complements modal speech" (S173); UNESCO: Silbo "replicates the islanders' habitual language (Castilian Spanish) with whistling" (S157); Stern's abridged vs lexical-ideogram split covers both types (S174) |
| Whistled speech needs a tonal language | R | no — Silbo Gomero transposes Castilian Spanish, a non-tonal language (S157), and non-tonal languages (Greek, Turkish, Spanish, Béarnese, Wayãpi, Tamazight) use formant-based whistling (S173); tone changes the strategy, not the existence |
| Drum messages travel hundreds of miles in minutes as a 'bush telegraph' | C | the relay is attested but the figure is not: one drum is audible 3–4 miles by day and 10–15 at night; a drum heard at 25 miles was no longer intelligible (TIME 1942/Good, S175); relayed village-to-village, "a hundred miles or more in a matter of an hour" (Gleick, S176) |
| Silbo Gomero descends from the Guanche language | C | UNESCO describes the inscribed practice as transposing Castilian Spanish (S157); the Canarian government asserts pre-Hispanic continuity of the practice (S177); a direct descent of today's Silbo from Guanche is not established by these sources |
| Every tonal-language culture drums its language | R | drum-speech is documented for named communities and instruments (S179, S174); that most tonal languages have none is (to-verify); tone is at most a favourable coding condition — the coupling stays at `phonetics-phonology.md:199-200` |
| There are more than X whistled languages worldwide | B | quote the counting rule with the number: "more than eighty … reported … around forty … attested" (Meyer 2021, S173); his 2017 figure was "more than 70" in a 13-year survey (S180) |
| 'Jungle telegraph' / 'bush telegraph' is a neutral technical name | R | it is an English idiom, "First recorded in 1875–80", whose dictionary sense speaks of "the natives of a jungle or bush region" sending news "by runners, drum codes, or smoke signals" (S181); a 1958 TIME report says a medical team "got native chieftains to pass the word by jungle telegraph" (S182) — name the community's own system instead |
| A talking drum is a lossless code | R | the drum encodes tone and rhythm, not segments (S179, S139), and leans on stock phrases whose extra length makes them identifiable (S152) — say a bounded tone-and-rhythm code with set phrases |

**Living-practice flag.** These are living practices of named communities — Gomeran whistlers on La Gomera (silbo gomero, taught in schools since 1999, S157), the whistled language of the Black Sea region of Türkiye (transmitted parent-child, inscribed 2017 on the Urgent Safeguarding List, S183), Yorùbá dùndún/gángan drummers (S179) — a fictional culture borrows mechanisms, never a community's identity; this lane rules on no heritage or legal question.

**Precision traps.** Whistled speech is a register of a named spoken language, not a second language (S173, S157); audibility is not intelligibility (S175); counts depend on the counting standard (S173, S180).

## 12. Scope

This entry is reference method and lane policy for the linguistics partition. It owns speech surrogates as a channel design problem — what a whistle, drum or other instrument can carry of one spoken language, how ambiguity is repaired, the ecology that favours a channel, and who may use it. It hands the instruments and ceremonial registers to `../culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md`, the tone and inventory couplings to `phonetics-phonology.md`, the manual modality to `sign-languages-manual-modality.md`, and making game sound to audio-shokunin. It makes no heritage-policy, clinical or legal ruling about any living community, and borrows mechanisms, never a community's identity.

Handoff: lore-shokunin decides which surrogates exist in a world, who holds them and what they have said; this entry ends at the design record (DR-SRG-01 to DR-SRG-05) and the calibration bank. It never decides canon.
