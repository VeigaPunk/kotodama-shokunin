## 1. The one idea

Contact is the cheapest realism lever in worldbuilding: you can write one people's entire history into the shape of another people's words. Two mechanisms, opposite signatures:

- **Borrowing** (recipient-language agentivity; van Coetsem 1988): speakers KEEP their language and import items from a source (trade, prestige, conquest administration). Words arrive first, structure last.
- **Imposition / shift-induced interference** (source-language agentivity; Thomason & Kaufman 1988): speakers ABANDON their language for a target and carry their old phonology and syntax into it. Structure leaks first, words least.

Everything else in this file hangs off that asymmetry.

## 2. The master asymmetry (use this first)

| | Borrowing (language kept) | Substratum (language shifted) |
|---|---|---|
| Transfers most | nouns, cultural vocabulary | phonology (accent), word order, aspect/usage patterns |
| Transfers least | inflectional morphology, pronouns, basic numerals | vocabulary |
| Historical trigger | prestige, trade, religion, administration | conquest, slavery, migration, assimilation policy |
| Fictional read | "we took their *things-words*" | "we speak their language with our mouth" |

Worked contrast: English after 1066 kept Germanic grammar while flooding with French/Latin lexicon (borrowing). Irish English and Indian English keep few source-language words but show substrate syntax/idiom — Irish "after-perfect" (*I'm after doing it*), Indian English focus particle *only* and invariant tag *isn't it* (shift). Same colonizer, opposite signatures, because the sociolinguistic event differed. Cf. Dawkins (1916) for the extreme shift case: some Asia Minor Greek varieties borrowed Turkish **inflectional verbal morphology** — the textbook ceiling of contact depth.

## 3. How deep can it cut: the Thomason–Kaufman intensity scale

Adapted from Thomason & Kaufman (1988: ch. 2). Their core claim: **depth of borrowing is set by sociolinguistic intensity and duration of bilingualism, not by grammar** — no category is unborrowable in principle, but the price rises steeply.

| Intensity | What passes | Typical situation |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Casual | content words, esp. cultural/technical items; almost nothing in basic vocab | sporadic trade, neighboring peoples |
| 2. Slightly more | more content words; conjunctions and adverbial particles (*only, also, too*); phonological effects confined to loans | regular trade, intermarriage begins |
| 3. Moderate | function words (prepositions, adverbs); pronoun borrowing begins (still rare); calques; derivational affixes enter | sustained bilingual border zones, elite second-language learning |
| 4. Intense | heavy lexical borrowing incl. basic vocabulary; new phonemes in **native** words; word order shifts; borrowed derivational affixes become productive | long-term widespread bilingualism, religious conversion, intensive literacy |
| 5. Very intense | everything above; **inflectional morphology** borrowed (Asia Minor Greek, Dawkins 1916) | submersion, slavery, dense mixed communities |

Design rule: pick a rung per contact pair per era, and let the artifact vocabulary honor the rung. A casual-trade neighbor language should never contribute a pronoun; if your story needs one, write the history that earned it (see §10, Norse pronouns).

## 4. Borrowability hierarchies — and how much to trust them

Two generations of scales; both are tendencies, not laws (Matras 2009; Tadmor et al. 2010):

| Classic implicational scale (often credited to Whitman 1970; preserved in Thomason & Kaufman 1988) | Matras (2009) revision, from multilingual-corpus data |
|---|---|
| nouns > adjectives > verbs > adverbs > prepositions > conjunctions > pronouns > interjections | **discourse markers, interjections, focus particles (*well, just, only*) borrow FIRST** (they escape bilingual monitoring); then nouns; then function items; inflectional morphology last; pronouns mainly under shift/extreme contact |

WOLD numbers back the classic noun>verb core: 31.2% of sampled noun meanings are loans vs. 15.2% of adjectives and 14% of verbs (Tadmor et al. 2010). Matras's counter-intuition explains why a fantasy culture's casual speech is dotted with foreign discourse tags ("vedra, the wine is dead") long before it takes a foreign verb.

- Derivational affixes can become productive after a heavy stratum: English -able/-tion (Norman), Sinoxenic -ka "ize" in Japanese. Inflection almost never does — treat inflection-borrowing as a story beat (see rung 5).
- **Calibration:** the hierarchies are sample-based averages; prestige, religion and literacy override word class (Matras 2009). Arabic verbs into Swahili, Sanskrit anything into Old Javanese — prestige strata break the ladder upward.

## 5. What the numbers say (World Loanword Database)

WOLD sampled 1,460 meanings in 41 languages (Haspelmath & Tadmor 2009; findings in Tadmor 2009; summary in Tadmor et al. 2010):

- Mean borrowing rate **≈24%** of the list; **no language in the sample is loan-free**.
- Spread: Selice Romani ≈63%, Tarifiyt Berber ≈52%, Gurindji ≈46%, Romanian ≈42%, Saramaccan ≈42%, English ≈41%, Japanese ≈35% … down to Mandarin Chinese ≈4%.
- Nouns (31.2%) > adjectives (15.2%) > verbs (14%). Agriculture/vegetation, food/drink and animals run ≈25–30%; the most resistant meanings are low numerals, close kin terms, core body parts (ear, eye, blood), perceptual verbs (see, hear) and basic pronouns — yet every item is borrowed in at least one sampled language.

**Worldbuilder calibration (counter-intuitive):** the highest borrowers in the real sample are not imperial superpowers — they are multilingual minorities under sustained pressure (Selice Romani) and trade-saturated societies (English). A marginal diaspora people in your world should out-borrow the empire. Isolation (≈4% loans, Mandarin-style) is what insularity looks like in the lexicon.

## 6. Loanword adaptation: making the donor fit the mouth

| Strategy | Mechanism | Attested example |
|---|---|---|
| Substitution | nearest native phoneme replaces donor sound | English *th* → /s/ (Japanese *disu* "this") |
| Epenthesis | vowels inserted to fit recipient syllable template | *strike* → Japanese *sutoraiku*; *McDonald's* → *Makudonarudo* (Shibatani 1990); Korean *aiseu keurim* "ice cream" |
| Coda repair | final clusters simplified or released | coda clusters broken in Korean/Japanese loans; simplified in many Bantu borrowings |
| Gemination | final consonant doubled to mark loan as foreign/fit prosody | final gemination in Hausa English loans (Newman 2000) |
| Nasal assimilation | borrowed nasal re-places to following consonant | Portuguese *tempero* → Japanese *tempura* (with semantic drift to the dish) |
| Prosody refooting | donor stress flattened to recipient rhythm | French *week-end* pronounced with final stress [wikɛnd] |
| Orthographic adaptation | donor spelling read with native letter values (dominant in literate, globalized eras) | modern loan floods worldwide |

Two principles worth their weight in canon:

1. **The recipient's phonology does the filtering** — adaptation runs through native phonotactic "filters", sometimes producing phonological illusions (Kang 2003; Peperkamp 2005).
2. **The adaptation freezes the donor's phonology at borrowing time** — this is the hinge of stratigraphy (§10).

Beyond single words: **calques** (loan translations) copy meanings without sounds — English *skyscraper* → French *gratte-ciel*, German *Wolkenkratzer*, Spanish *rascacielos*; and grammar itself can be replicated through calqued constructions (Heine & Kuteva 2005). Semantic adaptation drifts: narrowing, pejoration, and **pseudo-loans** coined inside the borrower — Japanese *sumāto* "slim", *manshon* "upscale apartment", German *Handy* "mobile phone" (Furiassi et al. 2012). Pseudo-loans are a superb era-marker: they only exist after the source culture's prestige peaks.

## 7. Pidgins and creoles

### 7.1 Definitions and life cycle

- **Pidgin**: contact variety with **no native speakers** — reduced lexicon (hundreds to low thousands of items), little or no inflection, multifunction particles, reduplication, serial verbs, little subordination (Arends et al. 1995).
- **Creole**: a pidgin **nativized** — learned as a first language by a new generation, which expands and grammaticalizes it (Hall 1966, the "life cycle": jargon → stable pidgin → creole → post-creole continuum, DeCamp 1971).
- Expansion without nativization also happens: Tok Pisin, now an official language of Papua New Guinea, grammaticalized *bai* "will" (< *by and by*) and *-pinis* completive (< *finish*). Nativization is a sociological fact, not a clean linguistic switch.

Real reference points by sociohistory: trade/barter — Russonorsk (Russian–Norwegian Arctic barter, 18th–19th c.), Chinuk Wawa (Pacific Northwest fur trade); military/fort — Chabacano/Zamboangueño (Spanish lexifier, Philippines); plantation — Haitian Creole (French/Gbe-Kikongo), Sranan Tongo (English lexifier, Suriname); maroon — Saramaccan (Suriname; hardest break with the lexifier); labor/mine — Fanagalo (Zulu lexifier, southern African mines); riverine/indigenous lexifier — Kituba and Lingala (Congo basin), Nagamese (Assamese lexifier, Naga Hills), Hiri Motu (Papua).

### 7.2 Genesis debate (calibration — contested ground)

| Theory | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Universalist bioprogram (Bickerton 1984) | children nativizing a pidgin default to an innate grammar; creoles sample human defaults | strong form largely abandoned |
| Substratist | substrate languages supply the syntax (Gbe structures in Atlantic creoles) | substantial evidence per case; not a single mechanism |
| Superstratist/gradualist (Chaudenson 2001; Arends 1989 on Sranan) | creoles grow out of second-language approximations of the lexifier's *dialects*, over decades, not abruptly | well supported historically |
| Relexification (Lefebvre 1998) | lexifier words slotted into substrate lexical entries | influential for Haitian; heavily criticized as a general theory |
| Ordinary mechanisms (Thomason & Kaufman 1988) | creolization needs no special device: extreme borrowing + shift under social rupture | the conservative mainstream position |

The loud open fight: McWhorter (1998) argues creoles are typologically the world's simplest grammars; DeGraff (2001, 2005) attacks "creole exceptionalism" as ideology and denies creoles form a special synchronic class. **No consensus.** For your world: a creole is a language like any other — if characters call it "broken X", write that as their prejudice, not as your setting's physics. Also note creoles break the family-tree model: calling Haitian "a French dialect" is a political act, and that is a usable story.

## 8. Mixed languages: the edge case that is NOT a creole

Genuinely intertwined systems, grammar from one language and lexicon from another, arising from **group identity** (new people, in-group secrecy) rather than trade necessity (Bakker & Muysken 1995, in Arends et al. 1995; Thomason ed. 1997):

- **Michif**: Cree polysynthetic verbs + French noun phrases (Bakker 1997) — the language of a new Métis nation.
- **Media Lengua**: Quechua grammar + Spanish lexicon (Muysken, in Thomason ed. 1997).
- **Mednyj Aleut**: Aleut grammar + Russian verbs (same volume).
- **Ma'a/Mbugu**: Bantu structure + Cushitic lexicon (discussed in Thomason & Kaufman 1988).
- **Cappadocian Greek**: Greek structure under massive Turkish lexicon (Dawkins 1916).

Fiction move: a founding people, guild, or secret society that forged its own tongue is historically plausible — but rare and emblematic. Do not scatter them.

## 9. Sprachbünde (linguistic areas): convergence without kinship

A **Sprachbund** is a region where unrelated (or distantly related) languages converge on shared features through long-term diffusion — features are shared, not words alone; genealogy is the wrong tree for the job.

| Area | Defining features | Sources |
|---|---|---|
| Balkans | postposed definite article; infinitive loss (finite subordinate clauses); future built on 'want' (Romanian, Greek *tha*, Bulgarian *šte*); dative–genitive merger; clitic doubling | Sandfeld 1930; Joseph 1983 |
| South Asia | retroflex consonants across Indo-Aryan, Dravidian, Munda; SOV + postpositions; conjunctive participle "having-done"; dative-subject experiencers ("to-me cold-is"); echo-word compounds (*chai-vai*) | Emeneau 1956; Masica 1976 |
| Mesoamerica | vigesimal numerals; relational nouns from body parts; body-part metaphor compounds; calques like "its-foot its-sole" = 'sole of foot'; non-verb-final order | Campbell, Kaufman & Smith-Stark 1986 |
| Ethiopia | quotative 'say'-verbs; 'say/do' compound verbs; SOV; shared ejectives | Ferguson 1976 |
| Pacific Northwest (brief) | lexical suffixes and reduplication patterns as areal traits | Sherzer 1976 |

Calibration: the concept is under critique — family-tree thinking and diffusion thinking fight over every case (Aikhenvald & Dixon 2001; Dixon's 1997 "punctuated equilibrium" is itself contested), Balkan causes are still argued (Greek koine influence vs. mutual reinforcement; Lindstedt 2000), and the East Asian "Sinosphere" (Sinitic loan strata, classifiers, honorific systems across CJK+Vietnamese) is a real geolinguistic areal label (coined in Matisoff 1991) whose membership and usefulness are still debated (Enfield 2005; Post 2011). **Drawing instruction:** on a fantasy map, draw Sprachbünde as shaded gradients and overlapping isogloss bundles, never as bordered blocks; features accumulate around markets, shrines, and courts, and leak along roads.

## 10. Stratigraphy: dating fictional history with loan strata

Method — a loanword is a fossil of the donor language **as it sounded when borrowed**; later donor-side changes do not apply to it. Corollaries:

1. Loan shows donor's **old** sound ⇒ borrowed **before** that change (dates the contact event as *terminus ante quem* for the change).
2. Loan shows donor's **new** sound ⇒ borrowed after the change.
3. Loan fitted to recipient's **old** phonotactics ⇒ contact predates the recipient's change; raw unadapted loans in a literate era ⇒ contact postdates wide literacy.
4. **Function words and pronouns** in the loan layer ⇒ the maximal-intimacy period (mass bilingualism or shift), never casual trade.
5. **Semantic fields layer by event type**: trade → food/cloth/measures; conquest → war/law/administration; mission → religion/soul/moral vocabulary; bureaucracy → titles and paper-words; a **purism reform** (deliberate purge of old strata) stamps a hard before/after seam inside one language.

Worked real cases to imitate:

| Language | Strata readable in its lexicon | Signal |
|---|---|---|
| Japanese | Sinitic in three readings of the same characters: go-on (Buddhism via Korea, 5th–6th c.), kan-on (Tang court standard, 7th–9th c.), tō-on (Zen and Song–Ming trade, 12th c.+); e.g. 行 = *gyō / kō / an*; then Portuguese (16th c.: *pan* < *pão*, *tempura* < *tempero*), Dutch (Edo learning: *biru*, *kōhī*, *garasu*), post-Meiji English | each reading-stratum = one contact era with its own prestige agent (Shibatani 1990) |
| English | Celtic substrate (mostly place-names and river names — few common words); early Latin (*wine* < *vīnum*, *wall* < *vallum* keep /w/ = borrowed while Latin still said [w]); Old Norse (*skirt* next to native *shirt*; **pronouns they/them/their** — a mass-settlement marker); Anglo-Norman vs. Central French doublets (*ward/guard*, *cattle/chattel*, *warranty/guarantee* — two French waves); Renaissance Latin/Greek; global flood | field + sound-shape + doublets date each layer (Baugh & Cable 2002; Celtic-syntax debate in Filppula et al. 2008) |
| Romani | digits 1–6/7 Indo-Aryan, but 8 and 9 are Greek (*oxto* < Greek *oktṓ*, *enya*) — in all dialects | numerals trace the Byzantine sojourn and route of migration (Matras 2009; Elšík & Matras 2006; further localized numeral loans per dialect) |
| Saramaccan | English-lexifier core relexified toward Portuguese, plus later Dutch loans | three colonial phases stacked in one lexicon (Goodman 1987) |
| Turkish | dense Arabic–Persian Ottoman stratum purged by the 1930s Dil Devrimi | a living generational register split: old speakers' vocabulary vs. reform vocabulary (Lewis 1999) |
| Hindi/Urdu | one colloquial base split by politics into Sanskritized Hindi and Persianized Urdu scripts and strata | stratum choice = identity badge (King 1994) |

Statistical echo for registers: by dictionary *types*, English is ≈28% French and ≈28% Latin vs. ≈25% Germanic (Finkenstaedt & Wolff 1973), yet running speech is dominated by Germanic core items. Translation for worldbuilding: **the learned stratum shows in high register, law, and the dictionary; the deepest stratum shows in the market and the mouth.**

Fiction toolkit: place a fort's name in stratum 2, let the army speak stratum 3 calques, give priests stratum-4 soul-words, and make the capital's youth coin pseudo-loans from stratum 5 — then have a scholar character reconstruct the city's conquest history from exactly these layers, and let them be slightly wrong, as real philologists were.

## 11. Design moves and failure modes

Moves:

1. Choose a contact **type** per pair: trade (field-specific nouns, rung 1–2), conquest elite (law/war strata + prestige register, rung 3), substrate shift (accent + syntax calques, few words), Sprachbund neighbors (shared grammar across genealogies), founding mixture (mixed language, §8).
2. Build the loan **ladder in time**: adapted trade nouns → learned religious stratum (calques + loans) → unadapted written administrative stratum → raw media flood. Each rung is an era stamp.
3. Encode class in adaptation: elites keep unadapted or pseudo- foreign forms; commoners' speech digests them (*sumāto*). Purists legislate; speakers ignore.
4. Rivers, hills, and old market names keep the deepest stratum — toponymy survives conquest (Celtic hydronyms across England).
5. Four settlements, four mechanisms from §7–§8: free-port pidgin, plantation creole, decreolizing capital, guild mixed tongue — instantly distinct histories.

Failure modes:

- Loans that ignore the recipient's phonotactics (every stratum-1 loan should look mouth-shaped for its borrowers).
- Casual borrowing of pronouns, inflections, or numerals 1–5 (violates §3–§5; knowing readers will feel it).
- No strata at all: a world where every foreign word arrived "long ago, somehow" reads as ahistorical texture.
- Sprachbund drawn as a bordered block with a uniform feature list, instead of overlapping isoglosses.
- Using "creole = simple/broken" as narrator truth — contested scholarship (§7.2); if it appears, it belongs to in-world bigots or dated in-world scholars.
- Confusing borrowing and shift signatures (§2) — the most common worldbuilding slip: a conquered-and-assimilated people should sound like their old language wearing a new one, not wear a heavy loan costume.
