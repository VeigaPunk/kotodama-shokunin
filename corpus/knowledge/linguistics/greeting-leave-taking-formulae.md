## 1. Scope and the four sourced cases

This entry is a design-and-method reference for the two ends of an encounter. It treats greeting and leave-taking as one family of speech acts, records what four sources report about the politeness and the access rituals that surround them, and ends with a procedure for building a fictional greeting register (design, not sourced).

It asserts nothing about canon, about lore acceptance, about real-world rights, or about cultural permission; whether any particular greeting practice may be used by a fictional people is a decision for a canon layer, not for this corpus (design, not sourced).

The Swiss case treats greeting and leave-taking as a single politeness type inside a study of linguistic politeness in German-speaking Switzerland, and it is the source that supplies the family framing this entry builds on (S961).

The Spanish case analyses two greeting formulae built on the atelic predicate for kissing the hands and the feet, and its interest lies in the fact that two formulae of nearly identical surface shape end up carrying opposite socio-pragmatic values (S962).

The Tanzanian case is an ethnographic description of access rituals among the Ngoni of Tanzania, and it is the source that shows the greeting repertoire moving under pervasive contact with Swahili (S963).

The Russian case is a pilot study of leave-taking behaviour in contemporary Russian, and it is the source that supplies the elicitation method and the situational variables behind the farewell end of the family (S964).

Read together, the four cases are not a typology and are not claimed to be one; they are four documented instances, and the four sections above give each one its own section for exactly that reason (design, not sourced).

## 2. Greeting and leave-taking as one speech-act family

The strongest single reason to treat greeting and leave-taking together rather than apart is lexical: Rash's object of study is "the speech acts of greeting and leave-taking denoted by the German verb" (S961).

One verb in that source language therefore covers both ends of an encounter, which is the family fact this entry takes as its starting point (S961).

The paper locates that family inside politeness research rather than inside greeting research, so the two ends are treated as one type of politeness rather than as two separate conversational genres (S961).

Rash reports that German-speaking Swiss people adhere to strict conventions of polite behaviour, and the abstract adds that such conventions have been eroded over time in the English-speaking world (S961).

The reported attitude inside the population is that speakers of Swiss German, young and old and from all walks of life, believe it is important to retain their traditional politeness rituals, and that greetings formulae are especially important within that retention (S961).

The stated reason is not self-interest: very few Germanophone Swiss think that people conform to polite greeting practices out of self-interest, and the belief reported instead is that politeness matters for social cohesion and as a sign of respect and affection for one's fellow human beings (S961).

Because one verb covers both ends, a designer who wants a symmetrical encounter can start from the assumption that the two acts are cognate, and a designer who wants asymmetry can deliberately split what one language keeps in a single item (design, not sourced).

Because the retention is reported to be a matter of cohesion and of respect, a fictional greeting register can be specified as a mechanism for marking belonging rather than as a formality observed for its own sake (design, not sourced).

Because the family is retained as a unit, a fictional culture can lose one end of the encounter without losing the other, and the interesting cases are the ones where only one end survives (design, not sourced).

## 3. Form and meaning in the kissing-hands pair

Zieliński's subject is two Spanish greeting formulae derived from the atelic predicate for kissing the hands and the feet, and the analytic problem he sets is that "despite their apparent formal resemblance, came to codify totally opposite" socio-pragmatic values (S962).

The direction of the evidence is diachronic and textual, because the analysis proceeds by systematic search in epistolary texts up to the beginnings of the twentieth century (S962).

What that corpus supports is a history of two formulae rather than a description of how Spanish speakers greet today (S962).

Three questions structure the analysis, and they are worth borrowing as a checklist because they separate the layers a design has to decide: which socio-pragmatic factors play the most important role in each type of greeting, what the paradigmatic origin of each formula is, and how the formulae are distributed across social and cultural groups (S962).

The paradigmatic-origin question is the one a designer cannot skip, because a shared predicate can sit underneath two formulae that no longer carry the same social meaning (S962).

The distribution question is equally load-bearing, because the sociocultural distribution of a formula is one of the three things the case sets out to establish (S962).

Reading present-day Spanish greeting practice off this source would exceed what the evidence carries, since the corpus searched is epistolary and bounded (S962).

That limit is what makes the case useful for design rather than misleading for it: a fictional greeting register can borrow the lesson that form and meaning come apart, but it cannot borrow the historical Spanish distribution as a template for a real community's usage (design, not sourced).

A second design reading follows from the split-value structure: if two formulae of near-identical shape can carry opposite force, then a fictional register can hold a pair of formulae that look alike and must be told apart by circumstance alone, which is a harder and more economical way to build a greeting (design, not sourced).

## 4. Politeness and access rituals under contact

Mapunda and Sommer's frame is that the object is Tanzanian Ngoni access rituals, described ethnographically and taken to include the verbal and the visual aspects of the communicative routines that make them up (S963).

Because the abstract records both the verbal and the visual aspects of those routines, the access ritual is not only a spoken formula (S963).

The methods are three, and each answers a different question: role-play, semi-structured interview and observation, with the role-plays recorded on a digital camcorder after the interviews had taken place and the observational notes written immediately after each exchange (S963).

The division of labour between the methods is itself part of the finding: the role-play elicits the ritual as the community acquired it while growing up, the semi-structured interview is aimed at diachronic developments such as how the expression of politeness through greetings may have changed, and the observation captures the behavioural patterns actually shown during greeting exchanges (S963).

The reported main findings are that, because of pervasive contact between the Ngoni and Swahili, "greetings tend to be brief today", and that there is widespread code-switching across all age groups (S963).

A repertoire compressed by contact is a repertoire whose formulae have been reduced rather than elaborated, so a fictional culture under contact pressure can be designed to show the same reduction (design, not sourced).

The generational split over interpretation is the second half of the finding: the tendency to prefer Swahili is viewed by older members of the community as a lack of respect, whereas the younger generations regard the use of Swahili as more appropriate and as a reflection of social changes that have been taking place in the community (S963).

That is the contact-driven shift this entry's topic names, and it has two separable components: the form of the repertoire changes, and the assessment of that form is renegotiated between generations (S963).

A fictional greeting register can therefore be specified as a shifting system rather than a stable one, with a current short form, a longer inherited form, and a stated disagreement between an older and a younger reading of which one is respectful (design, not sourced).

It can also be specified so that the shift is not evenly distributed across age, which is what makes the shift visible at all (design, not sourced).

## 5. Leave-taking formulae elicited as a pool

Trubnikova's article investigates strategies of leave-taking behaviour in contemporary Russian, and its stated sequence is that after reviewing recent literature on leave-taking formulas, with a particular focus on Russian, it presents the results of a pilot study of farewell expressions and conversational moves (S964).

The elicitation instrument is described precisely, and the description matters for anyone reusing the design: a purpose-designed questionnaire elicited situationally bound responses and "compiled a pool of words, expressions, and sayings" that speakers reported using when saying goodbye to both acquaintances and strangers (S964).

Situationally bound is the operative phrase, because the responses were gathered against situations rather than in the abstract (S964).

The pool is two-sided in the population it covers, since the reported items are what speakers say when leaving both acquaintances and strangers (S964).

The result the pilot supports is that parting interactions are context-sensitive rather than fixed (S964).

The variation is then specified by the factors the study names: the choice of terminal exchange formulas and the supportive moves that go with them vary with the degree of familiarity, with the time elapsed since the previous encounter, and with the anticipated duration of the separation (S964).

A farewell register built from a pool with only one of those two sides will misfire on the other, so a design that wants a usable set of formulae has to elicit against both (design, not sourced).

Read as design input, the three named variables become the parameters of a fictional register: familiarity, elapsed time and expected separation are the axes along which the choice of farewell formulae can be made to vary (design, not sourced).

The supportive moves are the part a table of formulae alone would miss, because the conversational work of parting is distributed across a formula and the moves around it, and a register that stores only the formulas loses the moves (design, not sourced).

## 6. Formulae in a register design

This section is a procedure. Nothing in it is sourced, and every statement in it is a design statement about a fictional greeting register rather than a claim about any real community or any real language (design, not sourced).

Step one is to fix the family before fixing any formula, which means deciding whether the greeting and the leave-taking are cognate in the fictional language or two unrelated acts, and then treating that decision as a constraint on everything that follows (design, not sourced).

Step two is to give the family an address, which means deciding whether the whole encounter runs through one verb, as the Swiss case does, or through two separate verbs, or through a verb plus a nominal formula on each end (design, not sourced).

Step three is to specify the access ritual around the formulae, since an access ritual includes the visual and the procedural parts of being admitted to an interaction, and a register that specifies only words has specified the smallest part of the ritual (design, not sourced).

Step four is to choose the level of granularity for each formula, and the choice to make is between a fixed string, a string with slots for rank and familiarity, and a fully compositional pattern that generates a formula per pair of participants (design, not sourced).

Step five is to decide how the politeness gradient is carried, and the options are a gradient inside the formulae themselves, a gradient in a surrounding grammatical or prosodic device, or a split between the two, in which the formulae mark rank and the device marks respect (design, not sourced).

Step six is to build in the pressure, which means specifying a contact language or a contact community and letting the formulae shorten and letting code-switching appear in one part of the register while the other part stays stable (design, not sourced).

Step seven is to make the generational split part of the specification, so that an older reading and a younger reading of the same formula can both be recorded as in-world positions rather than resolved by the author (design, not sourced).

Step eight is to elicit the farewell half properly, which means writing situationally bound items for both a familiar and an unfamiliar parting, and recording the supportive moves alongside the formulas rather than after them (design, not sourced).

Step nine is to hold the axes of variation constant and explicit, so that familiarity, elapsed time and expected duration appear as named parameters rather than as habits that emerge later (design, not sourced).

Step ten is to record what the register refuses, which means listing the social situations in which no greeting and no farewell is used, since a ritual with no breach has no meaning (design, not sourced).

The output of the procedure is a small, explicit artefact: a family decision, a slot list, a set of formulae with their supportive moves, a politeness device, a contact story with a generational split, and a refusal list (design, not sourced).

## 8. Limits and open questions

The base is narrow, and it should be read as narrow: four sources is a small base for a topic that spans two speech acts, several politeness traditions and at least one contact situation, so the entry supports a method and a set of readings rather than a survey (design, not sourced).

The Russian case is explicitly a pilot study, so its findings indicate a direction for leave-taking formulae rather than fix a distribution of them (S964).

The Spanish case rests on epistolary texts up to the beginnings of the twentieth century, so it is a historical case and not a synchronic claim about how Spanish speakers greet today (S962).

The Swiss claims made here are confined to what the Rash abstract records about greeting and leave-taking as one politeness type in German-speaking Switzerland (S961).

The Ngoni claims made here are confined to what the Mapunda and Sommer abstract records about access rituals and their shift under contact with Swahili (S963).

One candidate source could not be opened at all, because on 2026-11-05 the request returned an HTTP 403 Access Denied page from the publisher's edge instead of the article, and the raw response is kept with this round's evidence, so unreachable candidates are not evidence for this entry and the fact that a relevant article exists behind such a wall neither supports nor weakens anything written here (design, not sourced).

The open questions are the ones a next round of opening would settle: whether the family framing of greeting and leave-taking holds in settings where the two acts are lexically independent, whether the split between formula and supportive move is a general property of leave-taking or an artefact of one elicitation method, and whether the generational disagreement over politeness travels with the contact situation or with the contact language (design, not sourced).

A second open question concerns the procedure itself: the ten steps fix a family, an address, an access ritual, a granularity, a politeness device, a contact story, a generational split, an elicitation and a refusal list, but they do not say how much of the register should be invariant under a shift, which is the parameter a designer will most often get wrong (design, not sourced).

A third open question is what happens to a greeting register when the encounter is with a party that has no greeting at all, since a procedure that only specifies formulae has no answer for an absence of ritual (design, not sourced).

The last limit is the standing one: nothing here is canon, and any use of this entry in a fictional setting is a design act whose cultural sensitivity is judged elsewhere, in the layers that own such judgements (design, not sourced).
