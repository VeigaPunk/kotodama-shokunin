## 1. Scope and what this entry owns

This entry owns the verbal text of songs directed at a child — the sleep-inducing formulae, the nonsense-syllable refrains, the archaic retention preserved in lullaby text, the forms of address to the child, the register shift between the song and ordinary speech, and a design procedure for a fictional lullaby register — and it owns no melody or accompaniment (`culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md`, `culture-material/dance-performance-movement-codes.md`), no infant-directed prosody or caregiving interaction beyond the song text itself (`linguistics/language-socialization-and-acquisition.md`), no oral-epic or ballad composition machinery beyond borrowed vocabulary (`linguistics/orality-formulaic-language.md`), no liturgical refrain (`linguistics/sacred-registers-liturgical-language.md`), no name inventory or teknonymy (`linguistics/naming-systems.md`), and no engineered sound-meaning play (`linguistics/sound-symbolism.md`).

This entry is a design-and-method reference. It records what five opened sources report about the song text and then turns those reports into a procedure for building a fictional lullaby register.

It asserts nothing about canon, about lore acceptance, about real-world rights, or about cultural permission; whether any particular lullaby practice may be used by a fictional people is a decision for a canon layer, not for this corpus (design, not sourced).

The entry is NON-CANON by construction: it describes a design surface and its checks, it records no world's canon, and the handoff to lore remains a handoff (`synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md`), never an import (design, not sourced).

Each of the five cases below was opened by ordinary HTTP on 2026-11-05, with the capture, the located quote and the digest held under the round's evidence directory; every registry id cited below is tier A, mined, opened on that date, and every anchor quote is an exact substring of its committed capture (design, not sourced).

The base is deliberately small and heterogeneous, and it is read as small and heterogeneous: five cases from five language traditions, none of them a survey, and the sections below give each one its own section for exactly that reason (design, not sourced).

## 2. The speech of putting a child to sleep

A lullaby is a speech act with a specified effect, and it is not an ordinary speech act with a melody put on it. The effect it works for is a change in the state of the hearer: from wake to not-wake, from restless to settled. Any design that treats the lullaby as a song about sleep rather than as the instrument that produces the change has already got the wrong object (design, not sourced).

The Udmurt case states the function in its plainest form: the improvised lullabies are described as various rhythmic and melodic intonations that are meant to soothe or put the child to sleep (S967).

The Kanak case states it structurally: the first lullabies addressed to newborns are described as "Les premières berceuses qui sont adressées aux nouveau-nés sont constituées de sons vocaux rythmiques" — rhythmic vocal sounds rather than words (S965).

Those two statements together give a designer a starting point that is not a metaphor: the song is a technology for a state change, and its earliest stage is a sound with no words in it (design, not sourced).

That yields three separable components a designer can vary independently, and a design that varies only one of them will read as a rewrite of the same song (design, not sourced):

1. **The act** — what the song is doing to the hearer, and the stated conditions under which it is sung. (design, not sourced)
2. **The text** — the words, including the non-word material and the archaic layer. (design, not sourced)
3. **The carrier** — the melody, the rhythm, the timbre and the volume, which do much of the work the words cannot. (design, not sourced)

The third component is the one a text-only design usually forgets, and it is the reason two songs with the same words are not the same song (design, not sourced).

The function is also plural in a way that matters: soothing is one function, and holding the child while an adult completes a task is another, and the same song can be sung for either (design, not sourced).

A designer who wants a fictional culture's lullabies to feel like work rather than decoration should decide which of the two functions the song is primarily for, because the two want different lengths, and a short functional song and a long affective song are different artefacts (design, not sourced).

The act has a place, and the place is part of the register: a lullaby sung over a sleeping child, a lullaby sung in a dark room, a lullaby sung on a journey, and a lullaby sung as a performance for adults are four different registers that may share a melody (design, not sourced).

## 3. Nonsense syllable refrains

The refrain of a lullaby is often built from material that is not language: strings of syllables with no referent, sound-words, echoes of the ordinary words around them, or a child's name worked into a rhythmic frame. A designer should treat this as a *layer* of the text rather than as filler, because the layer is where the register's most distinctive property sits (design, not sourced).

The Irish case gives the layer its sharpest definition, and the definition is a negative one: "in Irish the vocables which occur almost invariably in the lullaby chorus find no place in" recorded speech outside the lullaby (S966).

That is the design fact in its purest form. The nonsense material is not bad speech, not baby talk, and not a degraded form of the language; it is licensed in exactly one place and is therefore a register marker rather than an error (S966).

The same case connects the refrain's shape to a wider class of formula, observing that the vocable pattern corresponds to what Combarieu described as the characteristic magic chant, a "repetitive, incantatory, unintelligible formula" (S966).

The Kanak case gives the parallel fact from the other end of the comparison: the lullabies are strongly marked by syllabic repetition, even where their structure is long (S965).

The Ibibio case gives the functional reading of the same device, reporting that in that repertoire "the refrain stands out as the leading sound modulator in the song" (S969).

Read together, the three cases support a design claim that is stronger than any of them alone: the refrain is the part of the song that carries its register identity, and it carries it through repetition and sound rather than through meaning (design, not sourced).

That gives the designer four distinguishable options for the refrain, each with a different effect (design, not sourced):

1. **Pure non-word** — syllables with no lexical source at all, so the refrain is unambiguously song and not talk. (design, not sourced)
2. **Echo** — the refrain re-uses the shapes of the song's real words, so it sounds like the song's own vocabulary turned to music. (design, not sourced)
3. **Lexical with a filler** — real words with a non-word syllable holding the beat between them, so the text stays meaningful and the melody stays distinctive. (design, not sourced)
4. **Name-bearing** — a child's name, a kinship term or a pet name inside the refrain, so the refrain is the address slot and the song is a personal object. (design, not sourced)

A pure non-word refrain loses the cultural payload; an echo refrain gains it but risks reading as ordinary speech; the third option is the compromise most fictional cultures will have invented for themselves; the fourth is the option that makes a lullaby a family object (design, not sourced).

A design rule falls out of the four options: fix the refrain before the verses, because the refrain is what the child remembers and what the adult hums without words, so it is the part of the song that has to survive translation into every other register the culture uses (design, not sourced).

A second rule: the non-word material must be phonologically licensed by the culture's own sound system, so that it is heard as a possible word rather than as a mistake, and a design that copies a foreign nursery refrain's syllable shapes without checking them will produce something the audience hears as broken speech (design, not sourced).

A third rule: give the non-word layer at least three distinguishable rhythmic shapes, because a single shape is a single sound effect and the register needs enough variety to be a tune (design, not sourced).

The limit that follows from the last rule is that a nonsense refrain cannot be semantically loaded, so any symbolic weight a designer wants in the song has to sit in the verses, in the tune's contour, or in the address slot, not in the non-words (design, not sourced).

## 4. Archaic retention in lullaby text

A lullaby's text is often older than the people who sing it, and this is one of the few places in ordinary speech where that fact is normal rather than exceptional. The song is the container in which an obsolete form is allowed to stay because changing it would break the song (design, not sourced).

The Udmurt case documents the mechanism as a documented stratification of the repertoire, reporting that "Udmurt lullabies are divided into two different musical and stylistic groups" (S967).

The first of those groups is the improvised one, described as "one, similar to many Finno-Ugric peoples, includes improvised songs - the most archaic" layer of the song folklore (S967).

The second group is the composed one, and the case reports the outcome of its spread: "second group. That lullaby has been recorded throughout Udmurtia, almost supplanting" the local improvised lullabies (S967).

This is a documented instance of the whole archaic-retention question in one tradition: an improvised older layer survives in a genre whose texts are fixed, and a single composed text then replaces it (S967).

The Gaelic case shows the same phenomenon from the side of the aetiology, describing the "trick of folk aetiology, of justifying the continuation of a pre-Christian practice by" attaching a Christian origin to it (S966).

The design point is not that the culture is old. The point is that the song's invariance is what makes the retention safe, because a line the singer cannot change is a line whose grammar no longer has to match the speaker's own (design, not sourced).

The design consequences are concrete (design, not sourced):

- Decide which layer of the culture's grammar the song freezes: the *morphology* (a case ending or an inflection that survives only here), the *lexicon* (a kinship term or a colour word used in a sense the ordinary language has lost), or the *syntax* (a word order that the prose language has replaced). (design, not sourced)
- Freeze exactly one layer per song. A song that freezes all three at once reads as a pastiche rather than as a register. (design, not sourced)
- Give the frozen layer a *use* in the song's meaning, so that it is doing work rather than being decorative antiquity. The usual work is intensity: the archaic form is what makes the word sound bigger, more final or more tender than its modern equivalent. (design, not sourced)
- Record the speaker's awareness of the freeze. A culture where nobody notices the old form, a culture where everybody notices and nobody changes it, and a culture where the old form is deliberately kept as a register choice are three different cultures, and the song is the evidence that distinguishes them. (design, not sourced)

The fourth point is the one a writer usually gets wrong, because the third option — a deliberate, aware retention — is the most interesting and the least automatic (design, not sourced).

The diagnostic to use in a handoff is a simple one: if a reader can tell which line of a song is archaic, the freeze is a topic of the song; if the archaic line is only conspicuous because the reader is a linguist, the freeze is an accident of the culture's history rather than a designed feature (design, not sourced).

The Udmurt case also supplies a warning the procedure needs: retention and replacement are both normal outcomes, and a fictional culture's repertoire may hold both layers at once, with the composed text winning in some households and the improvised one surviving in others (design, not sourced).

## 5. Address to the child

The lullaby is one of the few genres that addresses its audience by name throughout, and the address is not a courtesy added at the front of it. The address is the load-bearing slot, and the song's grammar of address is where the culture's view of the child is written down (design, not sourced).

The Connemara example in the Gaelic case is the cleanest instance, being described as an example consisting entirely of repeated vocables "except for the words mo leanbh (‘my child’) and, perhaps significantly, the" following vocables (S966).

The real-word exceptions are the whole design point in miniature: the vocables carry the music, and the few real words carry the relationship, and the culture's address system has a place to put them (S966).

The Ibibio case shows that address in a lullaby can also point away from the hearer. Its discussion of the apostrophe defines the device as an address to someone who is not physically present at the time and place of the speech act (S969).

The two cases together give a designer two distinct address slots to specify, the hearer and the absent third party, and a fictional register can use either or both (design, not sourced).

The design therefore has to fix three separate things, and a design that fixes only the first will produce a song that says "sleep" repeatedly and nothing else (design, not sourced):

1. **The addressee slot** — the name or kinship term used, and whether it is fixed or varied across the song. (design, not sourced)
2. **The address form** — the vocative or particle that marks the child as the one being addressed, and whether the language has one. (design, not sourced)
3. **The address behaviour** — what the child is called when the song wants to comfort, and what it is called when the song wants to frighten or to discipline. (design, not sourced)

The third is where a culture shows itself. A song that calls the child by a diminutive in every verse and by a formal name in the refrain is making a claim about the two moods of the moment, and a culture that uses only diminutives is making a different claim (design, not sourced).

Two design cautions (design, not sourced):

- Address in a lullaby is asymmetric and it is not a politeness register in the ordinary sense. Nobody is being polite to the child; the child is being held, and the register's business is the child's state, not the child's status. A design that reads the address slot through the politeness machinery of an adult conversation will mis-set its parameters. (design, not sourced)
- The address slot is where a culture's naming law does its quietest work. If the culture avoids naming the dead, or avoids naming a child before a certain age, or uses a special birth-name, the lullaby is the genre where that law is either obeyed or deliberately broken, and either way the song is a primary source for it. (design, not sourced)

The second caution is also the reason the lullaby is worth a separate entry at all: it is a genre that survives after the law it embodies has been forgotten, and its survival is a kind of evidence (design, not sourced).

## 6. Register shift between the song and ordinary speech

The lullaby is a register that every speaker of the culture can reach, and the shift into it is audible as a shift. The shift is not a matter of vocabulary alone: it has a grammatical face, a lexical face, a prosodic face and a social face, and a fictional culture can move each of them independently (design, not sourced).

The Kanak case supplies direct evidence that the speak/sing split is a *lexicalized* distinction rather than a matter of style. The article records that in Kanak languages the speakers linguistically distinguish the act of speaking from the act of singing: "En langues kanak, les locuteurs distinguent linguistiquement, l’acte de “parler”" (S965).

A culture that lexicalizes the speak/sing split is a culture in which the song is a separate linguistic object, and a fictional culture can be built with that property or without it (design, not sourced).

The Breton case shows the same shift at the level of a single text, and it shows it being measured. The article describes the teachers' decision to produce, besides the sung version, "une version lente chantée de la comptine ainsi que des enregistrements des trois vers en version parlée" — a slowed sung version and spoken recordings of the verses (S968).

The reason the teachers needed both is that the sung form made the words harder for the learners to locate, and the design conclusion is that a song's shift is a real obstacle to comprehension, not only a stylistic flourish (S968).

The four faces, and what a designer fixes in each (design, not sourced):

| face of the shift | what it changes | the designer's choice | status |
|---|---|---|---|
| grammatical | word order, case, inflection, agreement | which parts of the ordinary grammar the song relaxes | design, not sourced |
| lexical | which words the song may use, and which it may not | the song's own word stock against the ordinary one | design, not sourced |
| prosodic | tempo, pitch range, volume, breath placement, vowel length | the singing profile, and how far it departs from speech | design, not sourced |
| social | who may sing it, to whom, and who may hear it | the licence and the audience | design, not sourced |

The social face is the one most often left unspecified, and it is the one that decides whether the song is a *genre* or a *household item* (design, not sourced).

Three design rules follow (design, not sourced):

- Decide the shift's *size*. A song that shifts only in prosody is a speech register with a tune; a song that shifts in grammar as well is a different dialect; a song that shifts socially as well is a ritual. These are different design objects and they will not be repaired by editing the words. (design, not sourced)
- Decide who *may not* shift. The forbidden users of the register are what make it a register: a parent who has lost the right to sing it, a person singing it to an adult, a person singing it in a place where it is not sung. (design, not sourced)
- Decide how the shift *ends*. Does the song fall back into ordinary speech at its end, or does the ordinary speech of the household acquire a permanent trace of it? The second is a diachronic claim about the culture and is much harder to write consistently, so it should be a deliberate choice rather than an accident of the prose. (design, not sourced)

The last rule is the bridge to the next section, because the tune is the one component of the register that does not need a word to be transmitted and therefore the one that travels furthest (design, not sourced).

## 7. Tune and tone: the carrier, and where this entry stops

A song survives its words by carrying its melody, and the carrier is a first-class artefact for any designer who wants a fictional culture's lullaby to be a living object rather than a written text (design, not sourced).

This entry does not own the tune. Melody, rhythm as music, rocking motion and accompaniment belong to `culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md` and `culture-material/dance-performance-movement-codes.md`, and this entry does not restate them (design, not sourced).

What the entry owns is the verbal side of the coupling, and it is a real coupling: the tune constrains the text, and the text constrains the tune, and a designer who fixes one without the other produces a song that cannot be sung (design, not sourced).

The coupling shows up in the sources. The Breton case describes the performance of the counting rhyme as accompanied by gestures that evoke the sun's course in the sky and the arrival of the night: "L’interprétation de cette comptine traditionnelle s’accompagne de gestes qui évoquent la course du soleil dans le ciel et l’arrivée de la nuit" (S968).

The Ibibio case gives the phonetic side of the same coupling, reporting that the repertoire is rich in stylistic and aesthetic devices including "aesthetic devices such as repetition/refrain, rhetorical question device, and use of" epithets, and that the meaning of the lullaby is enhanced by the rhythm (S969).

Four things the tune must carry that the text cannot, which this entry names and hands to the music owner to specify (design, not sourced):

1. **Contour** — the shape of the pitch movement, and therefore the emotional trajectory the song asserts. A falling contour and a rising contour make different promises about the end of the waking state. (design, not sourced)
2. **Tempo and pulse** — the speed, and whether it speeds up or slows down across the song. A tempo that slows is doing something no word in the song can do. (design, not sourced)
3. **Tone and timbre** — the voice quality, and whether it changes. A song that starts in one tone and ends in another is a two-state machine even if its text is one state. (design, not sourced)
4. **Density of text** — how much text is carried per unit of music, and where the silences are. A song with long silences is asking the hearer to do the work. (design, not sourced)

The fourth is the one that gets lost, and the silence is the reason a lullaby can be sung by someone who does not speak the language (design, not sourced).

Because the tune can cross the boundary between singing and speech, it can also cross the boundary between registers: a tune from a work song can be reused for a lullaby, and a lullaby tune can be reused for a dance. A fictional culture's repertoire is therefore a graph of tunes rather than a list of songs, and the graph is where a worldbuilder's invented culture becomes internally consistent (design, not sourced).

The design rule: choose one tune and give it two texts, rather than choosing two tunes and giving them one. The first move makes the song part of a repertoire; the second makes it an isolated asset (design, not sourced).

A second rule: decide whether the melody is singable in one breath. A lullaby that cannot be completed in one breath is a song for a different moment, and that mismatch between the tune and the sleeping task is the most common way an invented lullaby stops working (design, not sourced).

A third rule: write the tune's contour down as a shape before writing any word, because a tune that has to be fitted to text already written will always be the weaker of the two (design, not sourced).

## 8. The lullaby as archive

The lullaby is one of the few genres that keeps things alive that ordinary language has dropped, and it keeps them in a form that is useless for practical purposes and therefore survives (design, not sourced).

Three kinds of thing survive in a lullaby that a fictional culture can be built to show (design, not sourced):

1. **Obsolete vocabulary** — the kinship term, the colour word, the word for the house or the river, in a sense the ordinary language has lost. (design, not sourced)
2. **Obsolete grammar** — the inflection or the word order that the prose language has replaced. (design, not sourced)
3. **Obsolete content** — the name of a place, an animal, a household object or a relative that the culture no longer has, or no longer has in that quantity. (design, not sourced)

The third is the most valuable for a worldbuilder and the most often missed, because the lyric content of a lullaby is exactly where a culture's older material life is recorded, and nobody thinks of a lullaby as a source for that (design, not sourced).

The Kanak case documents the transmission side of this, reporting that the constitution of "archives orales en ligne sur les langues kanak" — online oral archives on the Kanak languages — contributed over time to completing the knowledge of, and the diffusion of, children's corpora (S965).

That is a documented case of a song repertoire functioning as an archive, and it also shows that the archive in question is a technological arrangement rather than a natural one (S965).

The Udmurt case supplies the other half, because it shows the archive decaying as well as accumulating: the composed text spread through Udmurtia and has almost supplanting the local improvised lullabies it had been preserving (S967).

The character of the archive matters for what can be read out of it (design, not sourced):

- It records the *material* past in more detail than the spoken past, because a song's words were chosen for the ear and so name the concrete things. (design, not sourced)
- It records it *out of order*, because the lines are fixed individually and the sequence that made sense when they were written is not the sequence the singer uses. (design, not sourced)
- It records it *without dates*, so an archive of obsolete content gives no chronology and must be dated from elsewhere. (design, not sourced)
- It records it *as acceptable*, so an archaic word in a lullaby is not marked as archaic by its speakers, and the archive is therefore unmarked in a way a written record is not. (design, not sourced)

The last point is the reason the lullaby is a poor source for attitudes and a good source for vocabulary: it tells a researcher what the culture *called* things, not what it thought of them (design, not sourced).

The design procedure that follows from this is a reverse-reading procedure, and it is the one that gives an invented culture a documented past without a documentary record (design, not sourced):

1. List the obsolete content in the culture's songs — the objects, places, animals and relatives named in the lyrics that the culture no longer has. (design, not sourced)
2. For each, decide the era it belongs to and what changed. (design, not sourced)
3. Check the songs for the vocabulary and grammar that freeze the older state, and let them date the content by the same era. (design, not sourced)
4. Then let the songs be the record, and let the culture have lost its written archive but kept its songs, which is a cheap and internally consistent history. (design, not sourced)

The fourth step is the whole design move, and it is the reason a fictional culture can be given a deep past that no document in the world supports (design, not sourced).

## 9. Decision table for a fictional lullaby register

This section is a procedure and a table. Nothing in it is sourced; every statement is a design statement about a fictional lullaby register rather than a claim about any real community or any real language (design, not sourced).

The table is the design's output. Each row is a decision the designer must take, the options available, and the consequence of each option, so that a designer can take a row at a time rather than answering everything at once (design, not sourced).

| decision | options | what each option gives the culture | what it costs | status |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| primary function of the song | soothing to sleep; holding a child while a task is done; a song for adults about a child | the first gives the register an audience of one; the second makes it a work-song with a fixed duration; the third makes it a performance with an audience and changes the licence | the first cannot be sung to a group; the second cannot be sung at a ritual; the third loses the intimacy that made the genre work | design, not sourced |
| who may sing it | mothers only; all parents; any female relative; anyone who can hold the tune; a named office | a restricted licence makes the song a possession of a household and gives the culture a scarcity; an open licence makes it a folk object that no one owns | restriction can be deadening and can exclude; openness loses the intimate register that made it distinctive | design, not sourced |
| the refrain's material | pure non-word; echo of the song's own words; real words with a filler syllable; the child's name or a pet name | the non-word marks the song as song at the level of sound; the echo carries the culture's vocabulary into the tune; the filler keeps meaning while holding the beat; the name makes the song a family object | the non-word cannot carry symbolism; the echo risks sounding like ordinary speech; the filler is hard to keep from becoming a tic; the name narrows the song to one child | design, not sourced |
| the frozen layer | morphology; lexicon; syntax; content only | each gives the song a different kind of age, and each is checkable against the culture's own history | freezing more than one layer at once reads as pastiche; freezing morphology in a language with little morphology is not available | design, not sourced |
| how visible the archaism is | invisible to singers; noticed but never discussed; deliberately kept; used against a child in a joke | invisibility makes the song a pure artefact; deliberate keeping makes the song an archive; the joke gives the culture a way to be self-aware about its own past | deliberate keeping has to be paid for in other genres, where the same form is obsolete; the joke needs a culture with a tolerance for self-mockery | design, not sourced |
| the address form | a true vocative; a particle; an uninflected name; no marking, with prosody carrying the address | a vocative makes the address grammatical; a particle makes it light; an uninflected name lets a foreign or newly-named child be addressed without a declension; prosody alone works in a language with no case | a vocative requires a case system; a particle is easy to miss in text; prosody alone cannot be seen by a reader of a written song | design, not sourced |
| the shift's size | prosodic only; plus grammatical; plus social | the three sizes are three different design objects, and a designer should know which one is being built | a song with a grammatical shift but no social shift is a dialect, which is a claim about the culture's internal diversity and is expensive to keep consistent | design, not sourced |
| who may not sing it | nobody; the child's own parents after a rite; men; the child's name after it is retired | the forbidden users are what make the register a register, and each option gives a different reason for the taboo to exist | a taboo with no occasion for it will be ignored by the readers of the world; a taboo on a whole gender needs the song to have been sung by that gender in the first place | design, not sourced |
| the carrier | one melody with two texts; one text with two melodies; a tune shared with a work song or a dance | sharing makes the song part of a repertoire graph and makes the culture coherent; a private tune makes the song a possession | a shared tune makes the lullaby's provenance ambiguous, which is a gift for the archive reading and a burden for the lore record | design, not sourced |
| the tempo profile | constant; slowing across the song; speeding up; slowing then still | the profile is a second, wordless argument about the child's state, and it is the component a singer can get wrong in performance | a profile that is not in the score can be lost in transmission and leave the song standing still, which changes what the song is | design, not sourced |
| the density of text | dense, one word per beat; sparse, long held syllables; a text in the gaps of a wordless tune | sparse text makes the song singable by a non-speaker and gives the silence work; dense text makes the song a text first | dense text in a long song exhausts a tired singer at exactly the moment the song needs the voice to hold; sparse text gives a reader of the written song nothing to hold | design, not sourced |
| the archive status | the songs are the culture's only record of its own past; the songs are a decorative survival beside a written record; the songs are a contested claim to a past | the first gives the culture a documented history with no documents, which is cheap and internally consistent; the third gives a setting its central argument | the first needs the songs to actually carry the obsolete content, which the designer must then write; the second wastes the archive; the third needs a party who disputes the songs, which the designer must also write | design, not sourced |

Twelve rows, each a decision rather than a recommendation. The order matters: the first two rows decide what kind of object is being designed, and the last three decide whether it will still be running a century later (design, not sourced).

A reading of the table as a whole: the register is strongest when its licence is narrow, its refrain is non-lexical, its frozen layer is single, its carrier is shared with something else, and its archive status is the first one, and it is weakest when all of those are set to the permissive option (design, not sourced).

The table also gives the writer the two failure modes worth naming. The **inert** lullaby is one where every row took the permissive option, and it reads as wallpaper. The **over-determined** lullaby is one where every row took the restrictive option, and it reads as a ritual, which is a different genre with a different owner (design, not sourced).

## 10. A worked design example (NON-CANON)

NON-CANON. The people, the language, the songs and the names below are invented for this worked example only. Nothing here describes a real community, and canon belongs to lore-shokunin (design, not sourced).

**The people.** The Tavrin of the lower Sarn valley, a river people who live in longhouses on pilings and who move their herds between two river terraces. They have no written archive. They have an unusually old song repertoire and they know it: the old songs are the only record they have of a period when the valley held a second settlement (design, not sourced).

**The songs.** The Tavrin have a lullaby called *Oren tal*, "the counting one", and it is unusual among their songs in that it is sung to a *named* child rather than to "the child", because a Tavrin child is named at the naming rite and the name is the song's subject (design, not sourced).

The song's shift from ordinary Tavrin speech has four faces, and each is deliberately different (design, not sourced):

- **Grammatical** — the song drops the oblique case ending that the ordinary language uses on every possessed noun, so the objects in the song appear bare. A Tavrin speaker describing the same objects in speech would mark possession. (design, not sourced)
- **Lexical** — the song uses two words the ordinary language does not: *oren*, which is the word for the light of a lamp seen through oil, and *sarn-veth*, which is the name of the vanished upper settlement and is otherwise a place-name in old stories. (design, not sourced)
- **Prosodic** — the song is sung on a two-beat pulse, slower than Tavrin speech, and it slows further after its fourth line. (design, not sourced)
- **Social** — *Oren tal* is sung by whoever holds the child, and by nobody else, so the licence is the widest the table allows and the song is still a household object rather than a public one. (design, not sourced)

The refrain is a pure non-word: *anen anen anen*, three repetitions, with a fourth that is always the child's own name. So the refrain is the address slot, and the fourth repetition is the one place the song touches the ordinary name stock (design, not sourced).

The song's archaic layer is **syntax only**. The verses keep a word order — object before subject — that the ordinary Tavrin language replaced two generations ago, and the singer does not notice. The song is therefore an archive of one grammatical change and of the vanished content (*sarn-veth*, and a household object named *tal-kesh* that the Tavrin no longer make), and it is not an archive of attitudes, because the singers have no idea any of it is old (design, not sourced).

The archive status is the first one in the table: the songs are the Tavrin's only record of their own past. A Tavrin who wants to know what the upper settlement was must ask a singer, and the singers disagree, because there are three versions of the refrain and only one of them names the upper settlement at all (design, not sourced).

The carrier decision is the third one in the table: *Oren tal* shares its tune with a Tavrin boat-work song, which is why the tune survives when the song does not, and why the tune is the part of the design that the music owner must specify (design, not sourced).

**What the designer gets from this.** A culture with a deep past and no documents, a genre whose licence is the whole point, an archaic layer narrow enough to be checkable, and a song whose three versions give the setting a live argument without anyone in it having to be a villain (design, not sourced).

**What the designer must still supply.** The name of the child in the worked example is left as a slot, because the naming rite and the naming law are canon decisions and this entry does not make them; and the pace of the shift from the old word order to the new one is a claim about the Tavrin's history that only the world bible can fix (design, not sourced).

## 11. Limits, open questions and the handoff

The base here is narrow, and it should be read as narrow. The sourced cases are five, they come from five language traditions, and each is scoped to what its own captured span supports; the design procedure, the decision table and the worked example are this entry's own constructions and are marked as such line by line (design, not sourced).

The Kanak case reports a lexical distinction between speaking and singing in a specific language family and a specific repertoire; it does not establish that a singing verb is a necessary property of a lullaby tradition, and the design procedure treats the presence or absence of such a verb as a decision rather than a default (design, not sourced).

The Gaelic case reports the Irish and Scottish lullaby texts it examined; the claim that a vocable refrain may be the original form with verses added later is presented in that source as a hypothesis about the material it studied, and this entry records it as such and does not generalise it (S966).

The Udmurt case is an abstract-level source, and the two-layer split it reports is a finding about that repertoire rather than a typology of lullaby genres (S967).

The Breton case concerns a pedagogical engineering exercise in one classroom setting, and its evidence about the sung version making words harder to locate is an observation about that exercise rather than a general finding about comprehension in song (S968).

The Ibibio case analyses a selected repertoire and reports the refrain's prominence within it, which is a claim about that corpus's sound organisation and not a universal ranking of song components (S969).

The open questions are the ones a next round of opening would settle (design, not sourced):

1. Whether the retention of obsolete forms in sung text is a general property of fixed verse or a specific property of particular genres, and how far the answer travels across traditions that share no history with each other. (design, not sourced)
2. Whether the non-word refrain is better analysed as a prosodic device, a lexical one, or a marker of the genre's boundary, and whether the three accounts give different design results. (design, not sourced)
3. Whether a culture's songs can carry a reliable archive of its material past when the songs are transmitted by ear, since ear-transmission and content-fidelity pull against each other. (design, not sourced)
4. Whether the register shift in a lullaby is observed by speakers as a shift at all, or whether it is only audible to an analyst who already knows the ordinary speech. (design, not sourced)
5. Whether a singing verb in the language, of the kind the Kanak case reports, is a precondition for the register being felt as separate, or merely a symptom of it. (design, not sourced)

A sixth open question concerns the procedure itself: the twelve decisions in §9 fix the register's shape, but they do not say how much of the register survives a change of circumstance, and that is the parameter a designer will most often get wrong (design, not sourced).

**Handoff to lore-shokunin.** This entry supplies a method, a decision table and a worked example for the lullaby register, and nothing else. Under `synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md` a world bible receives a design surface as reference material and keeps the canon decision: which culture sings which songs, whether a song exists in a world at all, what a name in a song means, and whether the songs are the culture's archive or a decorated survival. The packet should carry the twelve decision rows, the four shift faces, the boundary statement of §1, and the worked example marked as invented; every scoped limit and every `to-verify` item above travels with it, and the claim ledger for the packet records each sourced row as real-world reference and each table row as a generative rule (design, not sourced).

This entry decides nothing about any world. It records no canon, asserts no lore acceptance, and claims no real-world rights or cultural permission; whether any particular lullaby practice is usable by a fictional people is a judgement for the canon layer, and this entry supplies the evidence and the design surface for that judgement rather than the judgement itself (design, not sourced).
