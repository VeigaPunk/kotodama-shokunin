## 1. The vocabulary of loss — define the state before you stage it

Worldbuilding failures here are usually definition failures: a world is said to have a "dead language" while it functions in ritual, or a "revival" while nobody speaks it at home. Fix the terms first.

| Term | Working definition | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Language shift | A community replaces one language with another across its repertoire over generations (Weinreich 1953) | Shift is a **process with stages**, not an event; every stage is a sceneable state |
| Obsolescence / moribundity | Intergenerational transmission has stopped or is failing | The diagnostic hinge; everything else is downstream |
| Death (of the spoken vernacular) | No remaining first-language speakers in everyday use | Death can be partial: registers may outlive the vernacular (cult, law, scholarship) |
| Dormancy / "sleeping" | No first-language speakers, but a community identity link and reclamation work exist | EGIDS separates *dormant* from *extinct*; the distinction is a design decision, not a fact of nature (see §2) |
| Extinction | No speakers and no reclamation movement; the label itself is contested for recently lost languages | UNESCO's own Atlas introduction records objections to the term (§2) |
| Semi-speaker | A speaker with partial command: understands more than they produce, regularizes irregular morphology, lacks registers (Dorian 1981) | The semi-speaker generation is the most useful scene in fiction: fluent enough to be trusted, broken enough to be tragic |
| Terminal / last speaker | The final fluent first-language speaker(s) | Treat with care: the "last speaker" is an artifact of counting rules as much as a fact (Hill 2002) |
| Reclamation / revival / revitalization | Deliberate action to restore use: from adult learning (reclamation) to restored home transmission (full revitalization) | Revival has its own ladder (§5); it does not simply reverse the death ladder |

- **Death is usually a century-long gradient, not a moment.** Even Sumerian — the best-documented case of an ancient language's demise — has no agreed date of vernacular loss; Michalowski's formulation, quoted in the lane's round-8 receipt, is "There is agreement on one thing only: the spoken language died in antiquity. The date of this death has been variously estimated" (Michalowski 2006, p. 164, quoted in `reports/round-8/freshness/R8-F01-receipt.md`).
- **Separate the three axes:** (1) transmission (does a child learn it at home?), (2) function (which domains use it?), (3) documentation (how well is it recorded?). They move independently. A language can be superbly documented and have no speakers (Sumerian), or badly documented with 200,000 speakers (many signed and minority languages). UNESCO's factor list makes documentation a *factor*, not a vitality measure (§2).
- **Do not import the metaphor uncritically.** Mufwene (2004) argues "language birth" and "death" metaphors smuggle in organism biology and hide the fact that what changes is *who speaks what to whom*; in-world, prefer the institutions over the biology.

## 2. The diagnostic frameworks — three ladders, three audiences

Use the frameworks as diagnostic instruments, not as truth. They disagree by design: they were built for different users.

### 2.1 Fishman's GIDS (1991): eight stages, read bottom-up

GIDS ranks the *disruption of intergenerational transmission* in eight stages, from Stage 8 (a few socially isolated elderly speakers; the language must be reconstructed and taught to adults) up to Stage 1 (used in education, work, mass media, and higher government). It is meant to be read **in reverse order** — Stage 8 first — and Fishman's priority claim is that Stages 8–5 (community-controlled, face-to-face, family-and-neighborhood work) are the *minimum basis* of any reversal; school-based measures sit above that floor and cannot substitute for it.

| GIDS stage (compressed) | State | What it takes to move up |
|---|---|---|
| 8 | Isolated elderly speakers; language must be reconstructed for adult learners | Documentation + adult teaching by the community itself |
| 7 | Speakers beyond child-bearing age | Build a young adult cohort *before* transmission can restart |
| 6 | **The hinge**: informal intergenerational transmission in home, family, neighborhood | This is the stage the whole model exists to protect — nothing above it works without it |
| 5 | Literacy in the language *in addition* to schooling in the dominant language, without impact on home use | Add written functions without displacing the home |
| 4 | Schools teach in the language (some accounts split 4a/4b by degree of state control) | School programs *downward-coupled* to Stage 6, never as a substitute |
| 3 | Used in the local work sphere, alongside the dominant language | Economic function: markets, wages, local administration |
| 2 | Lower government services + local mass media | Institutional floor above the community |
| 1 | Education, work, mass media, higher government at scale | Only meaningful if 6 holds; otherwise it is decoration |

- Stage wording above is compressed from secondary summaries (multiple academic and reference restatements, 2026-10-17); Fishman's own phrasing and page locators are (to-verify) (book not inspected).
- **The model's core empirical claim is the hinge**: Welsh, Māori, and Hawaiian all show that legal status, broadcasting, and schooling can coexist with a *declining or flattening* census (Welsh: 562,000 → 538,300 between 2011 and 2021 despite two decades of statutory protection — GOV.WALES 2022, §above). Treat this as the single most useful design constraint in the whole entry: **institutions can keep a language visible; only homes keep it alive.**

### 2.2 UNESCO (2003) / Atlas (2010): nine factors and six degrees

UNESCO's expert-group document proposed nine factors for vitality assessment; the *Atlas* 3rd edition adopted degrees of endangerment defined by **intergenerational transmission**, which the introduction quotes verbatim (read 2026-10-17):

- *Safe (5)*: "spoken by all generations. The intergenerational transmission of the language is uninterrupted."
- *Stable yet threatened (5–)*: unbroken transmission, "yet multilingualism in the native language and one or more dominant language(s) has usurped certain important communication contexts."
- *Vulnerable (4)*: most but not all children speak it as a first language, "restricted to specific social domains (such as the home…)."
- *Definitely endangered (3)*: "no longer being learned as the mother tongue by children in the home… parents may still speak their language to their children, but their children do not typically respond in the language."
- *Severely endangered (2)*: "spoken only by grandparents and older generations; while the parent generation may still understand the language, they typically do not speak it to their children."
- *Critically endangered (1)*: "the youngest speakers are in the great-grandparental generation… often remember only part of the language."
- *Extinct (0)*: "no one who can speak or remember the language."

The nine factors (intergenerational transmission — weighted most; absolute speaker numbers; proportion of speakers in the population; domain shifts; response to new domains and media; materials for language education and literacy; governmental and institutional attitudes and policies; community members' attitudes; amount and quality of documentation) are reported consistently across secondary restatements but were **not read in the primary 2003 document this round** (to-verify; unesdoc returned HTTP 403).

- **The Atlas itself flags the label problem**, and the passage is quotable in-world: its team "met with objections to the term 'extinct' to refer to languages that have lost their last first-language speakers within living memory… the bald term 'extinct' does not reflect the true situation for some languages" (Moseley 2010, introduction, read 2026-10-17). Manx is the worked case: the 2009 Atlas classified it "extinct", Isle of Man speakers and schoolchildren protested ("If our language is extinct then what language are we writing in?"), and the classification was revised to "critically endangered" (Babel Magazine PDF; London Manx Society lecture record — tertiary, (to-verify)). **A label is a political act with a date.**
- **Instrument dependence is not a bug to hide.** The Welsh census (self-assessment, 538,300 / 17.8%, 2021) and the Annual Population Survey (~862,700 / 28%, 2024) measure different things and differ by ~60%; both are official. In-world: whoever commissions the count chooses the instrument, and the number moves. Never let one count adjudicate a fictional language's health.

### 2.3 EGIDS (Lewis & Simons 2010): the operational extension

EGIDS expands GIDS to 13 levels — adding 0 (international) and 9–10 (dormant/extinct) at the ends, splitting GIDS 6 into 6a vigorous / 6b threatened and GIDS 8 into 8a moribund / 8b nearly extinct — precisely so that a large-scale catalogue can assign every language a level. Structure verified (SIL record; Wikipedia EGIDS; conference PDF, all 2026-10-17); exact level names are Ethnologue wording (to-verify). Use EGIDS when your fictional world needs a *bureaucracy of endangerment* — a census office, an academy, a UNESCO-analogue — because that is what the scale was built to be.

## 3. The mechanism ladder: what actually happens between generations

| Step | Social mechanism | Scene-visible marker | In-language signature |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0 | Monolingual community | No choice to make | Baseline |
| 1 | Diglossia: H language enters school, law, cult; L stays home | A notary, a priest, or a schoolmaster in another language | Register split; borrowing of institutional vocabulary (see `language-contact.md` §4) |
| 2 | Domain retreat: market, wage labor, administration switch | Grandparents negotiate in L; grandchildren answer in H | Code-switching as the normal adult mode; loans accelerate |
| 3 | **Transmission break**: parents speak L to children, children reply in H | The household's asymmetric conversation | Simplification begins: children's L is a reduced system (Schmidt's Young People's Dyirbal, 1985) |
| 4 | Semi-speaker generation | Fluent-sounding elders; the age cohort that "understands but doesn't speak" | Loss of allomorphy and irregulars; register collapse; lexical gaps in low-frequency domains (Dorian 1981) |
| 5 | Ritual / archival remnant: the language survives in fixed texts | A prayer nobody parses; a formulaic oath; a song | Fossilized register; the last fluent performance is a quotation, not a conversation |
| 6 | Dormancy: no L1 speakers; identity link + records + movement | A dictionary, a language society, a revival class | Reconstructed pronunciation debates; orthography disputes (corpus planning, §5) |
| 7 | Extinction (label contested, §2.2) | Records only; the language is data | Reconstruction from written witnesses — the historical-linguist's domain |

- **The age gradient is the fingerprint of the break.** Te Ara's 2013 Māori figures — 38.8% of Māori aged 65+ spoke te reo versus 16.6% under 15 — are the classic shape of a transmission break in progress; any fictional census table showing that inversion tells the story without a single speech.
- **Attrition is not decay into nothing; it is replacement by a new system.** Schmidt's Dyirbal young people's grammar is systematic, not random: it has its own rules. Write the semi-speaker variety as a *grammar*, not as mistakes.
- **Ideology is a mechanism, not a backdrop.** The Māori case records the director of education T. B. Strong (1930): "the natural abandonment of the native tongue inflicts no loss on the Maori" (Te Ara, citing *Maori and education*, 1931, p. 193) — and the Education Ordinance 1847 requiring schooling in English. Institutions with explicit language-replacement doctrines give you dated, quotable in-world documents; use them.
- **Where the language lives matters.** Diglossia (see `sociolinguistics-registers.md` §3) can be *stable for centuries* — Latin over vernacular Europe, Sanskrit over Indic speech, Church Slavonic over Slavic — because the H language is nobody's mother tongue and therefore has no transmission to lose. Death becomes possible when a *mother-tongue* competitor enters the home domains. Keep these two configurations apart.

## 5. Revival mechanics: what works, what is sold as working

Ranked by Fishman's priority (Stage 6 first) and by what the cases actually show:

1. **Restore home transmission (Stage 6).** Everything else is scaffolding. Instruments: family language policy, language nests (kōhanga reo 1982; the model has been exported to Hawaiian Pūnana Leo, Sámi, and elsewhere), intergenerational living arrangements, and status for caregivers who speak the language.
2. **Build the young adult cohort before expecting transmission (Stage 7 work).** Adult acquisition programs — master–apprentice models (Hinton 1994; Hinton & Hale 2001), Te Ataarangi's community-based method in the Māori case — create the *parents* who will one day run Stage 6.
3. **Immersion schooling and nests (Stages 5–4).** Necessary above the floor, insufficient alone; their output is a school register unless coupled downward to homes.
4. **Status planning** (official status, courts, signage, broadcasting): changes what the language can *do*; Welsh and Māori both show it does not by itself change what families *speak*.
5. **Corpus planning** (standardization, terminology, orthography): Cornish's Standard Written Form (2008) and Manx's school standard are the visible artifacts; expect in-world factions around script and spelling, and note that these are **political** settlements (Haugen 1966; Kloss 1969).
6. **Documentation and archives**: the last-resort layer; converts a language into recoverable data. Note the fragility chain: the lane's own round-9 receipt documents how volatile digital retrieval is (`reports/round-9/freshness/audit.md`: client-dependent interstitials, UA-sensitive walls) — an archive is a claim about the future, not a guarantee.
7. **Neo-speakers change the language.** Learners standardize, hypercorrect, and flatten registers; the revived variety is a new sociolect with its own politics (compare the new-speakers literature; O'Rourke, Pujolar & Ramallo's special issue is standard but was not inspected this round — (to-verify)). Decide, in-world, who counts as a speaker and whose variety is authoritative — that fight is the plot.

- **The counterfactual is the craft**: a revival movement is a set of institutions with dates. Give each one: founding event, funding, curriculum or corpus, authority, and first cohort. A worldbuilder who can list those five things for a fictional movement has done the work; one who writes "the language was revived" has not.

## 6. Failure modes and the debunk shelf

- **School-only revival.** The most common fictional (and real) error: immersion schools with no Stage-6 coupling produce graduates who cannot use the language at home; Welsh's census trajectory is the sobering calibration (§4).
- **Revival-as-emblem.** The language becomes a flag: anthems, signage, oaths — with no domestic transmission. Egypt's Coptic liturgy, Latin, and Sumerian show that emblematic survival is *stable*; it is simply not revival.
- **Dictionary without speakers.** Documentation is not vitality (UNESCO factor 9; the Sumerian case is the extreme).
- **Flattening.** Restored languages tend to lose registers — honorifics, avoidance styles, ritual diction — first, because revival classrooms teach one variety (see `sociolinguistics-registers.md`, `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md`; the oral formulaic repertoire is likewise the least recoverable layer, `orality-formulaic-language.md`).
- **The folk statistic.** "A language dies every 14 days" circulates widely with no traceable primary source and is best treated as **folklore**; the underlying scholarly projections (Krauss 1992: 50–90% loss by 2100) are explicitly contested projections, not measurements. In-world: let rival institutions publish rival numbers.
- **The strong knowledge-loss claim.** "Every language carries unique knowledge that dies with it" is true in specific, evidenced domains (ethnobiological nomenclature, navigational systems — see `linguistic-anthropology.md` on calibrated relativity) and false as a mystical blanket. Cite domains, not essences.
- **"Last speaker" romance.** Hill (2002) and Errington (2003) show the trope is an artifact of advocacy rhetoric; a fictional "last speaker" scene is powerful but should be written as an institution's counting rule made flesh — someone is "last" because a school, a census, or a tape recorder defined fluency first.

## 7. Staging ladder for fiction: the same facts as scene-visible states

| State | Domains using the language | Household conversation | Visible artifacts | In-language signature |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Full community | All | Children answer in it | Monolingual records | Full register range |
| Diglossic stability | H in cult/law/school; L at home | L only | Bilingual documents | Borrowing in the institutional register |
| Domain retreat | L in home + local market; H elsewhere | Adults L↔H, children H | Wage contracts in H | Code-switching; loan floods |
| Broken transmission | L in home only, aging | Children answer in H | The last monolingual elder's testimony | Reduced child grammar (semi-speakers) |
| Ritual remnant | Fixed texts only | None | Prayer books, oaths, songs | Fossilized register; no productivity |
| Dormant | Revival classrooms | None | Dictionary, society, archive | Reconstructed pronunciation debates |
| Extinct (label per §2.2) | None | None | Data | Reconstruction only |
| Revival stages | Nest → school → media → local work → wider | Restarts **last** and is the only true measure | Nests, charters, curricula | Neo-speaker standard; register loss |

- **Reversibility asymmetry**: the ladder runs down fast (one generation can complete the break) and up slowly (Māori's chain took a century, and is unfinished). If a fictional timeline revives a language in ten years, the reader who knows this file will not believe it — unless the world supplies an atypical condition (Hebrew's H-base, a concentrated immigrant society, mass schooling, a national project) and *says so*.

## 9. Handoff

What lore-shokunin receives from a staged death/revival: (1) the **state ladder** with the world's current stage marked; (2) the **institutions** that hold each domain (school, cult, court, market, household), each with a founding date and authority; (3) the **instrument** that counts speakers in-world (and who runs it); (4) the **artifacts** that exist at that stage (grammar, prayer book, census table, school charter); (5) the **open questions** the world's scholars would fight over (date of vernacular loss, authenticity of the revived standard, status of semi-speakers). No statement here decides what is true in any world; contested scope is reported to the integrator rather than absorbed.
