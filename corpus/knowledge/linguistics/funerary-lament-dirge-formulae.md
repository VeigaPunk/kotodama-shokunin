## 1. Scope: what this entry owns, and what it refuses to own

This entry owns the **sung speech of mourning** — the verbal layer of the lament, the dirge and the ritual cry of grief, in the traditions where such a form exists — and it owns four things inside that layer: who is licensed to perform it, how the performance is structured (monodic or antiphonal, through-composed or anchored by a refrain), what the words conventionally contain (the praise of the dead, the address to the dead, the blame and the consolation), and how the register is fenced socially (the gendered mourning rules that decide who may wail, who may not, and in what presence).

It explicitly does **not** own the following, each of which is owned elsewhere and is named here only as a neighbour:

- The mortuary system itself — the disposal of the body, the assemblage, the monument, the mourning economy that pays for grief, and the death register of avoidance and office terms. That is `culture-material/funerary-material-culture.md`; this entry borrows the idea of a stratified death vocabulary and restates none of it (design, not sourced).
- The composition machinery of oral verbal art — the formula, the type-scene, the epithet, the ring composition, the theme. That is `linguistics/orality-formulaic-language.md`, which already lists *lament* as one type-scene card in the epic inventory; this entry treats the lament as one specialised deployment of that machinery and adds no new theory of it (design, not sourced).
- The liturgical and ritual-speech registers, including their refrain and chant as institutional practice. That is `linguistics/sacred-registers-liturgical-language.md`; the boundary between a liturgical requiem and a professional keener's cry is drawn in §8 here, but the liturgical side is not re-owned (design, not sourced).
- The child-directed song register, which is a sibling of this one and not a part of it. That is `linguistics/lullaby-child-song-formulae.md` (design, not sourced).
- The instruments, the tuning, the dance and the procession. Those are `culture-material/music-instrument-lexicon.md` and `culture-material/dance-performance-movement-codes.md`; this entry takes only the words and the rules that govern who may say them (design, not sourced).

The entry is a **design-and-method reference**. It records what five opened sources report about lament and dirge practice, and then turns those reports into a procedure for building a **fictional mourning register** — a coherent sung grief language for an invented people. It asserts nothing about canon: no world, no people, no doctrine and no law is decided here (design, not sourced).

Everything invented in this entry is marked **NON-CANON** where it appears; every design statement is marked *design, not sourced*; and every statement about a real tradition carries the registry id of the opened source that grounds it (design, not sourced).

The base is deliberately small and heterogeneous — five cases from five language traditions, none of them a survey — mirroring the sample-design choice the lullaby entry made in `corpus/knowledge/linguistics/lullaby-child-song-formulae.md`, and the sections below give each case its own place (design, not sourced).

## 2. The lament as a sung register: speech, song, and the boundary between them

A lament is not a speech act with a melody attached to it. It is a *speech act performed in song*, and the difference matters for a designer because the song is what carries what ordinary speech cannot carry: repetition without new information, simultaneity between two groups, and a duration that is not obliged to be efficient (design, not sourced).

The Irish case states the boundary position most sharply. The keen there is described as something more than the raw cry: "the keen was a sacred improvised chant that evolved over many centuries", sung over the corpse and intrinsic to the ritual of the wake (S972). Three things are decided in that single formulation. The form is *improvised*, so it is a practice and not a text. It is *sacred*, so it is not a private cry but a licensed and constrained one. And it is *old*, so it belongs to the ritual and not to the individual mourner (S972).

The Corsican case states the same boundary from the other end, describing lament as a performance that combines musical and verbal signification — "in the forms of homage, praise, etc." — with non-linguistic utterances such as stylized crying or weeping whose meanings are culturally constructed (S971). The design fact is that a lament is a **mixed** channel: words and non-words carry different loads, and the culture decides which load goes where (design, not sourced).

Three properties therefore separate the lament from ordinary talk about grief, and a designer who wants a mourning register to read as a distinct register has to make all three decisions (design, not sourced):

1. **The occasion binds it.** A lament is not available at any time; it is licensed by a death, and often by a stage of the mourning. A register with no occasion is a vocabulary, not a register (design, not sourced).
2. **The words are conventional.** A lament says the expected things — the dead person's worth, the address to the dead, the accusation against the fate that took them, the consolation of the survivors. The audience can tell what is being said because the audience knows the formulae, and the familiarity is the point (design, not sourced).
3. **The sound outlasts the sense.** A lament is sung, which lets it continue past the point where new content is available, and lets it be long. Length is a licence, not an accident (design, not sourced).

That third property is where the register becomes a design problem: a designer has to decide *where the music takes over from the words*, and the choices are a small set of named mechanisms rather than a continuum (design, not sourced):

- **Refrain-anchored.** A fixed line or a fixed syllable-string returns at intervals; the verses between them carry the content, and the community can join in the refrain without knowing the words (design, not sourced).
- **Antiphonally paired.** One side sings a line, the other side answers; the lament is a dialogue between two groups, and the text is written to be answered rather than heard to the end (design, not sourced).
- **Free and monodic.** One voice, no fixed return, content-driven; the song-ness comes from the tune and the pacing rather than from a formal device (design, not sourced).
- **Modulated.** The lament changes mode or tempo partway — a section faster and flatter for the accusation, a section slower for the consolation — so the structure of the feeling is carried by the structure of the music (design, not sourced).

These are not styles of one thing; they are four different objects that a designer must choose *between*, because the first two can be joined by a whole community and the second two cannot (design, not sourced).

## 3. Professional keeners: the licensed voice of grief

The most consequential design decision a fictional culture makes about its mourning register is **who is allowed to perform it**, because the answer determines whether the lament is a public institution or a domestic practice (design, not sourced).

A three-way division has three positions, and a designer should pick one deliberately (design, not sourced):

1. **The professional mourner.** A person whose occupation is to lament — hired, paid, expected to know the formulae, expected to be able to keep singing. The mourner's skill is a craft with a repertoire, and the lament is a service the family buys (design, not sourced).
2. **The kin.** The duty falls on the closest relatives by a rule of descent; the lament is a duty and not a performance, failing to lament is a breach of kinship, and lamenting too little is as wrong as lamenting too much (design, not sourced).
3. **The mixed regime.** The mourner is hired for the public phase and the kin take over for the private phase, or the kin are required to lament but may hire a professional when they cannot manage it (design, not sourced).

The Irish wake gives the sharpest statement of what the professional mourner is *for*. The eighteenth-century wake is described as a sequence in which the corpse is laid out, "The family keened over the corpse. Professional keeners took over.", a stream of visitors comes and goes, and the keening continues at intervals through the night, particularly when a new visitor arrives (S972). The order matters as much as the fact of it: the kin go first and the professionals take over, which is the moment the lament stops being the household's own and becomes a performance with an audience. The scheduling matters too — this is a *staged* offering repeated on cue, not an eruption (S972).

The Irish case also shows that the office was gendered and institutional rather than incidental. The keening woman, the *bean chaointe*, is described as representing and maintaining community Catholicism in a context of scarce priests, and her role "was important in the role of the female professional mourner who performed ritualized laments" (S973). The reading for a designer is that professionalism in a lament tradition is rarely a market arrangement; it is a gap-filler — a role that exists because the religious or kin infrastructure cannot cover the need — and the mourner's licence is a licence to *represent* something, not merely to be paid for noise (S973).

Each option produces a different repertoire. The professional mourner needs *portable* formulae — lines that work for any dead person, with the family's own material slotted in; the kin-lament needs *specific* formulae, the dead person's own habits, their name, their trade; and the mixed regime needs both, which is why the mixed regime usually generates a two-tier archive, a public set of songs and a private set of verses (design, not sourced).

A professional mourner also implies a **disciplined rule set** the designer must state explicitly, because those rules are what a reader will look for (design, not sourced):

- What the mourner may and may not say about the dead. A praise that is too frank, an accusation that names the living, and an allusion the family has forbidden can all be breaches (design, not sourced).
- Who may hire a mourner and who may not, and whether the poor are lent a mourner rather than buying one (design, not sourced).
- Whether the mourner is paid in money, in food, in cloth, or in a fee the community collects (design, not sourced).
- Whether the mourner's own grief may be shown, or whether the mourner is a professional and therefore *not* to be mourned in turn (design, not sourced).
- Whether the mourner's gender is fixed, and if so what follows from it (see §7) (design, not sourced).

The last of those is the hinge: a professional mourner who is a woman and a professional mourner who is a man produce entirely different cultures, and the designer's choice is a canon decision that this entry does not make (design, not sourced).

## 4. Antiphonal lament: the duet between two groups

The antiphonal lament is the structure in which the lament is not performed *at* an audience but *between* two groups, and it is the structure that most efficiently encodes a social fact — that the mourners and the dead's household are, in this moment, two parties (design, not sourced).

The Seto case gives the mechanism plainly. Two reasons are given for the existence of the Seto choral lament, and the first is that "the antiphonal way of performing Seto songs" makes it possible for the lead singer to improvise the text, which is a necessary requirement of the lament genre (S970). This is the design fact the antiphonal form exists for: the alternation is not decoration around the text, it is the condition that makes improvisation legible, because the singer is always answering a known prompt and is therefore never lost. A designer who wants a monodic lament and also wants long improvised text has to solve that problem some other way; this case says the antiphonal form is one solution, not the only one (S970).

The Seto material also shows that the form is not a song with a lament put into it. The article frames its own inquiry around "the relationship between the genres of lament and song in Seto culture", and the Seto repertoire is unusual in that the laments of a deceased maiden are performed by a group of lamenters as a kind of polyphonic lamenting song (S970). The design reading is that in this tradition the two genres are entangled enough that the question of which is which is a live research question; a fictional culture can decide the question cleanly either way (S970).

The Corsican material shows the antiphonal form carrying an argument as well as a performance. The Corsican antiphony claim is the source's own "suggests"; the stronger truth-claim reading — that Maniat laments can function as truth-claims supported by the "inherently jural and historicizing structure" of antiphony — is Seremetakis (1990:508), reported in S971's footnote 11, not a Corsican claim, and "The antiphonal nature of voceru performance" suggests the Corsican lament served a similar "record-keeping function" for a community in conflict (S971). The design consequence is that antiphony is not only a musical structure but a *documentary* one: a form in which two parties alternate can be treated as a record of what one party said to another (S971).

Three properties follow from the form (design, not sourced):

- **The lines are written to be answered.** A first line sets a term; the answering line is expected to take it up, complete it, or contradict it. The pair, not the line, is the unit (design, not sourced).
- **The audience can enter.** Because the answer is predictable, a group that does not know the whole text can still sing the answering part; a monodic lament cannot be joined except by silence (design, not sourced).
- **The alternation paces the grief.** The dialogue gives the performance its rhythm and its escalation: the accusation lines get shorter and more insistent, the consolation lines lengthen (design, not sourced).

The design consequences are concrete. If a designer chooses the antiphonal form, then (design, not sourced):

1. The text must be written in pairs, and the pairs must be *answerable* — each answer able to stand alone as a line, and metrically equal to its prompt, because the answering voice is a group and not a trained soloist (design, not sourced).
2. The two voices need different texts, so the culture has two repertoires, one for the mourners and one for the household, and the two repertoires must be *legible to each other* or the alternation collapses into two unrelated songs (design, not sourced).
3. A question-and-answer pair is the cheapest way to carry a complaint, because the answer can be an evasion and the audience reads the evasion as the point (design, not sourced).
4. The alternation must terminate, and the term is normally the last speaker alone, so the design needs a rule for who breaks the pattern and at what cost (design, not sourced).
5. The two voices need different physical dispositions — one seated or standing still, one moving — and that is a dance and procession question, not a linguistic one, and it is owned elsewhere (design, not sourced).

A designer who wants the antiphonal form but not the duet as a conversation should make the answering voice a **ritual answer** instead of a reply: a fixed cry, a name, a formula, that acknowledges rather than argues. The lament then has a solo who carries the content and a chorus that carries the ritual, and the two can be split by gender, by age, by status, or by nothing at all (design, not sourced).

## 5. The lament refrain: the anchor that makes a lament a lament

The **lament refrain** is the fixed element that returns in a lament and gives it its shape. It is the element that lets a listener know, within the first line, that they are hearing a lament rather than an ordinary song about sorrow, and it is the element a community can sing without learning the text (design, not sourced).

The Svan field account gives the refrain in action. An elderly village woman approaching the body of the dead village doctor produces intense vocalizations with increased weeping and sobbing, and "Each phrase of her agonized litany ends with a refrain exclamation" — the dead man's name Pridon inside the exclamation "woy Pridon!", a grammatical gloss this entry adds (design, not sourced) — which is then followed in turn by the other women mourners with loud utterance-type sobbing (S974). Three design facts sit in that sentence: the refrain is a *name*, it closes each phrase rather than opening each stanza, and the lament's content is what happens between the refrains, not the refrains themselves (S974).

The Irish keening case describes the same device at the far end of the lexical scale. The communal wail is characterised as a largely non-lexical element — Breandán Ó Madagáin's description, reported through S973 — which might include the name of the deceased followed by a non-lexical refrain, and the quoted description is that "No words were used, only vocables such as" — vocables alone — so that "so that the community gave poignant expression to their emotion in purely musical terms", using its voices as an instrument, just as in the instrumental lament played on pipes or harp (S973). Read against the Svan case, the two ends of the scale are the same slot in the same form: a returning element the whole community can produce without knowing the content (S973).

Three things a designer must decide about a lament refrain, and they are independent of each other (design, not sourced):

1. **Its material.** It may be a line of real words (often a vocative — the dead person's name, or a kinship word), a syllable-string with no lexical meaning, a phrase borrowed from a hymn or a prayer, or a song-word (a nonsense sound the culture uses for un-speakable things) (design, not sourced).
2. **Its position.** It may open the piece and return at the head of each stanza, it may sit at the end of each stanza as a close, or it may be an interstitial response between stanzas. Opening and closing positions make it a frame; the interstitial position makes it a floor on which the verses stand (design, not sourced).
3. **Its duration.** A short refrain is a stamp; a long one is a chorus and can be the actual content of the song (design, not sourced).

The reason the refrain matters more in a lament than in most other songs is that a lament's **content is formulaic**, and a refrain is where a formula can be repeated without the repetition reading as a failure of invention. The community's expectation is that a lament will not surprise in its wording; the surprise is supposed to be in the voice (design, not sourced).

Two design warnings follow (design, not sourced):

- A lament refrain that is fully lexical and fully transparent will be heard as a chorus song, and the register will lose its edge. If the register must feel *other*, the refrain should carry at least one element ordinary speech cannot — a syllable, a sound, a broken or archaic word (design, not sourced).
- A lament refrain that is *too* singable — too short, too metrically regular, too cheerful in contour — will be mistaken for a work song or a lullaby. The melody is not this entry's business, but the choice of words is, and the words should not be trivially repeatable by a child without the context that makes them a lament (design, not sourced).

A useful test for a designer: **can the refrain be sung by someone who does not know the song?** If yes, the refrain is doing its job. If the whole lament has to be known to be joined, the form is not a refrain form and the joiner has been given nothing (design, not sourced).

## 6. Formulaic grief content: praise of the dead, address to the dead, blame and consolation

A lament repertoire is a **formulae inventory**, and the inventory has four named compartments that this entry uses to sort the reports. A designer who writes a lament should place each line in one of these four compartments, and should know which compartment a line belongs to, because the compartments carry different social risks (design, not sourced).

**Praise of the dead** is the first compartment. It states the dead person's worth, and it does so with the expected topics: their trade or office, their manner, their generosity, their looks, their relation to the survivors. It is the safest compartment and the least contested, and it is where a family's specific material can be inserted — the praise of the dead is the part of a lament most often individual to the dead person (design, not sourced).

The Svan account shows this compartment in action, and shows it immediately adjoining the address: in the recitation "Sima praises the good deeds and grace of the deceased", beseeching the dead to take good care in the afterlife of her relatives and others (S974). The design reading is that the praise of the dead is the content that the *address* is the grammar of: the praise is what the mourner says to the dead, not what the mourner says about the dead to the living (S974).

**Address to the dead** is the second compartment, and it is the one that makes the form what it is: the lament speaks *to* the dead, in the second person, as if the dead could hear. This is a grammatical fact with a large design consequence, because the address form is a slot the designer fills with a relationship (the dead person as mother, as wife, as elder brother) and the address slot therefore determines what the lament can be about (design, not sourced). The address also carries the vocative — the name or the kinship term in its vocative shape — and the vocative is often the most archaic part of the whole repertoire (design, not sourced).

**Blame** is the third compartment, and it is the dangerous one. Blame in a lament is aimed at three different targets, and each has a different cost (design, not sourced):

- *Fate, illness or misfortune* — the safest target, and the one that lets the singer express rage without indicting anyone (design, not sourced).
- *The divine order or the gods* — costly in a culture with an active priesthood, and a design that puts a complaint about the gods into a professional mourner's mouth has to decide who answers it (design, not sourced).
- *The living* — the most dangerous and often the most dramatic: the accusation of the dead's family, of the spouse, of the physician, of the one who did not come to the deathbed. A lament that names the living has to have rules about whether the mourner is protected while saying it (design, not sourced).

**Consolation** is the fourth compartment, and it is the one that lets the performance end. Consolation in a lament typically does one of three things: it promises the dead a place in the afterworld, it promises the survivors that the dead's line continues, or it promises that the grief itself will end. The designer's choice matters, because a consolation that promises an afterworld commits the culture to a doctrine, and this entry makes no doctrinal claim; the design procedure only requires that the choice be *made once and held* (design, not sourced).

The four compartments also have a **sequence**, and the sequence is a design decision rather than a discovery: praise first and blame last is a common order, but a lament that opens with blame and closes with praise reads as a reconciliation rather than a lament, and a designer should know which of the two they are writing (design, not sourced).

A designer should also decide the **degree of conventionality**: how much of the text is fixed formulae and how much is improvised per occasion. The two extremes are a culture with a canon of fixed lament songs, where the family's grief is expressed by choosing among known items, and a culture where everything is improvised and no two laments are alike. The middle position — fixed frame, improvised verses — is the one most designs end up at, and it is the one that leaves room both for the professional mourner's craft and for the family's specificity (design, not sourced).

## 7. Gendered mourning rules

The **gendered mourning rules** of a culture are the rules that decide who may wail, who must be silent, who may be mourned by whom, and who may not mourn at all. They are the most consequential rules in this entry, because a lament is a loud, public, emotional act, and loud public emotion is exactly what a culture polices first (design, not sourced).

The Corsican case states the structural fact: lament is "a predominantly (in many contexts exclusively) feminine mode of expression", and in the Greek context specifically it has been shown that lament can serve as a privileged arena within a male-dominated society in which women express gender-specific concerns — a demonstration the source reports from Auerbach 1987 and Seremetakis 1990, 1991 via S971 (S971). The design reading is that a gendered mourning rule is not a restriction bolted onto a neutral practice; it is a positive allocation of a public voice, and the culture that has it is a culture that has decided something about who speaks and about whom (S971).

A designer has to settle four separate questions, and the answers are independent (design, not sourced):

1. **Who performs.** If the lament is the kin's duty, the duty is almost always gendered; if it is a professional office, the office is usually gendered too, and the two cases produce different systems (design, not sourced).
2. **Who is mourned.** Rules about which deaths may be lamented publicly, and by whom, are common: a death by violence, a death in childbirth, a suicide, a death of an outsider or an enemy, each of which may fall outside the licence (design, not sourced).
3. **Who may hear it.** Some cultures restrict the lament to the women's quarter, or require it to be performed out of earshot of the men, or forbid it entirely inside the house where the dead lies (design, not sourced).
4. **When it stops.** A culture that regulates the *duration* of mourning is regulating the lament, since the lament is the audible form of the mourning period (design, not sourced).

The designer's structural choice is whether the gendered rule is a **licence** (a permission granted) or a **prohibition** (a boundary defended). These produce different texts: a licence produces a repertoire with variants for different performers, and a prohibition produces one repertoire for everyone plus a set of taboos on who may use it (design, not sourced).

A second structural choice is whether the rule is **hard** (breaking it is a serious offence) or **soft** (breaking it is a shame). A hard rule gives the lament a high status and makes it dangerous; a soft rule makes it ordinary, which changes how the audience hears a professional mourner's cry (design, not sourced).

Two design warnings (design, not sourced):

- A gendered mourning rule with no occasion will be ignored by the readers of the world. If the rule says that women alone may lament, the setting must give the reader at least one scene in which a man's lament is visibly wrong, or the rule reads as decoration (design, not sourced).
- A gender rule that the design has not costed will read as a modern projection. A designer should be able to say what the rule *buys* the culture — control over who may express grief publicly, a division of labour, a status ladder between mourners, a licence for women to speak in a register men do not use — and if the answer is nothing, the rule should be dropped (design, not sourced).

## 8. Error patterns: genre confusions to avoid

The funerary verbal repertoire is dense with near-neighbours, and a fictional mourning register is most often spoiled by being one of them instead of being what it claims to be. The following are the confusions this entry names, with the test that distinguishes them (design, not sourced).

**1. Lullaby functions imported into a lament.** A lullaby's job is to put a child to sleep, and its refrains are address-to-the-child and repetitive in a soothing contour. A lament's job is to work the grief, and its refrains work *for* the singer, not for the listener's calm. The test: if the refrain could be sung to a living child without changing a word, the design has imported a lullaby function into a mourning register, and the lament will read as a lament about someone who is merely away (design, not sourced).

**2. Keening confused with liturgical requiem.** A professional keener's cry is a *service*: hired, skilled, non-liturgical, publicly delivered, and it asserts nothing about doctrine. A requiem is a liturgical act: institutional, authorised, textual, and doctrinal. The test: if the lament is performed inside a religious office, has a fixed written text, and its content is a claim about the dead's salvation, it is a requiem and not a keening — and a design that wants a keener must keep the two apart. The presence of a priest does not make a cry liturgical, and the presence of hired mourners does not make a service irreligious; the test is *authority and text*, not personnel (design, not sourced).

**3. Dirge confused with funeral march.** A funeral march is an instrumental processional music: it moves at a walking pace, it is scored for instruments, and its words are usually few or none. A dirge is a song of grief; it is stationary or slow-moving, it is vocal, and its text carries the formulae of §6. The test: if the piece's function is to regulate the pace of a procession, it is a march; if its function is to let the mourners say the expected things, it is a dirge. A culture can have both and they must be distinguishable in the design notes (design, not sourced).

**4. The lament confused with the elegy.** An elegy is composed for a readership, in the writer's own voice, about one dead person; a lament is performed for a community, in conventional formulae, on behalf of the dead. The test: who is the audience, and is the text the mourner's or the author's? A lament with a named author is a lament written for a stage, which is a different object (design, not sourced).

**5. Vocative confusion.** A lament that uses the dead person's name as its *refrain* is not a lament with a refrain; it is an elegy with a refrain. The vocative belongs to the address-to-the-dead compartment, where the designer's content lives; it should not be promoted to the structural element that the whole community can join (design, not sourced).

**6. Ritual cry confused with speech.** A cry that carries no propositional content at all — a vocable string, a sob-shaped syllable — is a legitimate lament component, but if *every* line is a cry, the design has produced a sound and not a register, and the culture has no repertoire to transmit (design, not sourced).

**7. Mourning-rule confusion.** A rule that restricts *who may be mourned* (which deaths admit a lament) is not the same as a rule that restricts *who may mourn* (which mourners admit a lament). Designs that state one and mean the other produce a setting whose reader cannot predict the scene, and the scene is the thing the reader is there for (design, not sourced).

**8. Musicalising the prose.** If the lament's text is written so that it reads well only as prose and not as song, the register has collapsed into a speech act with tunes attached — the exact error §2 names (design, not sourced).

Eleven rows, each a decision rather than a recommendation. Read as a whole, the register is strongest when its licence is narrow, its performer set is defined, its anchor is partly non-verbal, its content inventory is wide and its rules are costed; and it is weakest when every row takes the permissive option, in which case the lament is a private utterance with a tune attached (design, not sourced).

Two failure modes are worth naming. The **inert lament** is one in which every row took the permissive option; it reads as a mood. The **over-determined lament** is one in which every row took the restrictive option; it reads as a ritual with no grief in it, which is a different genre with a different owner (design, not sourced).

## 10. Checklist for designing a fictional mourning register

This checklist is the operating procedure for the table above. It is a design instrument and nothing in it is sourced (design, not sourced).

**Before writing a single line of text (design, not sourced):**

1. Fix the occasion. Name the event at which the lament is sung, its position in the mourning sequence, and who is present (design, not sourced).
2. Fix the performer set. Decide whether the lament is a paid office, a kin duty, or both, and write down the rule that decides it (design, not sourced).
3. Fix the audience. A lament performed for a community and a lament performed for the dead's family are different objects (design, not sourced).
4. Fix the four compartments of §6 and decide, for each, whether it is present, absent, or restricted to a named performer (design, not sourced).
5. Fix the consequence of each rule. For every prohibition, write the scene in which it is visible; a rule with no scene is decoration (design, not sourced).
6. Decide the canon boundary explicitly: what the lament claims about the dead's fate, and whether it claims anything at all (design, not sourced).

**While drafting the text (design, not sourced):**

7. Write the answering voice first if the form is antiphonal; the answers reveal what the prompts must contain (design, not sourced).
8. Write the refrain as a joinable part. Test it: can a listener who knows nothing else sing it? If not, it is a verse, not a refrain (design, not sourced).
9. Give each compartment its own contour. Praise should not sound like blame; if they do, the singer's audience cannot tell where the accusation starts (design, not sourced).
10. Keep the vocative in the address compartment and out of the structural anchor (design, not sourced).
11. Keep the blame inside the target the design chose. A lament that slides from fate to the living without a marked transition will be read as an accusation the culture forbids (design, not sourced).
12. Check the lament against the genre tests of §8. In particular: could the refrain be sung to a child as a lullaby? If yes, rewrite it (design, not sourced).

**Before the design is declared done (design, not sourced):**

13. Check the register against the sibling registers in the corpus. A mourning register that reproduces the culture's lullaby logic, its liturgical logic or its work-song logic has been copied rather than designed (design, not sourced).
14. Check that the repertoire is transmissible: someone who is not the designer could sing it, teach it, and argue about it (design, not sourced).
15. Check that the culture's own grief policy has a cost. Grief that is regulated but free of consequences is not a policy (design, not sourced).
16. Record the non-canon status and the handoff: what is decided by canon, what is decided here, and what is left open for the world bible (design, not sourced).

## 11. A worked design example (NON-CANON)

NON-CANON. The Vessemi, their names and their songs are invented for this worked example only. Nothing here describes a real community, and canon belongs to lore-shokunin (design, not sourced).

**The people.** The Vessemi of the middle Toln valley, a salt-trading people who live in stilted houses along a brackish river and who burn their dead on the shore. They have no written archive; their dead are remembered in the songs that are sung over them (design, not sourced).

**The performer.** The Vessemi have **professional keeners** as a hereditary office: the *tolo*, of a specific family, who are hired by a household to sing the shore-burn, and who are paid in salt rather than money, because the Vessemi do not keep coin (design, not sourced). A *tolo* who is a woman is the norm; the design gives the office a male variant as well, because the two produce different scenes and the setting will need both (design, not sourced).

**The structure.** The Vessemi lament is an **antiphonal lament**: the *tolo* sings a line, and the household's women answer from the shore, one answer per line, in a low register that is not the *tolo*'s (design, not sourced). The answers are short and formulaic — the dead's kinship term, the name of the river, a call to the water — and they are the same answers in every lament, which is what lets a household that has never heard the song before still take part (design, not sourced).

**The anchor.** The **lament refrain** of the Vessemi shore-burn is the syllable-string *so-ai so-ai*, sung by the *tolo* alone at the head of each stanza. It is non-lexical, which is what keeps the form from sounding like a hymn, and it is the one part of the lament that is not in the shore dialect (design, not sourced).

**The content.** The Vessemi lament uses all four compartments of §6, in the order praise, address, blame, consolation (design, not sourced):

- **Praise of the dead** names the dead's trade and their standing on the water. A specific *tolo* is given the household's dead by name and by trade, so the praise compartment is the family's own material (design, not sourced).
- **Address to the dead** is the second person throughout, and the *tolo* addresses the dead as the salt-master or the boat-keeper, using the trade-term as the vocative (design, not sourced).
- Blame is aimed at the **river and the salt**, never at the living and never at the gods; the river is said to have been thirsty. This keeps the *tolo* inside a rule the Vessemi can enforce, and the rule itself is a real one: a *tolo* who accuses a living person is not paid and is not asked back (design, not sourced).
- **Consolation** is that the tide will come in and the salt will not be lost, which is the flattest possible consolation and the reason the Vessemi laments are said to be hard to sing (design, not sourced).

**The gendered mourning rules.** The Vessemi rule is a prohibition, defended rather than granted: the men of the household do not answer the *tolo*, they stand on the further shore and are not addressed, and a man who answers is not a person who has made a mistake but a person who has no standing to speak for his dead (design, not sourced). The rule is **hard** — it has an enforcement body, the salt-guild, which licenses the *tolo* — and it is visible in every scene, because the further shore is always part of the picture (design, not sourced).

**What the designer gets from this.** A register with a named performer office, a joinable answer, a non-lexical anchor, a full content inventory aimed at a safe target, and a rule with a body to enforce it — a **fictional mourning register** that behaves like an institution rather than a mood (design, not sourced).

**What the designer must still supply.** Whether the dead are mourned for a year or for a season; whether the *tolo*'s office passes to a daughter, a sister or a nephew; what the salt-guild does to a man who answers; and what the Vessemi believe the tide brings. All four are canon decisions, and this entry does not make them (design, not sourced).

## 12. Limits, calibration, and the handoff to the lore world bible

**The base is small and should be read as small.** The sourced material in this entry is five opened sources, each scoped to what its own captured span supports; none of them is a survey of world lament genres, and this entry does not generalise any of them into a typology of dirges (design, not sourced).

**Calibration.** Where a source reports a practice in one tradition, this entry records it as a report about that tradition and as a *design option* for a fictional one. Nothing here is claimed to be a universal property of lament. The four-compartment inventory of §6 and the three-performer division of §3 are the entry's own analytical apparatus applied to the reports; they are not a claim that every tradition organises its grief content under those four headings (design, not sourced).

**No canon, no rights ruling.** This entry makes no claim about what is true in any world, no ruling on the use of any real community's mourning practice, and no judgement about who may adopt it. Whether a particular real-world lament practice may be used by a fictional people is a decision for a canon layer and, where rights are involved, for a rights process; this entry records design surfaces only (design, not sourced).

**Handoff to the lore world bible.** This entry hands the **fictional mourning register** to the world bible as a *surface*, not as canon. What is handed over: the decision table of §9 as a set of open options; the checklist of §10 as a procedure a writer can run; the error patterns of §8 as failure modes to avoid; and the worked example of §11 as a demonstration that the design closes, not as a people to be adopted (design, not sourced). What the lore layer must decide and this entry does not: the occasion, the performer set, the compartment inventory, the gendered rules, the target of the blame, the consolation's doctrine, the archive status, and the names, the language and the history of any invented people (design, not sourced). Kotodama records the surface and the method; it does not record canon, and the handoff is a handoff (`synthesis/worldbible-integration-contract.md`), never an import (design, not sourced).
