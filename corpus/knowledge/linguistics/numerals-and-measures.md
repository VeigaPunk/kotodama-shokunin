## 1. Base typology: six types and what each commits you to

The base of a numeral system is the value *n* in expressions built as *x·n + y* (order and notational conventions irrelevant). Mandarin 26 = *èr-shí-lìu* "two-ten-six" ([2×10]+6) is the transparent type specimen (WALS ch. 131 §1, fetched 2026-10-17).

WALS 131A census (Comrie; 196 languages, fetched 2026-10-17):

| Type | Count | Structure | Worked exemplars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decimal | 125 | x10 + y | Mandarin, English |
| Hybrid vigesimal–decimal | 22 | vigesimal to 99, decimal for hundreds (x100 + y20 + z) | Basque 256 = *berr-eun eta berr-ogei-ta-hama-sei* "two-hundred and two-twenty-and-ten-six" |
| Pure vigesimal | 20 | x20 + y | Diola-Fogny 51 = "two twenties and eleven"; Classical Mayan (prototypical, per §4) |
| Other base | 5 | any non-10/20 base | Ekari base 60 (71 = "one and ten and sixty"); Supyire base 80 (399 = 80×4 + 20×3 + 10 + 5 + 4); Sumerian base 60 |
| Extended body-part | 4 | no arithmetic base; body-point sequence | Kobon, 1–12 up the left arm/trunk, continuing down the right side |
| Restricted | 20 | no productive system above ~20 | Mangarrayi (to 3), Yidiny (to 5), Pirahã (claimed none — §6) |

Four facts a worldbuilder should take from the table:

1. **Decimal is not "natural"; it is just dominant.** 125/196 ≈ 64% of the sample, and the chapter's §4 reads the distribution as a historical trend of decimal encroaching on other systems, not a cognitive necessity. Non-decimal systems are "even more endangered than the languages in which they occur".
2. **Mixed systems are normal, not sloppy.** The chapter assigns a language to one type by the *most productive* base in the range 20–400; Igbo (base 20) builds 1–19 on base 10; Supyire (base 80) builds below 20 on a mixed quinary-decimal base 5. A fictional culture can therefore legitimately use "tens up to 20, twenties after" without breaking typology.
3. **Exponentiation is optional.** Chukchi is vigesimal but has no special word for 400 (= 20×20). English *hundred*/*thousand* are opaque exponents; the transparency of *bi-llion*/*tri-llion* is a Latinate exception, not a rule.
4. **The boundary of "numeral" is a judgement call.** English *seventy* counts as a numeral; *three score and ten* does not, in modern standard usage — but did in earlier English and still does in some regional varieties. *Pair* is a quantifying expression, not a numeral, because it necessarily denotes a set of 2. Fictional legal/ritual registers that need exact counts should specify which expressions count as numerals in that register.

## 2. Numeral words: morphology, transparency, and the traps

Numeral expressions depart from the ideal *x·n + y* formula in ways that are themselves datable, borrowable evidence (WALS ch. 131 §3):

- **Morphophonological idiosyncrasy**: English *fif-ty* (first morpheme transparent, second not); Spanish tens in *-enta* bear no resemblance to *diez* 10 but stay consistent; Russian *sorok* 40 is monomorphemic while 50 is *pjat´-desjat* "five-ten".
- **Portmanteau tens**: Turkish 20–50 (*yirmi, otuz, kırk, elli*) are monomorphemic with addition for the remainder (*yirmi bir* "twenty-one"). A portmanteau tens series is a fossil layer: it usually means the tens were once separate words that fused, and it marks the numeral system as old rather than borrowed whole.
- **Suppletion** at the low end (*one/first, two/second*; and cross-linguistically, "two" and "one" being formally unrelated to their ordinals) is the numeral-system analogue of *go/went*: never regularize it in a sketch unless the sketch declares a reform register.
- **The low-number core is conservative**: numeral words resist borrowing at the low end (the "one"/"two" slot) far more than higher numerals; whole-system borrowing happens (§7) but low numerals usually survive beside the imported set.

Design levers: (a) choose transparency per stratum — an old core (opaque 1–10), a productive system (transparent 11–99), a borrowed layer (the hundreds and above) gives three datable strata in one lexicon; (b) keep at least one irregularity in the core or the language reads as a cipher of English; (c) if the culture has a reform, the reform's signature is exactly the regularization of portmanteau tens and suppletive ordinals — a known contact-modernity trope (cf. Turkish language reform material in `language-contact.md`).

## 3. Ordinals, collectives, distributives — the number-word family

One numeral root set typically feeds a family of derived words; designing the family is what makes a numeral system feel native rather than a list:

- **Cardinals** (the base set).
- **Ordinals** — English *-th* with suppletive *first/second/third*; Japanese ordinals built on Sino numerals with *-banme* (native ordinals via *-me*); Korean *-jjae* `(to-verify)` on forms (Shibatani 1990; Sohn 1999 for the numeral strata).
- **Distributives/collectives** — "two each", Latin *bini/terni* `(to-verify)` (Hurford 1987), English *-some* (*twosome*) and *pair*.
- **Multiplicatives/frequentials** — *twice*, *twofold*, *double* (and *-ple*/*-plex* Latinate layer).
- **Approximatives and fractions** — "a few", "a dozen or so", half/quarter words; fraction words are their own borrowable stratum (compare English *half* vs Latinate *semi-*, and the trade-driven *per cent*).

Failure mode: designing cardinals only. In a market scene, the words actually used are distributive and approximative (*apiece*, *about twenty*), and in a ritual scene they are ordinal and collective. A sketch with cardinals alone cannot stage either scene.

## 4. The numeral–noun interface: classifiers and measure words

Not every language counts a noun directly. WALS ch. 55 (Aikhenvald, fetched 2026-10-17) splits languages three ways by the numeral-plus-noun construction:

| Type | Definition | Exemplars |
|---|---|---|
| No numeral classifiers | numeral attaches to the noun directly | English, Tagalog (*dalawa=ng aso*), Yoruba, Hebrew, Tiwi, Shipibo-Konibo |
| Optional | classifiers present but omissible | Minangkabau (*duo (ikue) anjiang* "two (clf) dog"), Hungarian, Chantyal, Tongan, Haida |
| Obligatory | numeral cannot quantify the noun without a classifier | Vietnamese (*hai con chó*), Boko, Nivkh, Coast Tsimshian, Warekena |

Two distinctions carry the worldbuilding weight:

- **Sortal vs mensural classifiers.** Mensural classifiers (*glass of water*, *pound of sand*, *plume of smoke*) provide a unit of measure for low-countability nouns — most or all languages have them. Sortal classifiers classify count nouns into semantic classes (*urang* human, *ikue* nonhuman animate, *batang* elongated in Minangkabau) — the type WALS maps. The mensural side is where counting meets metrology; the sortal side is where counting meets a culture's noun taxonomy.
- **Classifier choice can be meaning-bearing.** Mandarin *sān zhī huā* (classifier for elongated objects) is "three flowers on their stalks"; *sān duǒ huā* (round objects) focuses the florets. Flexible classifier systems are a semantic resource, not a defect; one-classifier-per-noun systems (as in many languages) are the rigid variant.
- **Register overrides.** Vietnamese textbooks call classifiers obligatory, yet at food stalls *ba gà hai bò* "three chicken two beef" is normal. Ordinary-citation grammar and in-group register differ — a ready-made in-world sociolinguistic marker (`sociolinguistics-registers.md`).
- **Distribution.** Classifiers concentrate in one zone centered on East and Southeast Asia, peter out across South Asia, reappear optionally in western Asia, and extend through Indonesia into Micronesia and western Polynesia; outside that arc they sit in smaller hotbeds (West Africa, Pacific Northwest, Mesoamerica, Amazonia).

Where the measures side lives: unit systems, standards, and money-words belong to `culture-material/trade-economy-lexicon.md`. This entry owns only the *words* — mensural classifiers, counting units (*pair*, *head of cattle*, *dozen*, *score*), and the way a classifier system maps a noun inventory into count classes.

## 5. Counting technologies: fingers, bodies, tallies, knots, rods

The base is not the whole story: what the hands and objects do generates a register and often the lexicon itself.

- **Finger counting** is culturally patterned, not universal: direction (left-to-right, right-to-left), start finger, whether the thumb is a counter or a folder, and whether the same gesture is a digit or a word differ across cultures (Bender & Beller 2012 `(to-verify)` on the statistics; Flegg 1983 for the historical finger-arithmetic traditions). A fictional counting gesture system should be specified like any other body code.
- **Extended body-part systems** (WALS type 5) are tallies, not arithmetic: Kobon runs 1–12 up the left side, 13–23 down the right, then reverses; the same body-part word is multiply ambiguous (*siduŋ* "shoulder" = 10, 14, 33, 37, 56 …). Languages usually have optional or obligatory means to disambiguate side and pass. Oksapmin is the classic ethnographic case: a 27-point count around the upper periphery of the body (Saxe 1981; later documented as shifting under market and school arithmetic — Saxe & Esmonde 2005). **Do not call such systems "base 27"** — WALS is explicit that extended body-part systems make no use of an arithmetic base. This is the single most common worldbuilding error in this area.
- **Tally marks** are the material counterpart: one mark per unit, grouped for legibility. Prehistoric tally interpretations (Ishango bone and similar) are contested in both directions — read Marshack (1972) as the classic argument, and treat any "Paleolithic arithmetic" claim as `(to-verify)`.
- **Knotted-cord and rod records**: the khipu (Inca) and Chinese counting rods are counting *technologies* that generate their own vocabulary and notation (Ascher & Ascher 1981; Urton 1997; `civilizations/inca.md`). Khipu's numeric readings are the well-supported part of its interpretation; narrative readings are contested (see `civilizations/inca.md` for the in-corpus calibration).
- **Numeral notation vs numeral words** is a separate system: cumulative tallies, ciphered signs (Greek alphabetic numerals, Roman), and positional notation with a zero (Babylonian place value; the Indian decimal positional system) are typological stages (Chrisomalis 2010). A culture can count in words fluently and write numerals cumulatively — the mismatch is a dating tool (`writing-systems.md`).

## 6. Restricted and anumeric systems — with the debate calibrated

WALS type 6: 20 languages whose numeral system does not effectively go above ~20 (Mangarrayi to 3, Yidiny to 5); the chapter cites Dan Everett (p.c.) that Pirahã may lack numerals entirely. This is the most contested corner of the whole topic and must be handled as a live disagreement, not a result:

- Everett (2005) argues Pirahã lacks number words and counting; Frank et al. (2008) find approximate-only performance; Gordon (2004) reports no exact counting beyond small quantities.
- Nevins, Pesetsky & Rodrigues (2009) dispute the grammar/cognition claims; C. Everett (2012) re-examines the "supposedly anumeric" characterization.
- In-corpus receipts: `linguistic-anthropology.md` and `semantic-fields-lexicon-building.md` carry these citations with their own calibration; this entry adds the numeral-system frame and does not take a side.

Design use (the useful part): restricted systems are a *realistic* configuration, not a primitive one. A culture with "one, two, many" functions by pairing with an external numeral system for trade, tax, and ritual — exactly what happened historically when restricted systems met decimal states. For fiction, the interesting content is the interface: which domains demand exact counts (tax, contract, ritual calendar) and therefore import numerals, and which stay approximate.

## 7. Diachrony and contact: growth, borrowing, replacement

Three mechanisms produce the numeral systems in the census, and each is a worldbuilding tool:

1. **Growth** — numeral systems build up in stages; the synchronic upper limit of one language can be the historical stage of another. Epps (2006) reconstructs this for the Nadahup family, where numerals can be argued to grow from body-part and quantity expressions into arithmetic systems; Epps et al. (2012) survey numeral complexity across hunter-gatherer languages. Design lever: a young system has transparent, compositional higher numerals; an old one has portmanteau tens and suppletion (§2).
2. **Whole-system borrowing** — Japanese, Korean, and Vietnamese each run a native numeral set beside a Sino-Xenic set (native *hitotsu/futatsu…* vs Sino-Japanese *ichi/ni/san…*; native *hana/dul…* vs Sino-Korean *il/i/sam…*). The imported set takes over higher counts, formal registers, dates, and money; the native set survives in low counts, counting things, and intimacy. This is the cleanest case of a *double numeral system* in the corpus's reference range (Shibatani 1990; Sohn 1999; cf. `language-contact.md`).
3. **Replacement under contact** — the WALS chapter's Mesoamerican case: pre-Conquest systems were largely vigesimal; Spanish *ciento* 100 and then Spanish numeral expressions replaced the higher ranges after the Conquest, so that "in practice Spanish numeral expressions are used for all but the lowest numbers" in many communities. Numeral replacement is one of the sharpest, most datable contact signatures a language can carry — and it is asymmetric: the counting system of the dominated language erodes from the top down.

Stratigraphy rule for fictional worlds: order the numeral strata (native core → borrowed system → modern reform) and date them like loanword layers, using `synthesis/loanword-material-dating.md`; a numeral layer that contradicts the stated contact history is a detectable defect.

## 8. Culture coupling: calendar, trade, symbolism, taboo

- **Calendar arithmetic** — a base choice becomes a calendrical constraint: vigesimal counting underlies Mesoamerican 20-day units and the 360+5 count (`maya-classic.md`); sexagesimal counting underlies Babylonian time and angle units (`mesopotamia-sumer-akkad.md`); the interaction of numeral base and calendar cycle is a design axis in `culture-material/calendar-festival-systems.md`.
- **Trade and measures** — counting units and mensural classifiers are the interface where numerals meet metrology (`trade-economy-lexicon.md`); the market register (*apiece*, *by the dozen*, *two for one*) is where distributives earn their keep (§3).
- **Symbolism** — the meaning of numbers (3, 4, 7, 12, 40, 108 …) and the base→symbolism coupling are `myth-symbol/number-symbolism.md`'s territory; this entry supplies the lexicon that system reads. Practical rule: when `number-symbolism.md` asks "can this culture's counting system even support number X as a salient unit?", the answer comes from this file's base table.
- **Taboo** — numeral taboos (tetraphobia and its analogues) are lexical facts with real-world precedent; the symbolism/valence side is `number-symbolism.md`, and the euphemism mechanics (replacement words, hospital floor numbering, gift counts) are `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md` and `synthesis/taboo-euphemism-lifecycle.md`.

## 9. Decision tables

### T1. Base choice cost sheet

| Choose | You get | You commit to | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| Decimal (125/196) | maximal borrow-compatibility, calendar arithmetic that maps to fingers | transparent tens, exponent words for 100/1000 | reads as default/unmarked — justify it (contact, trade, or a reform) |
| Vigesimal, pure (20) | body-based plausibility; strong Mesoamerican reference frame | 20-count calendar units, score-like trade units | higher ranges need exponent words or stay unwieldy |
| Hybrid vigesimal–decimal (22) | "twenties to 99, hundreds decimal" — historically common | a documented reason for the shift at 100 (contact, reform, or counting practice) | if unmarked, reviewers read it as an error, not a system |
| Other base (5) | distinctive arithmetic; calendar/coinage with real constraints | exponent words at that base; arithmetic in-world that actually works | few reference cultures (Ekari 60, Supyire 80) — harder to source |
| Extended body-part (4) | counting as choreography; rich register of body-part terms | disambiguation rules for side and pass; low upper range | **never** describe it as "base N" — it has no arithmetic base |
| Restricted (20) | realistic "one–two–many" plus an import interface | a named external system for exact domains (tax, trade, ritual) | do not use it to imply cognitive deficit (see §6 calibration) |

### T2. Classifier system design

| Decision | Options | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Classifier presence | absent / optional / obligatory | optional systems give register variation (food-stall style); obligatory systems force every count to name a class |
| Classifier basis | shape, animacy, function, material, social status | becomes a visible slice of the culture's noun taxonomy — audit against `lexicon-coverage-audit.md` field coverage |

| Mensural layer | present in every language | supplies units of measure; hands metrology to `trade-economy-lexicon.md` |

## 10. Worked example (NON-CANON, illustrative only)

A fictional highland culture, *Tavesh*, counts on the body: 1–8 up the left arm to the shoulder, 9–16 down the right arm (mirror tally), then "a full man" = 16. Design, per this entry's tables:

- **Base**: 8 for the arithmetic register (T1 "other base"), with 16 (*man*) and 64 (*four men*, i.e. 8²) as exponent words; the body tally stays for ritual and counting-of-things — the Oksapmin-style coexistence, not a replacement.
- **Words**: opaque core 1–8 with two suppletive ordinals; tens (multiples of 8) portmanteau in the elder register, regularized in the trade register — a two-stratum fossil difference (T3).
- **Classifiers** (T2): obligatory in the ritual register, optional in the market register, three sortal classes (living / elongated / worked-metal) plus a mensural layer for grain and cloth.
- **Contact layer**: a decimal set borrowed from a lowland trade language for the hundreds and above; the borrowed set carries the lowland phonology (e.g. final -n) and is used for prices and dates — replacement from the top down (§7.3).
- **Calendar hook**: the 8-count yields 64-day and 512-day units; the culture's week is 8 days with a named rest day (handed to `calendar-festival-systems.md` for the cycle machinery).
- **Failure modes to test**: does arithmetic in-world actually work in base 8? do the borrowed hundreds conflict with native 64-words? does the ritual register ever need a count above the tally range (then it must borrow or circumlocute — a plot-grade constraint)?

## 11. Calibration, plainly

- **WALS census (ch. 131)**: a 196-language sample with explicit coding decisions ("most productive base in 20–400"); percentages are sample-dependent, and the chapter says so. Use the types and exemplars as generative material; do not quote the counts as universal frequencies.
- **Anumeric/relativity debate (§6)**: live and unresolved; Everett (2005)/Gordon (2004)/Frank et al. (2008) vs Nevins et al. (2009)/C. Everett (2012). No side is adopted here; design claims must not rest on either.
- **Diffusionism**: Seidenberg's "ritual origin of counting" line and similar diffusionist accounts of counting practices are a minority position; treat as `(to-verify)`-grade historical argument, not doctrine.
- **Prehistoric tallies**: Marshack's readings of Paleolithic marks as notation are contested; the safe claim is "marks were made in countable patterns", not "they were arithmetic".
- **Popular histories** (Ifrah; Menninger as cultural history; Kaplan on zero): useful narrative and image sources, secondary evidence; do not cite them for linguistic or archaeological facts that have primary literature.
- **Body-tally ≠ base**: WALS is explicit that extended body-part systems lack an arithmetic base; the "base 27" phrasing common online (including for Oksapmin) is a category error this corpus should not repeat.
- **Glottochronology-adjacent numeral dating**: numeral replacement and layer order are relative-sequence evidence; do not convert them into absolute dates (`historical-linguistics.md` calibration).
- **to-verify items** carried in the frontmatter: Danish *halvfjerds* arithmetic, Latin/Greek distributive morphology, quipu numeric readings beyond the count registers, Ishango-bone tallies, Mangarevan binary steps, finger-counting statistics, and any page-range detail of the library-level bibliography.
