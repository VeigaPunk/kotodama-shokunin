## 2. What a work song is: a task, a crew, a leader and a beat

A work song is not a song about work. It is a song that a crew uses while working, and its parts are fixed by that use: a task that needs a common instant, a crew that supplies the answer, a leader who starts and varies the call, and a beat that falls on the effort.

- Work songs are usually sung by groups of people engaged in heavy, repetitive work; they consist of lines, half-lines or couplets sung with strong rhythm by a soloist and answered by the other workers in chorus; and at its most basic a work song helps the singers establish the rhythm of their work and coordinate their effort and breathing (S1024).
- Where the work cannot be done by one person alone, the individual worker is not free in the measure of his movement but bound to his fellows; the songs are then either pure choral songs or alternating songs, and in the alternating kind the precentor is also the foreman (S1021).
- A chanteyman's own account of the effect: "It was only just a few words we had, but it made the thing come lighter"; the chantey infected the work with the spirit of play (S1019).
- So far as the music was concerned a shanty was a song with a chorus, rendered by one singer called the shantyman while the sailors performed their work in time with the music (S1018).

The identity of singer and work-leader is the structural fact the rest of this entry builds on (design, not sourced). It is why a work song has an office rather than a soloist, and why the beat can be authoritative without anyone giving an order. In the maritime, Gaelic and Sea Islands reports the song is the device by which a gang's separate exertions become one movement (S1018, S1019, S1020, S1022, S1024, S1025, S1026); the Kalinga page names occasions without claiming coordination (S1027).

Four slots therefore define the object, and a design that leaves one empty has not made a work song (design, not sourced):

- **the task**, and the part of it that needs a common instant;
- **the crew**, and what each member does with hands and breath while singing;
- **the leader**, who starts, varies and stops the call;
- **the beat**, which marks the effort rather than the interval between efforts.

A song that satisfies all four can still be bad, but a song that lacks one is a different object: without the task it is a song about labour, without the crew it is a solo, without the leader it is a chorus with no one to start it, and without the beat it is a lyric that happens to be sung at work (design, not sourced).

## 3. The song leader, the call and response, and the crew's answer

The leader is the load-bearing part, and the sources describe the office in remarkably consistent terms.

- The shantyman took up his position near the workers and announced the shanty, sometimes by singing the first line, which established the tune the crew was to answer; he was a privileged person, excused all work save light or odd jobs, and a good one with a pretty wit was worth his weight in gold (S1018).
- The chanteyman sang the refrain or a shortened form of it by way of introduction, to announce the chantey and remind his hearers of the tune; a capable chanteyman was said in the Merchant Service to be worth a couple of extra hands (S1019).
- The leading singer starts the words of each verse, often improvising, and the others, who "base" him, strike in with the refrain, or even join in the solo when the words are familiar; when the "base" begins, the leader often stops, leaving the rest of his words to be guessed at or taken up by another singer (S1020).
- There was a recognised leader of song in every boat; he started whatever air he pleased, and no matter what jumble of words came first he aimed at turning them into lines that would jingle, the rest following and keeping time most faithfully (S1022).
- In the waulking team the soloist calculated the psychological moment to raise the tune after the thumping had already begun (S1024).

The answer is not a decorative refrain; it is the crew's half of a division of labour (design, not sourced). The stop in the Sea Islands account is the interesting detail: the leader yields the line once the crew has taken it, which is what makes the form a handover rather than a performance. Call and response, then, is a coordination grammar with three positions rather than two — the leader's call, the crew's answer, and the next motion — and the answer does three jobs at once: it confirms that the crew has heard, it sets the instant of the effort, and it gives the leader a place to rest and think of the next verse (design, not sourced). A chorus that answers nothing is a refrain; a call that expects no answer is a solo.

The boundary with the encounter sense of the phrase is sharp: karanga is a call and response of welcome and challenge at a meeting, owned by `civilizations/maori.md:52`, and it coordinates no labour (design, not sourced).

## 4. Work rhythm and the task: hauling song, rowing, pounding, field and craft

The rhythm is where a work song is most easily faked, because a regular beat is easy to write and a task is not regular. The opened sources separate two rhythmic situations, and the separation is the most useful single tool in this entry.

- Work in alternating rhythm has several workers alternate their exertions to produce the rhythm the task needs; in work in uniform rhythm all exert themselves at the same moment, and the songs there become almost like a continuous command (S1021).
- The capstan-chantey accompanies work of a regular rhythmical character such as marching round the capstan or working the pumps, while the pulling-chantey accompanies work that is irregular or spasmodic, such as a succession of hard pulls to raise the yards (S1019).
- For the pulling case the pulls must be made in perfect accord, regular intervals for rest must let the workers take a fresh grip and recover breath, and the song must interest them and occupy their attention (S1019).
- Hauling shanties were shorter than capstan ones and came in two types, one for the long hoist with two short choruses and a double pull in each, and one for the short pull or "sweating-up" with a single short chorus and one very strong pull; so much effort was needed at that pull that it was difficult to sing a musical note, and the last word was usually shouted (S1018).
- Capstan work was continuous — walking round the capstan pushing the bars — and its tunes and choruses were longer with much greater variety of rhythm (S1018).
- In the Sea Islands collection two measures were sung to each stroke, the first accented by the beginning of the stroke and the second by the rattle of the oars in the rowlocks; the passenger boat at the ferry rowed from sixteen to thirty strokes a minute, twenty-four being the average, and the livelier tunes were kept for light loads while slower ones were used when the load was heavy or the tide against the boat (S1020).
- Purser reports steady inter-island rowing at 25-30 strokes a minute, a recorded maximum of 46 under oar in the trireme trials, and the sea state as the main determinant of stroke speed and length; with singing and rowing, both can be adapted to each other, but the needs of the rowers come first (S1025).
- The waulking was heavy work with the cloth wet, the rhythm a brisk four-time, and every action in time to singing; quoting Collinson, Bourke records that the thumping invariably began before the song, and that the preliminary rhythmic pulsing could last from a bar or two to nearly a full minute before the soloist raised the tune (S1024).
- The waulking work was measured by the song rather than the clock: towards the end of the job the cloth was measured at intervals, and if it had not shrunk enough another song was sung, each song lasting about eight minutes and no song sung twice (S1024).
- Tug-om is sung by women while pounding rice or coffee (S1027).
- Suggiyaw is a harvest song, often sung in the rice field after a day's work of cutting and hauling in the ripe grain (S1027).
- Dagdag-ay is a solo song, sung only by men, usually during a resting time from work or travel (S1027).
- Soldiers' marching songs, rowing songs and the waulking songs of the Scottish weavers are labour songs of a similar type, because occupations of that kind give rise to regular rhythmical movements that a song can accompany and emphasise (S1019).
- Blankenhorn's classification groups waulking and milling songs, clapping songs and rowing songs under songs to facilitate group labour, and puts milking, spinning and quern songs under songs of occupation (S1023).
- The title "dreg-song" seems to have been given in Scotland to labour songs in general and in particular to those of the harvest field (S1022).

The design rule that follows is narrow: put the beat on the effort, not on the bar (design, not sourced). A hauling song that marks the pull, a rowing song that marks the catch and the rattle of the oars, and a waulking song that marks the thump are all doing the same thing; a song whose accents fall between the efforts is a song the crew will fight.

## 5. The shanty and other occupational repertories

The shanty is the occupational repertoire the opened sources document most fully (inference), and it is also the clearest case of a repertoire whose life is tied to a technology.

- Whall, who collected at sea from 1861, writes that since 1872 he had not heard a shanty or song worth the name, and that steam spoilt them (S1026).
- The foreword to Terry's collection states the same fact from the other end: the tunes died out with the sailing vessel and now belong to a chapter of maritime history that is definitely closed (S1018).
- Whall refuses the shore spelling "chanty" and keeps "shanty", on the ground that it was not so pronounced at sea and that the presumed French derivation is wrong (S1026).
- Terry divides shanties by use into hauling shanties and windlass and capstan shanties, and notes that a few were interchangeable between halliards and capstan (S1018).
- Sharp's two kinds are the capstan-chantey, for work of a regular rhythmical character, and the pulling-chantey, for work that is irregular or spasmodic (S1019).
- Collinson's oyster dredgers sang from the moment the dredge was driven until it was hauled up, and their leader started whatever air he pleased (S1022).
- Purser's Hebridean iorram covers boat songs and other rhythmic work songs, and many rowing songs survived in an alternative use as waulking songs when serious rowing declined (S1025).
- Bourke records that no genuine waulking of cloth appears to have been done on Barra since the Second World War, and that the recordings made in the 1960s used a simulated waulking, with women pounding a dry blanket in the way they remembered working the wet tweed (S1024).
- Blankenhorn's example is the sharpest: a song whose text is a lament has come down as a waulking song with a lively tempo and a choral refrain, and she asks whether those features were intended from the start (S1023).

That is the repertoire's life cycle in one page: a task creates a stock of songs, the stock outlives the task, and the songs either change function — waulking song to concert piece, rowing song to waulking song, work song to lament or dance tune — or they stop (design, not sourced). A fictional tradition that gives a trade a repertoire but no death and no afterlife has skipped the part of the institution that a reader can check.

The word itself is worth a design note: a fictional culture will have its own name for the repertoire, and the name should come from the people who worked the task rather than from the collectors who wrote it down (design, not sourced).

## 6. Improvised verses, complaint and satire licensed by the work

The licence is the part of a work song that most often goes wrong in fiction, because a writer wants the song to say something and forgets that someone in the scene has to permit it. The sources are clear that the improvisation is real and that it is bounded.

- The shantyman sang a stereotyped opening of one or more verses, then improvised words for all succeeding verses, with topics that were many and varied — personal allusions to the crew and officers, sarcastic criticism of the quality of the food, wistful references to the good time coming on shore — and no need for connection or relevancy between verses, nor for rhymes; the main thing was that the rhythm be preserved and the words keep the workers merry or interested (S1018).
- Once the improvisation began, things became so intimate and personal as to be unprintable (S1018).
- The chanteyman kept a store of stock lines or "tags" to draw on when inspiration failed, and it was the excitement of the improvised words that riveted the workers' attention (S1019).
- Collinson records a dredging song about Lady Hyndford, a former proprietress who had been kind to the fishermen: the dredgers saw her coming down the brae one night and simply put her ladyship into their dredging song, with the milk for her and the whey for them (S1022).
- In the account of how a song is made, a rower describes singing about the driver who kept calling on the gang as they toted rice, one man answering with his mother's warning about drivers, and then the singer "made a sing, just puttin' a word, and den anudder word", at which the others joined in the chorus as if it were an old acquaintance (S1020).
- A second account describes a song made at a praise-meeting about a master's order of a short peck of corn and a hundred lashes, worked into shape by singers who knew how, "work it in — work it in" until they got it right (S1020).
- In passenger and troop ships the sailors were given to understand very forcibly that their words were to be decent or that they were not to shanty at all, and when the passengers were landed and the prohibition removed the notorious "Hog-Eye Man" at once made its appearance (S1026).

The song is not a petition and not a trial; it is a licensed piece of insolence inside a working day (design, not sourced). The licence is bounded by who can hear: the same crew, the same repertoire, a different audience, and a different permitted text.

Two boundaries keep this section honest. The mockery in a work song is not a song duel: nobody adjudicates it, nothing is settled, and there is no stake beyond the crew's own standing with each other and with the man who hears them (design, not sourced; the contest frame is `linguistics/verbal-duelling-ritual-insult.md:91-109`). And the complaint is not praise: it builds no patron and seeks no reward, which is why the praise office at `linguistics/praise-poetry-panegyric.md:69-97` is not the model here (design, not sourced).

## 8. Decision procedure: a fictional work-song tradition whose songs stay tied to their task, crew, leader and setting

The sequence below is a set of decisions to make in order, not a form to fill in (design, not sourced).

**Before a song is written:**

1. Fix the **task**: what the crew is actually doing, and which part of it needs a common instant — a heave, a stroke, a blow, a step, a turn.
2. Fix the **motion signature**: how long one unit of the task takes, whether it is cyclic or one-off, and where in the cycle the effort peaks. The song must mark the peak, not the interval between peaks.
3. Fix the **crew**: how many sing, whether they are all doing the same motion, and whether any of them is not (a steersman, a cook, a child carrying water).
4. Fix the **leader's office**: who may call, how the office is acquired, whether it is paid, and whether it can be lost. A leader is a role inside the crew, not a soloist in front of it.
5. Fix the **answer**: what the crew sings back, how long the answer is, and what the answer does to the next stroke. An answer that does not close the cycle is a chorus, not a response.
6. Fix the **verse stock**: what the leader may improvise about — the crew, the cargo, the employer, the weather, the road — and what is off limits.
7. Fix the **licence**: what complaint, mockery and satire the work permits, in front of whom, and what happens when the licence is exceeded.
8. Fix the **setting**: where the song is sung, who else can hear it, and which of those hearers must not hear certain verses.
9. Fix the **life cycle**: how a song is learned, who owns it, how it is retired, and what happens to it when the task disappears.
10. Fix the **off-job use**: whether the song can be sung away from the work, and what changes when it is.

**While drafting:**

11. Put the **beat on the effort**, not on the bar; a song that is metrically regular and task-irregular will drift against the motion.
12. Give the **call a job**: name, warn, count, mock or instruct. A call that only fills time is decoration.
13. Let the **answer be short**; the crew must be able to breathe and work at the same time.
14. Keep the **verse stock local**: a verse about a place the crew has never seen is a poem, not a work song.
15. Show the **hearer**: an overseer, a passenger, a customer or a child is part of the scene, and their presence changes what can be sung.
16. Mark the **improvisation** as improvisation in the fiction; a leader whose verses are all fixed is a reciter, not a caller.
17. Give the **song a cost**: breath, attention, a share of the wage, a fine for a wrong call — something the crew pays for the coordination.

**Before the tradition is called finished:**

18. Test every scene against the failures listed in §9.
19. Confirm that this entry has not restated a neighbour: composition technique belongs to the orality entry, melody migration to the lullaby entry, mourning antiphony to the lament entry, the sung contest to the duelling entry, the drinking round to the toast entry, the surrogate channel to the speech-surrogate entry.
20. Check that nothing canonical has been decided here: the trade, the crew, the leader, the verses and the licence belong to the lore layer.

**Worked example (NON-CANON).** A salt-pan town on a tidal flat keeps a **sluice song** sung by the crew that opens the feeder channels at low water. The task is a single heave on a wooden bar every four heartbeats, repeated until the gate is up; the song's answer falls on the heave, and the leader's call fills the three beats between. The leader is the **gate-caller**, an office held by whoever has worked the pan longest, unpaid but exempt from the heaviest lift. The verse stock is the tide, the salt price, the overseer's boots and the last crew that lost a gate; the licence runs to mockery of the overseer but not of the tide, because a tide mocked in song is a tide that will take the gate. The song is not sung off the pan: sung at a wedding it becomes a dance tune with the calls replaced by nonsense, and the crew says the tune has "gone over" — the boundary case §9 item 4 describes. The town's lore layer decides whether the gate-caller exists, who holds the office and what the tide does; this paragraph only shows the decisions above in one place.

## 9. Error patterns: work songs that fail

Every failure below is one slot left empty or broken; each comes with a test that can fail (design, not sourced).

**1. The work song with no task.** The song has a beat, a leader and a chorus, and nothing that the beat could be timing. The test: ask what motion the crew makes on the strong beat, and what would go wrong if the song stopped. If the answer is *nothing*, the song is a song about work, not a work song.

**2. The call with no response.** The leader calls and the crew listens, or the crew sings a chorus that does not answer the call. The test: ask what the crew's part does to the next motion. If the answer is *nothing*, the form is a solo with accompaniment.

**3. The rhythm that does not fit the motion.** The song is metrically regular and the task is not: a heave that takes one and a half beats is forced into a two-beat bar, or a stroke rate is set by the tune rather than by the water. The test: count the task's units against the song's beats for twenty cycles. If they drift, the rhythm is wrong, however good the tune.

**4. The shanty sung off the job with no change of function.** The song is performed in a concert room, a film or a festival with the same words and the same form, and nothing about it changes except the audience. The test: ask what the song is doing now that it is not coordinating a motion. If the answer is *the same thing*, the fiction has not noticed that the function has changed; if the answer is *entertainment*, say so and let the form change with it.

**5. The satire with no licence.** The leader mocks the employer, the overseer or the customer, and no one in the fiction has granted the licence, bounded it, or can withdraw it. The test: ask who may be mocked, in front of whom, and what the punishment is for going too far. If the answer is *anyone, anywhere, nothing*, the satire is the author's, not the crew's.

**6. The crew with no leader and the leader with no crew.** Either every verse is communal and no one starts it, or one singer performs and the crew is an audience. The test: ask who decides when the next verse starts. If the answer is *no one* or *the author*, the office is missing.

**7. The song that could time any task.** The words are generic labour sentiment and the beat is a generic four. The test: swap the task — hauling for rowing, pounding for hoeing — and reread. If nothing in the song has to change, the song was never tied to its task.

**8. The repertoire with no trade.** The songs are called work songs but no occupation, tool, material or hazard appears in them. The test: ask what trade a verse could only have been sung in. If no verse answers, the repertoire is a genre label.

**9. The setting that cannot be heard.** The song is sung where no one could hear it, or where everyone could, and the fiction never says which. The test: name the nearest hearer and what they do with what they hear. If the answer is *no one* or *everyone, equally*, the setting has not been decided.

**10. The song that outlives its task with no trace.** The trade vanishes and the song survives unchanged, with no shift of function, no loss of verses and no new owner. The test: ask what the song is for now and who sings it. If the answer is *the same*, the tradition has no life cycle.

**11. The complaint that costs nothing.** The crew sings a grievance and no one in the fiction reacts — not the employer, not the overseer, not the crew itself. The test: ask what the song risks. If the answer is *nothing*, the complaint is scenery.

**12. The tradition that belongs to no crew.** The songs could be sung by any workers in any world with any cargo. The test: name one thing these songs say about these workers that no other crew's songs could say.

## 10. Checklist

Apply these checks to each work-song scene while it is being drafted (design, not sourced):

- [ ] Is the **task** named, and does the song time a specific part of it?
- [ ] Is the **motion signature** known — cycle length, effort peak, one-off or repeated?
- [ ] Is the **crew** defined, and does every singer work while singing?
- [ ] Is the **leader's office** defined — recruitment, payment, revocability?
- [ ] Does the **answer** close the cycle, and is it short enough to breathe through?
- [ ] Is the **verse stock** local to the trade, the place and the crew?
- [ ] Is the **licence** for complaint and satire granted, bounded and revocable?
- [ ] Is the **setting** decided — who hears, and who must not?
- [ ] Is the **life cycle** present — learning, ownership, retirement, survival after the task?
- [ ] Is the **off-job use** decided, and does the form change when the function does?
- [ ] Does the song pass the **swap test** of §9 item 7?
- [ ] Are the **neighbour boundaries** respected: no composition engine, no melody-migration theory, no lament, no sung contest, no drinking round, no surrogate channel?
- [ ] Does the tradition belong to **these** workers, or would it survive a move to any world untouched?

## 11. What these sources reach, and the handoff to lore

**How far each source reaches.** No source in the base is a survey of the genre: each of the field, collection and archival sources reports work song inside one setting, and Bücher is the exception — a comparative treatise whose developmental scheme is not used here (S1021). The entry therefore claims no grammar of the genre as a whole (design, not sourced).

- Whall collected at sea from 1861 and dates the disappearance of the shanty to the coming of steam (S1026).
- Terry's collection reports the English-language sea repertoire of the sailing-ship era (S1018).
- Sharp's collection reports English folk-chanteys from a different collector's hand (S1019).
- The Sea Islands collection reports rowing, corn and general labour songs in one coastal region of the United States in the 1860s (S1020).
- Purser reports Hebridean rowing songs and their stroke speeds (S1025).
- Blankenhorn reports a classification of Gaelic song and the waulking repertoire within it (S1023).
- Bourke describes the waulking team, its invitations, its table and its songs (S1024).
- Collinson reports one Scottish oyster fishery from printed and oral sources (S1022).
- Bücher is a comparative nineteenth-century treatise, not a field report (S1021).
- The Mangali page describes a Kalinga repertoire of vocal song types in one valley, including a pounding song, a harvest song and a solo song for rest (S1027).

**Calibration.** The alternating/uniform distinction taken from Bücher is usable and is corroborated in the maritime and Gaelic material (inference). His developmental scheme, which orders peoples on a ladder from "natural" to "cultural", is a nineteenth-century stage theory and is discredited as a ladder: only the two rhythmic situations are used here, and no claim is made that any culture is earlier or later on such a scale (design, not sourced).

- Purser reports Collinson's suggestion that there were two types of Gaelic rowing song, one for larger and one for smaller vessels, and states that he could not verify it despite wide enquiry; it stays a suggestion here and supports nothing (S1025).
- The 1867 collection is a period document: its descriptions of practice are used, its racial vocabulary and its judgments are not, and no sentence here rests on its framing (S1020).

The Mangali page is a tier-B institutional description: it names song types and their occasions in one line each, and this entry takes from it only those names and occasions (design, not sourced).

**Where description stops.** A work song is a genre name and a use, not a transcript of anyone's afternoon. The recorded verses stay records; the described practices stay descriptions of one setting. The entry settles no world's facts and grants no permission to borrow a living community's songs; the first belongs to lore-shokunin and the second is a rights question (design, not sourced).

**Known seams, recorded rather than repaired.** Three seams are open at the base and are not this entry's to close. First, `culture-material/material-culture-inference-method.md:15` still routes "work songs" to `linguistics/orality-formulaic-language.md`; that pointer is stale, the orality entry owns no work-song surface, and the correction belongs to a later round that may edit the sibling (design, not sourced). Second, `culture-material/maritime-seafaring-lexicon.md:29` already names "the songs that pace shared labour" inside the hull's working-register row and asks "who times the work"; this entry cites that row one way and does not re-derive shipboard command vocabulary, so the seam is a shared pointer rather than a duplicated claim (design, not sourced). Third, `linguistics/sacred-registers-liturgical-language.md:101-107` owns chant as an enforced recitation norm; a labour chant has no doctrine, no institution and no fixed target text, and this entry borrows nothing from that surface (design, not sourced).

**Handoff.** What lore-shokunin receives is working material rather than a canon: the four slots of §2, the leader's office and the three jobs of the answer (§3), the alternating/uniform distinction and the task-fit rules (§4), the repertoire's life cycle (§5), the licence and its audience bound (§6), the table of §7, the build procedure of §8, the failure list of §9 and the checklist of §10. The world bible decides the trade, the crew, the leader, the verses, the licence and what any song is worth in the fiction (design, not sourced).

## 12. Inference ledger

The readings listed here are mine; none of the opened sources puts them this way. Each is tagged *inference* or *design, not sourced* as it appears, and a reader who rejects one keeps every sourced claim beside it (design, not sourced).

- That the four slots — task, crew, leader, beat — are separable, and that a song missing one is a different object rather than a worse work song (inference).
- That the leader's stop when the crew takes the line is a handover rather than a lapse, and that it is what makes the form a division of labour (inference).
- That the answer does three jobs at once — confirmation, timing and a rest for the leader — and that a chorus which does none of them is a refrain (inference).
- That the alternating/uniform distinction predicts which part of a task a song can mark, and therefore which songs are interchangeable between tasks (inference).
- That the tune stock, not the individual song, is the unit a crew owns, which is why a repertoire can survive a change of task while a single song changes function (inference).
- That the licence is bounded by the nearest hearer rather than by the song's content, so the same verse can be permitted on the water and forbidden in harbour (inference).
- That a repertoire's death is a design event with a date and a cause, and that a tradition without one reads as a list of songs rather than an institution (inference).
- That the work song's complaint is not a contest and not a praise, because it has no adjudicator and no patron (inference).
