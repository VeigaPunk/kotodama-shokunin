## 1. Typology: classify by the sign-to-speech mapping

Daniels' framework (Daniels 1990; Daniels & Bright 1996): classify a script by **which linguistic unit one basic sign encodes**, not by glyph shape or beauty.

| Type | One sign encodes | Vowel handling | Canonical examples | Typical inventory |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| logosyllabary (logographic) | word/morpheme, plus syllabic readings | fully present inside phonetic readings | Sumerian cuneiform, Chinese hanzi, Maya glyphs, Egyptian | hundreds → several thousand |
| syllabary | syllable (mostly CV) or mora | built into the sign | Japanese kana, Linear B (~87 signs), Vai (~200), Cherokee (85), Cypriot | ~40–90 (up to ~200) |
| abjad | consonant | mostly unmarked; optional diacritics | Phoenician, Aramaic, Hebrew, Arabic | 22–30 |
| alphabet | consonant *and* vowel, equal footing | full | Greek, Latin, Cyrillic, Runic, Hangul | 16–40 |
| abugida | consonant + **inherent vowel** | default vowel overridden by diacritics or conjuncts | Devanagari and the Brahmic family, Ethiopic (namesake: Ge'ez fidel order runs *ä-bu-gi-da*), Canadian Aboriginal syllabics (Daniels' classing) | ~30–60 base; hundreds of combos |
| featural (supplementary class) | distinctive phonetic features | — | Hangul; Pitman shorthand | — |
| semasiography (not writing proper) | meaning directly, not speech | — | math notation, Blissymbolics (Bliss 1949), traffic signs | — |

Craft notes:

- **Pure types don't exist.** Egyptian = word signs + ~24 uniconsonantal signs + silent determinatives (Gardiner's sign list: ~750 signs). Japanese = kanji (logographic) + kana (moraic) on the same page. Maya = logosyllabic mix.
- **"Logographic" is the least pure class.** Chinese characters are ~80–90% phono-semantic compounds (xíngshēng) per Xu Shen's six-category liushu classification in the *Shuowen Jiezi* (c. 100 CE): sound is inside the sign. DeFrancis 1989 dismantled the "ideograms bypass sound" reading.
- **Determinatives/classifiers are a reusable fiction device:** silent semantic tags (Sumerian ~25 common ones: god, man, place, wood, fish; Egyptian determinatives after phonetic spellings) disambiguate homophones without extra phonetic writing. Cheap to invent, highly authentic.

## 2. Calibration: discredited and contested frameworks

- **Gelb's unilinear evolution** (logography → syllabary → alphabet as "progress"; monogenesis in Sumer) is **discredited**. Polygenesis (3–4 independent inventions) is standard; scripts adapt to language and medium rather than evolve "upward." Gelb himself softened monogenesis in the 1963 2nd edition (Naveh 1982; Daniels 1990). Never write a world where alphabets are the teleological goal.
- **"Chinese characters are pure ideograms"** — refuted as description (DeFrancis 1989; Unger 2004). The myth recurs in conscription as "a universal script readable by all": Blissymbolics promised this and failed without a phonetic layer and grammar. The real cross-dialect legibility of hanzi comes from shared literary education, not magic.
- **"The alphabet made Greek philosophy/logic possible"** (Havelock 1963; Goody's literacy thesis): technological determinism, deprecated among classicists and anthropologists. Fiction fuel, not fact.
- **Independent inventions:** Mesopotamia (c. 3300 BCE) and China (oracle bones c. 1250 BCE; Neolithic symbol corpora like Jiahu have no demonstrable continuity) are consensus-independent. Mesoamerica is broadly accepted as independent (Zapotec c. 600 BCE; Olmec Cascajal Block c. 900 BCE, Rodríguez Martínez et al. 2006 — authenticity still contested). Egypt (tomb U-j labels, c. 3250 BCE) is the debate: independent vs stimulus diffusion from Mesopotamia (Dreyer et al. 1998; Baines 2004; unresolved).
- **Indus/Harappan script:** undeciphered; Farmer, Sproat & Witzel (2004) argue it may not even be writing (texts too short and formulaic); contested, and the dispute is unresolved. If you want a genuinely mysterious ancient script, model this real uncertainty, not a solved puzzle with a warm hole.
- **Quipu (Andes):** recording technology with no demonstrated phonetic encoding; Urton's binary-cord hypothesis remains open research. Empires can run without writing — the "writing = civilization" equation is a Victorian relic.
- **"Hangul is the most scientific script":** featural design is real and learner-friendly, but "unique perfection" is nationalist-era rhetoric; acquisition speed also reflects pedagogy and state policy.

## 3. Evolution lineages worth cloning

### 3.1 Sumerian → cuneiform (the accounting-first path)

- Uruk IV tablets, c. 3300 BCE: several hundred to ~900 pictographic signs. Medium = reed stylus on wet clay → abstract wedge forms by Early Dynastic III (~2500 BCE); writing direction rotated 90° during the 3rd millennium, sign shapes followed.
- Invention engine: Schmandt-Besserat's token theory (clay counting tokens → impressed envelopes → tablets) — influential, contested in detail by Assyriologists.
- Key devices: **rebus principle** (spell homophones phonetically; attested in the earliest phonetic stage); determinatives; polyvalence (one sign, many readings per language and genre).
- Death: cuneiform outlived Sumerian (serving Akkadian, Hittite, Elamite) then died under alphabetic competition; latest tablets are 1st-century CE astronomical ephemerides (75 CE Babylon almanac — Sachs 1976; latest now an Uruk almanac of 79/80 CE — Hunger & de Jong 2014).
- Lesson: writing begins as bookkeeping and labels for a temple economy; literature is a late add-on. Script shape is tool physics — stylus angle makes wedges.

### 3.2 Egyptian → abjad → alphabets (the acrophony path)

- Hieroglyphs from c. 3250 BCE (Abydos, tomb U-j); full system by Dynasty 1 (~3100 BCE). Media split: chisel on stone (monumental) vs reed pen on papyrus (hieratic from ~2600 BCE; demotic c. 650 BCE). Coptic = Greek alphabet + 6–7 demotic letters (2nd–4th c. CE); extinct as a spoken language ~17th c., survives liturgically.
- **Invention of the abjad:** Proto-Sinaitic inscriptions at Serabit el-Khadim (found by Petrie 1905; Gardiner 1916 read *bʿlt* "the Lady" = Hathor), c. 1900–1700 BCE; Wadi el-Hol inscriptions (Darnells, 1994) show Canaanite experimentation on the Nile road. Goldwasser 2006: Canaanite semilingual miners applied **acrophony** to Egyptian uniconsonantals — one sign per West Semitic consonant.
- Spread: Phoenician 22 letters (c. 1050 BCE) → Aramaic (imperial lingua franca under Assyria/Persia) → Hebrew, Arabic; and → **Greek c. 800 BCE with vowels**: Greeks repurposed consonant letters as vowels (aleph→alpha, he→epsilon, yod→iota, ayin→omicron, waw→upsilon) because Greek phonology demanded vowels on the page; → Etruscan → Latin (7th c. BCE). I/J and U/V split only in early modern Europe; W is a medieval double-V.
- Lesson: a script carries the donor's phonological blind spots. No vowels is a *feature* for Semitic root-and-pattern morphology (vowels are inflection), a *bug* elsewhere — and borrowers patch it (Greek vowels; Persian's four added Arabic letters; Kurdish written with full vowels).

### 3.3 Brahmi family (the abugida path)

- Secure start: Ashokan edicts, c. 250 BCE. Earlier epigraphy contested (Anuradhapura potsherds 4th c. BCE; Tamil-Brahmi claims to the 5th c. BCE — dates debated). Origin is an open fight: Bühler 1895 (Aramaic derivation), South Semitic thesis, indigenous invention — Salomon 1998 surveys, no consensus. Kharosthi (Gandhara, right-to-left, Aramaic-derived) coexisted and died by ~3rd–4th c. CE.
- Abugida mechanics: consonant letters carry inherent /a/; the virama "killer" suppresses it for bare consonants; clusters written as conjuncts; traditional charts order vowels a-i-u-e-o.
- Branching (one ancestor → ~200 modern scripts): Gupta (4th c. CE) → Siddham/Sharada/Nāgarī → Devanagari (~10th–13th c.); southern Pallava/Grantha → Tamil, Khmer, Mon, Thai, Lao, Burmese; Kawi → Javanese/Balinese, Baybayin (Philippines, attested 16th c.). Siddham stayed in Japanese Buddhist copying for Sanskrit after dying in India.
- Lesson: every daughter script is a phonology patch — inherent vowels changed, tone marks added (Thai), conjuncts rebuilt for local clusters. The horizontal headstroke (shirorekha) is often explained by palm-leaf media — a folk etymology, reported but not demonstrated in the epigraphic literature.

### 3.4 Fresh starts (proof scripts can be engineered)

- **Hangul:** created 1443, promulgated 1446 (*Hunminjeongeum*); consonant glyphs encode articulator and manner (featural); letters compose into syllable blocks — alphabetic phonology, syllabic typography. Ledyard 1966/1997 proposed 'Phags-pa (Mongol script) as partial model: plausible, debated.
- **Avestan script** (Sassanian era, ~5th–6th c. CE): ~40+ letters engineered to record an older oral liturgy with phonemic precision.
- **Cree syllabics:** James Evans, 1840s; shapes from shorthand systems, rotation encodes vowels; spread to Inuktitut. **Cherokee:** Sequoyah 1821, 85 signs; letter shapes lifted from a Latin book, values original.
- Indigenous inventions column: **Vai** (Liberia, 1830s, ~200 signs), **N'Ko** (Kanté, 1949), **Pahawh Hmong** (Shong Lue Yang, 1959; tone written with letters — Smalley 1990), **Adlam** (Barry brothers, 1980s, for Fulfulde).
- Lesson: new scripts live when they solve a community's felt need *and* get institutional backing; design elegance alone dies (most constructed scripts of history are extinct).

## 4. Script spread and reform (era-building material)

| Event | Date | Mechanic | Worldbuilding use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Qin standardizes small seal script | 221 BCE | state unification | script reform as empire-building |
| Greeks add vowels | c. 800 BCE | acrophonic repurposing | borrower patches donor's blind spot |
| Aramaic imperial spread | 8th–6th c. BCE | lingua franca | script follows empire |
| Islam carries Arabic script | 7th–20th c. | religion | sacred-script prestige; *ajami* adaptations (Persian پ چ ژ گ; Hausa hooked letters) |
| Devanagari matures | ~10th–13th c. | scribal drift | script families over centuries |
| Aramaic→Syriac→Sogdian→Old Uyghur→Mongolian→Manchu | 1st c. CE–17th c. | trade-route chain, rotated vertical | scripts travel and turn 90° |
| Hangul promulgated | 1446 | royal decree | top-down design vs scribes' conservatism |
| Turkish adopts Latin | 1928 | nation-state break | script change as identity surgery |
| Vietnamese quốc ngữ official | early 20th c. (coined 17th c. by Jesuits) | colonialism + literacy campaign | romanization wins via bureaucracy |
| PRC simplification / Japan shinjitai | 1956/1964 / 1946+ | literacy politics | divergent standards split one script community |
| Cyrillic→Latin waves | post-1991 | geopolitics | script as foreign-policy signal |

- **Digraphia** — one language, two scripts (Hindi/Urdu; Serbian Cyrillic/Latin; Chinese trad/simp) — is the Eurasian norm for multilingual polities. A culture with exactly one script and no scribal politics is the anomaly.
- **Literacy texture:** Greco-Roman texts ran as *scriptura continua* (no word spaces); Irish scribes popularized word separation (7th–8th c.; Saenger 1997). Punctuation is late and uneven. Tironian shorthand notes numbered ~4,000 in Tiro's core system and ~5,000 after Seneca's systematization (Isidore, *Etym.* I.21–22), expanding to ~13,000 in Carolingian monasteries; one Tironian sign survives in modern Irish. Your conscript needs a punctuation/abbreviation layer or it reads like an alien bureaucrat wrote it.
- Literacy cost by script: alphabet ~26 signs; syllabary 50–90; abugida ~40–60 base + conjuncts; hanzi ~2,500–3,500 graphs for functional literacy (PRC common-character tables; Japan's jōyō list: 2,136). The size of the scribe class is a first-order social variable.

## 5. Decipherment: solved vs unsolved, and the design lessons

Solved — what actually broke them:

| Case | Key(s) | Lesson |
|---|---|---|
| Egyptian (Champollion 1822) | Rosetta Stone (1799); cartouches of Ptolemy/Cleopatra; Young's partial phonetics (1818–19) | bilinguals + royal names first, then extend phonetics to the whole system (Allen 2000; Pope 1999) |
| Old Persian (Grotefend 1802; Rawlinson 1835–46) | Behistun trilingual; recurring royal-genealogy name patterns | names + known genre formulas |
| Akkadian (1850s) | second column of Behistun | 1857 Royal Asiatic Society test: four scholars independently translated the same text, converged — decipherments need verification protocols |
| Ugaritic (1930) | an abecedary tablet; acrophony | scribes learn via ordered sign lists — abecedaries are Rosetta stones; Bauer, Dhorme, Virolleaud cracked it independently within months |
| Linear B (Ventris 1952; Ventris & Chadwick, *JHS* 1953) | Kober's inflectional "triplets" (1940s); Ventris' grid of complementary sign distributions | structural analysis can beat bilinguals: a CV-only syllabary exposed Greek clusters (*po-to-li* = *ptolis*) |
| Maya (Knorozov 1952; Proskouriakoff 1960; Palenque round table 1973) | Landa's misread "alphabet" + Yucatec | even a wrong key, correctly doubted, can seed a syllabary; inscriptions turned out to be dynastic history, not just sky-religion (Coe 1992) |

Unsolved — and what blocks them:

| Script | Status | Blocking condition |
|---|---|---|
| Linear A | undeciphered; shares sign shapes with Linear B but readings don't transfer reliably | unknown language + small corpus |
| Etruscan | alphabet fully readable, language only partly understood | knowing the script ≠ knowing the language |
| Indus/Harappan | even "is it writing?" contested | tiny texts (average 4.6 signs; longest single-surface text 17 signs — Farmer, Sproat & Witzel 2004; the sometimes-cited 26 is cumulative across faces of one object), no bilingual, unknown language |
| Proto-Elamite, Cretan hieroglyphs, Byblos pseudo-hieroglyphs, Rongorongo (26 objects; readers exterminated in the 1860s slave raids), Voynich MS (radiocarbon-dated early 15th c.), Phaistos Disc (essentially a unique artifact) | undeciphered | Robinson's **triple curse**: unknown script + unknown language + tiny corpus |

Design lessons for fiction:

1. Plant decipherment keys where you want plot: cartouches (royal onomastics), bilingual decrees, schoolboy abecedary tablets.
2. Typology leaks into texts: a CV syllabary misrepresents clusters; an abjad hides inflectional vowels; in-world philologists can exploit exactly this.
3. Media bias writes history: clay survives fire and gets baked harder; bamboo and silk rot; stone outlives archives. What a culture writes *on* decides what its future archaeologists can ever know.
4. Dead literate classes kill scripts (Rongorongo). War, slavery, plague give you a "lost script" with real mechanics.
5. False decipherments are a standing genre (Phaistos Disc "solutions" appear every few years); model that industry too.

## 6. Matching script type to phonology (decision table)

Step 0: freeze the language first — phoneme inventory, phonotactics (max cluster, codas), morphology (root-and-pattern? agglutinative? isolating?), tone, mora timing.

| Phonology profile | Best-fit type | Why / anchor case |
|---|---|---|
| templatic roots, consonantal morphology | abjad | vowels are inflection; Semitic |
| small coda set, ~40–120 legal syllables, mora/syllable timing | syllabary | Japanese kana; Cherokee; Vai; Cypriot |
| CV-heavy, cluster-light, agglutinative affixes | abugida | Indic, Burmese, Khmer; conjuncts absorb residual clusters |
| rich clusters, big vowel system, loanword influx | full alphabet | Greek patching a Phoenician-type abjad |
| many homophones, opaque morphology, dialect diversity, bureaucratic state | logosyllabary | Chinese hanzi: stable across topolects and three millennia; cost: ~2,500–3,500 graphs to literacy |
| state wants fast adult literacy | shallow orthography | Seymour et al. 2003: English (and Danish) children slowest to foundation literacy in Europe; Finnish/Spanish/Italian fastest |
| tone language | native scripts usually ignore or under-mark tone | hanzi don't mark tone; Thai tone = f(consonant class, vowel length, diacritic) → deep orthography; Pahawh Hmong writes tone with letters (Smalley 1990) |

**Inventory arithmetic (the syllabary trap):** signs ≈ (C+1) × (V+1) × (1 + coda classes). 20C × 5V = 100 signs; add 4 codas → ~500. That's why real syllabaries are small and lossy: Linear B spelled Greek *ptolis* as *po-to-li*, and Ventris had to reconstruct underlying words through the script's distortion. If your conscript language has Georgian-style clusters, a pure syllabary dies — use conjuncts (abugida) or full letters.

**Orthographic-depth dial:** deep (spelling preserves etymology: English, French, Tibetan, Thai) vs shallow (spelling ≈ sound: Finnish, Korean, Italian). Derive depth from history: sound change *after* standardization → deep. Deep orthographies grow spelling-bee cultures; shallow ones grow reform movements.

## 7. Conscript pitfalls checklist

1. **Cipher-script trap:** a 1:1 substitution of Latin letters in fancy shapes. If swapping it for Courier changes nothing, it isn't a script — design for the language's phonology (Tolkien's Tengwar, graded in phonological series, and Cirth are the canonical exemplars; screen conscripts like Klingon pIqaD or Aurebesh are mostly substitution alphabets, fine on screen, thin for deep settings).
2. **Wrong typology for phonology** (§6) — the most common fatal error.
3. **Uniform glyph soup:** no stroke-order logic (ductus), no ascender/baseline system, no ligature potential. Real scripts run on a small stroke inventory (Chinese: 8 basic strokes in the 永 model; Hangul: 5) and compress common sequences.
4. **Inventory denial:** count your signs and price the learning; a state needs scribes, schools, exams — the scribal class is a social institution, not a prop.
5. **Homogeneity fallacy:** "the" script of the culture. Real worlds have monumental vs cursive hands, regional schools, archaizing temple styles, secret merchant registers, failed reforms, digraphia.
6. **Missing plumbing:** numerals (Brahmi→Hindu-Arabic via 9th-c. Baghdad; Roman numerals paired with the abacus; Maya vigesimal zero; Chinese rod numerals), punctuation, word division, abbreviations, colophons, correction marks.
7. **Featural overdesign:** Hangul-style glyphs-that-explain-themselves are historically near-unique; don't hand every culture one, and don't overpromise learnability (calibration: §2).
8. **Timelessness error:** scripts mutate. Give yours epochs — invention → monumental standard → cursive drift → reform — with datable forms (cuneiform drifted for 3,000 years; uncial → Caroline minuscule under the Carolingian reform c. 780–800).
9. **Media-blind design:** reed-on-clay makes wedges; chisel makes Roman capitals (serif origin debated: Catich 1968 argues brush-first; chisel-only accounts persist); palm leaf resists horizontals. Derive glyph shapes from tool physics, per §3.
10. **Ideographic myth import:** "a universal script readable by everyone" fails without a phonetic layer and grammar (DeFrancis 1989; Unger 2004).
11. **Canon leakage:** hand lore-shokunin systems, alternatives, and contested claims — not finished history. Mark anything unverified `to-verify` (lane rule).

## 8. Workbook: fitting a script to a fictional culture

1. Freeze phonology + morphology; cite the language file this script serves.
2. Choose medium and tool from economy and environment (clay/stone/papyrus/palm/bark/bamboo/paper; stylus/chisel/brush/pen) → derive stroke physics and glyph silhouettes.
3. Pick typology from the §6 table; compute the inventory; state the literacy price (years to scribe-level vs merchant-level vs farmer-level).
4. Derive history: invention mode (independent? borrowed with patches? divine-gift myth → couples myth-symbol), 3+ mutation epochs, monumental/cursive split, reform events, digraphia if the polity is multilingual.
5. Decide the archive: which medium survives collapse? That choice is what future in-world scholars can ever recover (couples era).
6. Plant or withhold decipherment keys (cartouches, bilinguals, abecedaries) depending on whether you want the script solved in-story — and remember the triple curse if you want it honest.
7. Tag scripts with ISO 15924-style codes for machine-readable cross-reference; hand systems and open questions to the world bible via lore-shokunin.
