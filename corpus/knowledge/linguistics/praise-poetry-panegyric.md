## 1. Scope: the praise surface this entry owns, and the neighbours it defers

## 2. Praise poetry as a public speech act: what the words build

- In its specialised form, panegyric is the type of court poetry, and it seems to go with a particular ethos — a stress on royal or aristocratic power and an admiration for military achievement; its most developed forms are the formalised praises directed publicly to kings, chiefs and leaders by members of a ruler's official entourage (S1007).
- Praise names are nearly universal, and praises also occur among non-centralised peoples who stress personal achievement in war or hunting, so the court form is the specialised case of a much wider practice (S1007).
- The subjects of praise are not only persons: clans, animals, divinities and inanimate things — a divining set, a bicycle, a train — are praised in the same high style, which is the clearest evidence that the genre is a way of speaking rather than a fact about its object (S1007).
- What the performance builds is stated as three things at once: status is validated by the content of the praise, by the number and quality of the performers, and by the public nature of the recitation (S1007).
- The validation is acknowledged materially: the ruler answers the praise with gifts or money, and the gifts are often given publicly, so the reward is itself part of the speech act's uptake (S1007).
- Praise is also a medium of public opinion: up to a point praisers can withhold praise or include implicit or explicit derogatory allusions as a negative sanction on a ruler's acts (S1007).
- A praise poem is a performance of something understood to have pre-existed the moment of utterance — people speak of "the praises of Dingaan" rather than of one singer's performance on one afternoon (S1009).
- That capacity to be detached from the occasion and re-embodied in a later performance is what makes a praise tradition a tradition rather than a series of unrelated compliments (S1009).
- Praise is interested speech: the Tamil public genre field treats praise, appeal, admonition and lament as modes of address with an interested speaker behind each, not as neutral description (see `civilizations/india-tamil-sangam.md:57`).

The speech event itself is best read with the ethnography-of-communication grid already in the corpus (`linguistics/linguistic-anthropology.md:9-28`), which asks for setting, participants, ends, act sequence, key, instrument, norms and genre; this entry applies that grid and does not re-teach it. What the grid makes visible here is that the same words can be a praise, a petition or a taunt depending on the participants and the key, and that a praise scene is therefore specified by its occasion before it is specified by its text (inference).

Four objects can be built by the same act, and a tradition usually privileges one of them (design, not sourced):

| object built | what the words assert | what the audience is asked to accept |
|---|---|---|
| a person | deeds, qualities, a promised future | that this person is worth naming aloud |
| a lineage | descent, order of forebears, inherited claim | that the line is continuous and known |
| an office | the dignity of the seat apart from its holder | that the seat is legitimate whoever sits in it |
| a place | the land's wealth, history and defenders | that the place is a worthy subject of praise |

The distinction between the office and its incumbent is explicit in the recorded material: public praise stresses both the dignity of the office and, more explicitly, the achievements of its present holder (S1007). A tradition that can praise the seat while criticising the sitter has a resource that a tradition which confuses the two does not (inference).

## 3. The praise singer: office, recruitment, licence, patronage and reward

- Every Zulu king had one or more specialists who both recited the praises of previous rulers and composed new ones to commemorate the achievements and qualities of the present king (S1007).
- The praise poets — Zulu *imbongi*, Tswana *mmoki* — were respected members of the chief's official retinue, with the function of recording the praise names, victories, characteristics and exploits, or expected exploits, of the chief to whom they were attached (S1007).
- The office was graded: among the Hausa a District Head's *maroka* team normally contained several drummers, eulogists, two or more pipers and sometimes a horn-blower, while lesser chiefs kept bards modelled on the king's but less skilled and less specialised (S1007).
- The grading is audible: the amount or type of accompaniment is laid down for the praises of each grade of ruler, wooden gongs may not be used to praise anyone below a certain level, and some instruments may praise only kings and leading vassals (S1007).
- The singer may also be a freelance: expert Mandingo singers wander through the streets or attend festivals on their own initiative, pick an outstanding or reputedly wealthy individual, and are rewarded with gifts and thanks by people who call them beggars and are glad to send them away content (S1007).
- The reward is not only mercenary: gifts are showered on the praise poets from enthusiasm as well as fear (S1007).
- The Xhosa *imbongi* lived in close proximity to the chief's Great Place and accompanied the chief on important occasions, directing the performance at the chief — decrying what was unworthy, praising what was worthy, and forecasting what was going to happen (S1011).
- The office is not a relic: the oral poet still forms an important part of contemporary Xhosa oral traditions, and the role is linked to changing social norms rather than fixed by them (S1011).
- Xhosa oral poets produce spontaneous poems in praise of dignitaries, whether local chiefs or visiting professors, and improvisation is the tendency that distinguishes Xhosa practice from the more memorial Zulu mode (S1008).
- The Greek epinician shows the same office in a commissioned form: Pindar wrote five of the longest of his extant odes for the Sicilian princes Hieron of Syracuse and Theron of Akragas, and worked as a song-writer for Hellenic states without discrimination (S1012).
- The poet's independence is part of the office's value: asked why he did not accept a prince's invitation to live at court, Pindar answered that he had chosen to live his own life and not to be the property of another (S1012).
- The Welsh bard states the office as a choice of praise over reproach: it will not be his part to speak reproachfully of the fallen lord, and a more choice act is to celebrate his praise in song (S1013).
- The Tamil anthologies describe bards, singers and poets moving from place to place, staying in a patron's house for many days, praising him and singing until he honours them with gifts, and serving also as counsellors and ambassadors (S1014).
- Among the Mande griot's functions are genealogist, historian, advisor, spokesperson, mediator, interpreter, musician, composer, teacher, exhorter, witness and praise-singer, and the praise-singing function is called the most obvious and audible manifestation of the profession (S1015).
- Among the skalds the relation was openly economic: there was money in poetry, court poets received generous rewards and renown in exchange for panegyrics, and skalds competed for a patron's favour by crafting the most elaborate praise (S1016).
- The Mande jeli is the same kind of office seen from the inside: a named verbal and musical specialist whose work includes genealogy, praise, mediation and counsel, with authority that involves training, social relationships and patronage (`civilizations/mali-songhai.md:53`).
- The keener's office is the nearest parallel in the corpus, and it is a different office: it is death-facing and duty-bound where the praise singer is patron-facing and competitive (`linguistics/funerary-lament-dirge-formulae.md:56-84`).

The tradition-side questions a bardic institution must answer are already listed in the corpus as a five-item checklist — repertoire ownership, payment, permissible mockery, commemorative duty, audience competence (`civilizations/parthia.md:183`). This entry does not repeat that list; it asks the four questions that sit on the **singer's** side of the same relation (design, not sourced):

1. **Recruitment** — hereditary lineage, apprenticeship, or free entry; who may hold the office and who may not.
2. **Licence** — who authorises the singer to speak for a bearer, and whether the licence is revocable.
3. **Reward** — what is given (gift, cattle, land, cloth, money, exemption, hospitality), when, and whether it is given publicly.
4. **Obligation** — what the singer owes in return: attendance, memory, discretion, or the duty to speak unwelcome things.

A singer with a licence and no reward is a volunteer; a singer with a reward and no licence is a flatterer; a singer with neither is a bystander (inference).

## 4. The praise epithet: a name that carries a history

- Praise names are most often given to people but may also describe clans, animals or inanimate objects, and they are usually explicitly laudatory; the Zulu king Shaka is praised in one name as "The Ever-ready-to-meet-any-challenge" (S1007).
- Not every praise name is laudatory: Hausa *kirari* may be derogatory or concerned with insight into inherent qualities, and the stock name of a guitar can mock the company it keeps (S1007).
- Among the Yoruba the *oriki* are permanent titles held by individuals, given to them by friends or, most often, by the drummers, and a collection of them recited together resembles a loosely constructed poem about the person praised (S1007).
- Individual laudatory epithets refer to character or deeds and are usually bestowed on kings, leaders and outstanding warriors; Shaka is said to have had several dozen (S1007).
- An epithet can expand until it takes a whole line, and a praise poem is largely built up of a series of praise names and praise verses (S1007).
- Praise poets renew well-known formulaic expressions by linguistic adaptation and coin new metaphors, which is where the tradition's creativity actually lives (S1010).
- The genre has remained resilient in its potential for renewal and relevance, which is why the epithet stock is a living inventory rather than a closed list (S1010).
- Obscurity is a resource rather than a defect: a laconic, incomplete and allusive expression sits at the centre of the mode, and it could be deployed for political reasons — to encode discreet criticisms of royal power, or by a ruling group to display a meaning while withholding it (S1009).

Two boundaries govern this section. The epithet's behaviour as a metrical unit — its slot, its economy, its aptness-independence — belongs to `linguistics/orality-formulaic-language.md:56-62`, and is cited here rather than restated. The divine epithet's grammar, where a god's name takes a jurisdiction-bearing modifier, belongs to `synthesis/deity-name-derivation-pipeline.md:69-88`; the epithets this entry owns are human, and a divine epiclesis that appears in a praise scene should be routed there (design, not sourced).

The epithet's grammar is a bearer plus a claim plus a warrant (design, not sourced). The claim is what the phrase asserts — descent, deed, place, virtue, or a curse obliquely worded. The warrant is the occasion or evidence that made the claim utterable: a battle, a hunt, a birth, a gift, a judgement. An epithet with a claim and no warrant is a compliment; an epithet with a warrant and no claim is a nickname (inference).

The bearer units a tradition may praise are already enumerated in the corpus for carried marks — person, crest, clan, office, affinity, community, property and hereditary right (`myth-symbol/heraldry-emblems-identity-marks.md:35-48`). The list transfers to spoken praise as a checklist of what an epithet can attach to; what does not transfer is transmission, because a mark is inherited and an epithet must be re-uttered to exist (inference).

Three tests keep an epithet tied to its bearer (design, not sourced):

- **The substitution test.** Move the epithet to another bearer in the same tradition. If it fits equally well, it is a genre adjective, not an epithet.
- **The history test.** Ask what happened that made this phrase sayable. If nothing happened, the phrase is decoration.
- **The contest test.** Ask who would dispute the epithet and on what evidence. An epithet nobody would dispute is either sacred or empty.

## 5. Genealogical recitation: the lineage as an argument

- Formal praises were delivered on ceremonial and public occasions, when the bard recited a whole series of eulogies, starting with the famous praises of the king's or chief's ancestors — praises handed down by word of mouth — and finally reaching the praises, often composed by himself, of the present chief (S1007).
- Among some of the Yoruba the praises of the king, with the complete list of his predecessors and their praises, must be recited once a year in public (S1007).
- The recitation of the praises of the chief and his ancestors pointed out to the listeners the chief's right to the position he held, both through his descent from those predecessors whose great deeds were commemorated and through his own qualities (S1007).
- Genealogy is usually alluded to rather than catalogued: there is little straightforward cataloguing of genealogies, because knowledge of them is assumed in reciter and listener and merely touched on (S1007).
- What is commemorated is the great deeds and characters of earlier heroes rather than their mere names or ancestry, so a recitation is an argument about inherited merit, not a list of begats (S1007).
- A praise tradition can carry a lineage's standing across generations: the poems about earlier chiefs were handed down and probably known by the chief's followers as well as by the specialist reciters (S1007).
- The royal register already treats praise-recitation as performed pedigree, publicly auditable, where an omitted title is a political statement (`myth-symbol/sacred-kingship-mythic-charter.md:150`); this entry supplies the genre mechanics that make the audit possible and owns no charter.

The order of a recitation is the argument (design, not sourced). Reciting forebears first and the present holder last makes the present a conclusion drawn from the past; reciting the present holder first and the ancestors after makes the past a decoration of the present. Neither order is wrong, but they say different things, and a tradition should fix one and let a rival break it (inference).

Selection is part of the claim, not a defect in it: a recitation may select an appropriate line rather than exhaust the whole genealogy, and a shorter performance is not automatically a corrupted longer one (`civilizations/polynesia-navigation.md:146`). The critique that genealogies serve the present rather than record it (`linguistics/kinship-terminology.md:153`) applies here in its strongest form: a praise genealogy is a charter being performed, and the audience is invited to audit it (inference).

Rival versions are the normal condition, not an accident: where competing praise names and genealogical recitations exist, the contest between authorized versions is itself part of the political record (`civilizations/inca.md:151`).

Four questions fix a genealogical recitation (design, not sourced):

1. **Depth** — how far back the line runs, and what happens at the point where memory stops.
2. **Direction** — ascending from the founder to the present, or descending from the present to the founder.
3. **Selection** — which forebears are named and which are passed over, and whether an omission is an insult.
4. **Verification** — who may challenge a link, and what evidence a challenge requires.

## 6. The order of a praise performance: stations, delivery and audience

- Praise poems are delivered much faster, and in a higher tone, than ordinary prose utterances; the reciter pours forth the praises with few pauses for breath and at the top of his voice (S1007).
- The delivery is semi-chanted: a special stylised intonation is expected, with formalised cadences and glides at the ends of lines and stanzas acting as concluding formulas (S1007).
- The audience plays its part and often shouts out encouragingly in support of what the praiser is saying or to cheer him on (S1007).
- One traditional occasion for reciting a chief's praises was early morning, when the praiser shouted them out (S1007).
- Praises were also declaimed after a victory, on the arrival of distinguished visitors, at the installation of a new chief, and at the distribution of royal bounty (S1007).
- The stanza order is variable because stanzas are linked by their general application to the hero rather than by a chronological narrative, so the poem conveys a picture rather than a plot (S1007).
- The occasion can be a rite of passage: when an individual or group moves from one status to another, the transition is celebrated by praises marking the new status or commemorating the old (S1007).
- The performance is also a test of the performer: the power of a praise poem seems to depend partly on the delivery and personality of the reciter, though in the complex forms the literary effect rests mainly on the composer's art (S1007).
- In a contemporary Xhosa performance before a conference audience the *imbongi* greets the assembled listeners, praises, turns to exhortation, and closes on a further praise — the sequence reads as a set piece, not as a single outburst (S1008).
- The singer's duty can be as precise as a greeting: it is the *imbongi*'s duty to greet a lord with a royal praise name, and a traditional audience is expected to repeat the praise name after him (S1008).
- Praise in performance is not a fixed text: the singer may introduce a stanza or line from the stock language and imagery in that particular performance, so each recitation involves a certain amount of composition (S1007).

The general rules of an ordered speech sequence are already stated for the toast — every turn has a caller, the reply quotes the theme, and the order must be checkable (`linguistics/toast-drinking-speech-formulae.md:122-171`). They apply here, but the praise sequence is a different occasion and its stations are its own (design, not sourced):

| station | what happens | what the audience learns |
|---|---|---|
| summons | the singer takes the floor, by office or by intrusion | who has the right to speak |
| naming | the bearer is named, and the name is placed | who is being built |
| genealogy | the line is recited, in the fixed order | why this bearer and not another |
| deed chain | the epithets and verses are chained | what the bearer has done |
| uptake | the audience answers, and the patron rewards | whether the praise was accepted |

The caller is the singer's office, not a master of ceremonies: the *imbongi* takes the floor because the office entitles him to, and the audience's shout is the reply that quotes the theme (inference). The order is checkable when the genealogy's order and the rank of the persons named can be reconstructed by a listener who knows the line (inference).

The uptake is the station most often missing from a draft. A praise that no one answers is a monologue; the recorded audience shouts encouragement, and the recorded patron answers with gifts (S1007). The audience's answer is also the moment at which a praise can be contested, which is why the next section treats rivalry and satire as part of the performance rather than as an interruption of it (inference).

## 8. Panegyric, rivalry and the limits of flattery

- Praise poems frequently include derogatory remarks, veiled or otherwise, or give advice as well as praise, and the frank assessment of a ruler's character accompanies the lauding of his power (S1007).
- Sarcastic or even insulting remarks are sometimes included and, among the Ngoni at least, are said to represent a high kind of praise, because such comments are so ludicrous that they could not possibly be true (S1007).
- Praise poetry could exert control on a ruler as well as uphold his position, which is the institutional form of the limit (S1007).
- The *imbongi*'s role allowed for criticism, and the voice of protest is implicit in the role of the bard in traditional and contemporary society (S1011).
- The *imbongi*'s right to speak freely, with impunity, has been seriously threatened, and the voice of protest has often been censored to serve the ends of petty politicians, thereby undermining the credibility of the singer (S1011).
- In a recorded Xhosa performance, praise yields to criticism of the audience's ancestors as imperialists, and the singer then turns to exhortation before closing on praise (S1008).
- The genre's own critics ask whether the ancient panegyric should, or can, be recovered as anything but a curiosity (S1010).
- The genre's reach is wide: praise poems occur from the private bedroom to the public political meeting, and on subjects from toddlers to recently deceased elders (S1010).
- A praise poem may be the composer's property: among the Tswana, until the composer's death no one may recite his poem in public, and the names of the original poets may be remembered (S1007).
- The poet's standing against the patron is a limit in itself: Pindar's refusal to become the property of a prince is the same limit stated as a biography rather than as a rule (S1012).
- A law-text states the limit as a rule: a poet should not satirize or over-praise a subject, because the *fili* depended on the wealth of his patron for his own economic standing, and it was in his interest to grow the patron's honour and take the *dúas* in return (S1017).
- Obscurity can carry the criticism the surface cannot: an allusive phrase can encode a discreet criticism of royal power, or be used by a ruling group to display a meaning while guarding it (S1009).
- Pindar names the audience-side limit: praise is overtaken by distaste, and that distaste comes from covetous men who try to darken the good man's deeds (S1012).

Three boundaries keep this section from becoming a second contest entry. The boast, the taunt, the riposte and the adjudicating audience belong to `linguistics/verbal-duelling-ritual-insult.md:110-146`, and a praise scene that turns into a flyting should be routed there. Satire with binding or legal force — a lampoon that is a curse — belongs to `linguistics/oath-blessing-curse-formulae.md:20-38`. The panegyrist-and-satirist role split is a recorded institutional fact, not a claim of this entry: Diodorus's three roles of bard, druid and diviner are set out with their evidence grading at `civilizations/celts.md:50`.

Five limits keep praise from becoming flattery (design, not sourced):

1. **The withheld praise.** The singer can decline to praise, and the refusal must be visible to the audience.
2. **The oblique correction.** The praise can carry advice or a veiled derogation inside the laudatory frame.
3. **The licensed insult.** A tradition may license one occasion or one register in which the singer may mock the bearer.
4. **The rival.** A second singer, a rival house or a hostile audience can answer the praise with a counter-praise.
5. **The patron's refusal.** The patron can refuse the reward, which turns the praise into a failed claim rather than a success.

A praise tradition with none of these five is not a praise institution but a propaganda apparatus, and its praises will read as commands (inference).

## 9. Decision procedure: a fictional praise tradition whose epithets stay tied to their bearers, singers, patrons and occasions

What follows is a sequence of decisions, not a template (design, not sourced).

**Before a line is written:**

1. Fix the **occasion**: installation, victory, wedding, hunt, annual recitation, arrival of a visitor, or a regular morning performance.
2. Fix the **object built**: a person, a lineage, an office, a place — and say which one the tradition privileges.
3. Fix the **singer's office**: who may sing, how they are recruited, who licenses them, and whether the licence can be revoked.
4. Fix the **patron and the reward**: who pays, in what, when, and whether the payment is public.
5. Fix the **epithet stock**: which epithets attach to which bearers, what each asserts, and what warrant a new epithet needs.
6. Fix the **genealogy**: whose line is recited, in what order, how deep, who may hear it, and who may challenge a link.
7. Fix the **order of performance**: the five stations of §6, and which of them may be cut.
8. Fix the **audience and the uptake**: who is present, who may answer, and what answer counts as acceptance.
9. Fix the **limits**: what may be withheld, what may be implied, who may insult, and what a rival may say.
10. Fix the **afterlife of the praise**: whether it is remembered, re-performed, written down, or owned by its composer.

**While drafting:**

11. Give every epithet a **bearer with a history**; a phrase with no warrant is a new coinage and should be marked as one in the fiction.
12. Keep the **genealogy ordered**; a recitation with no fixed order is a list, not a claim.
13. Put the **reward on the page**; if the reader cannot see what the singer got, the office has no economy.
14. Show the **audience's answer**; the uptake is the difference between a praise and a soliloquy.
15. Give the **rival a standing**; an opponent with no office and no patron cannot contest anything.
16. Bound the **flattery with a checkable claim**; a praise that asserts nothing falsifiable cannot be tested, and therefore cannot fail.
17. Tie **place-praise to a boundary**; a place praised without an edge is a landscape description.

**Before the tradition is called finished:**

18. Run every scene past the error patterns of §10.
19. Confirm that the entry has not restated a neighbour's material: the formula slots go to the orality entry, the contest to the duelling entry, the dead to the lament entry, the name system to the naming entry, the charter to the kingship entry, the divine epithet to the deity-name pipeline.
20. Confirm that no canon has been settled: the people, the tradition, the epithets and the rewards are the lore layer's.
21. Put the non-canon status and the handoff on the record.

**A worked example (NON-CANON).** A river-port town keeps a **harbour praise** performed when a new lighter-captain takes his oar. The singer is a **tide-caller**, an office held by two families who alternate years and are paid in a share of each cargo's toll, publicly handed over on the quay. The praise opens with the harbour itself — the bar, the two channels, the year the west wall went out — then recites the captains of the line in the order they first took an oar, then chains the new captain's epithets, each of which must name a voyage that the assembled lightermen can confirm. The limit is the **ebb-turn**: for the length of one tide the singer may name the captain's failures, and the captain may not answer. A captain who is not praised at all loses his turn in the queue, which is the reward's negative face. Every epithet in the scene is checkable against a voyage, and the genealogy is checkable against the harbour roll, so the reader can audit the praise rather than take it on trust.

## 10. Error patterns: praise traditions that fail as speech acts

Each failure below is a missing or broken slot, and each has a test (design, not sourced).

**1. The epithet with no bearer history.** A striking phrase is attached to a character and nothing in the fiction explains why it became sayable. The test: ask what happened that made this phrase utterable, and who first said it. If the answer is *the author*, the phrase is decoration.

**2. The singer with no patron or reward.** The singer performs, the praise lands, and no one pays. The test: ask what the singer lives on and who provides it. If the answer is *nothing*, the office has no economy and the praise has no cost.

**3. The praise with no audience.** The words are spoken to the bearer alone, or to no one in particular. The test: ask who hears it and what they do. If the answer is *nobody*, the speech act has no uptake and cannot succeed or fail.

**4. The flattery with no limit or satirical counterweight.** Every praise is accepted, nothing may be withheld, and no one may answer. The test: ask what the singer may say against the bearer. If the answer is *nothing*, the tradition is a propaganda apparatus.

**5. The genealogy with no order.** The ancestors are named in whatever order the plot needs. The test: ask why the third forebear precedes the fourth. If the answer is *no reason*, the recitation is a list and asserts nothing.

**6. The office with no succession.** The singer is unique, irreplaceable and dies with the story. The test: ask who sings next. If the answer is *no one*, the institution cannot outlive its scene.

**7. The praise that could be given to anyone.** The epithets are generic virtues and the genealogy is a set of names. The test: swap the bearer and reread. If nothing breaks, nothing was built.

**8. The reward that arrives from nowhere.** A gift appears at the end of the scene with no prior obligation. The test: ask what the patron owed and when the debt was contracted. A surprise gift is a plot device, not an economy.

**9. The place-praise with no boundary.** The land is praised as vast, rich and beautiful, and nothing says where it ends or who contests the edge. The test: ask where the praised place stops. If the answer is *everywhere*, the praise is scenery.

**10. The rival with no standing.** A second singer appears, is ignored, and the praise proceeds. The test: ask what the rival's office, patron or audience is. If the answer is *none*, the rivalry is decoration.

**11. The performance with no stations.** The praise begins in the middle, skips the naming, and ends without an answer. The test: ask which station is missing and what the audience lost by its absence.

**12. The tradition that floats free of its people.** The praise could be delivered in any world by any singer to any patron. The test: ask what this tradition says about *these* people that no other tradition would say.

## 11. Checklist

Run these checks on every praise scene before it is used (design, not sourced):

- [ ] Is the **occasion** named, and does it explain why these words are spoken now?
- [ ] Is the **object built** one of person, lineage, office or place, and is it the one the scene needs?
- [ ] Is the **singer's office** defined — recruitment, licence, revocability?
- [ ] Is the **patron and the reward** visible, and is the payment public?
- [ ] Does every **epithet** have a warrant, and would it fail the substitution test?
- [ ] Is the **genealogy** ordered, and does the order argue something?
- [ ] Are the **five stations** of the performance present or deliberately cut?
- [ ] Does the **audience answer**, and is the answer legible?
- [ ] Is there at least one **limit** — withheld praise, oblique correction, licensed insult, rival, or refused reward?
- [ ] Is the **flattery checkable** against a claim the audience could dispute?
- [ ] Is the **place** bounded if a place is praised?
- [ ] Are the **neighbour boundaries** respected: no formula-slot re-derivation, no contest grammar, no lament, no naming system, no charter myth, no divine epithet grammar?
- [ ] Is the tradition **tied to its own people**, or could it be moved to any world unchanged?

## 12. What the opened sources can carry, and the handoff

**How far each source reaches.** A source is used here only as far as its captured passage goes, and each of them reports praise inside one setting. None surveys the genre across the world, so this entry claims no universal grammar of panegyric (design, not sourced).

**Calibration.** A report about one tradition remains local to that tradition; it becomes a design option only when a tradition is being invented. The five stations (§6), the five limits (§8), the twelve failure modes (§10) and the checklist (§11) are this entry's own apparatus, and no claim is made that any real culture shares them (design, not sourced). Where a source records a particular practice, the record is not generalised into a type.

**Where description stops.** A praise is a genre name, not a transcript of anyone's words on any afternoon. Recorded formulae stay records; described practices stay descriptions (design, not sourced).

**Canon and rights.** Nothing here settles what is true in a world, and nothing here decides whether a living community's praise tradition may be borrowed; the first is lore-shokunin's question, the second a rights question (design, not sourced).

**Handoff.** lore-shokunin receives working material, not a canon: the four objects (§2), the office's four questions (§3), the epithet's grammar and three tests (§4), the genealogy's four questions (§5), the five stations (§6), the tradition table (§7), the five limits (§8), the build procedure of §9, the failure list of §10 and the checklist of §11. The world bible decides the people, the tradition, the epithets, the singers, the patrons and the occasions, and rules on the legal and sacred status of any praise in the fiction (design, not sourced).

## 13. Inference ledger

The readings below are this entry's own; no opened source states them in this form. Each carries *inference* or *design, not sourced* where it appears in the body, and a reader who rejects all of them still keeps the claims that carry a source id (design, not sourced).

- That the four objects of praise — person, lineage, office, place — are the same act with different targets, and that a tradition's character is set by which target it privileges (inference).
- That the office's four questions (recruitment, licence, reward, obligation) are separable, and that a singer missing one of them is a different social type (inference).
- That the epithet's warrant is the load-bearing element, and that an epithet without a warrant is a compliment rather than a claim (inference).
- That the order of a genealogical recitation is itself an argument, and that reversing it changes what the praise asserts (inference).
- That the audience's answer is the uptake without which the speech act cannot succeed or fail (inference).
- That the five limits are what distinguish a praise institution from a propaganda apparatus (inference).
- That a praise which asserts nothing falsifiable cannot be tested, and therefore cannot be trusted by a reader (inference).
- That the reward's visibility is what makes praise an economy rather than a compliment (inference).
- That the bearer-unit list transfers from carried marks to spoken epithets as a checklist of attachment, while transmission does not transfer (inference).
