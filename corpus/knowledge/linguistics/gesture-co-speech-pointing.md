## 1. The register property: gesture is part of the utterance

**Master claim.** Co-speech gesture is produced with speech as one system, not decoration: gesture is "visible bodily action that is seen as intimately involved in the activity of speaking" (S191), gestures and speech come out of "a single, unitary, project of action" (S192), speech and gesture are "part and parcel of the same system" (S193), and the two channels together make composite utterances whose gesture half adds semantic, syntactic, discursive and pragmatic information (S194); McNeill's answer is to "examine language and gesture together" (S195), so for a designer the hands belong on the same spec sheet as the words.

| Test | Pass when | Fails as (owner) |
|---|---|---|
| **Produced with the utterance** | the hand, body and face move in visible actions "intentionally used to communicate" and "expressed together with the verbal utterance" (S194) | a sign language: a full language with its own grammar and community, not speech's accessory (`sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64`, `:161`) |
| **Carries meaning, not decoration** | the gesture adds what the words do not say, or settles which reading the words get (S194) | body-language wallpaper: a gesture "not perceived as mere incidental accompaniments to the speech channel" (S194) |
| **Conventionalization is a gradient** | forms turn conventional where pointing, interaction regulation and pragmatic work are done, and a fully conventionalized gesture can stand in for a word (S192) | an emblem with a dictionary gloss and a fixed etiquette (`adornment-body-codes.md:79`) |
| **Not posture** | the movement is part of what is being said and is timed to the utterance (S194, S192) | a posture or bow gradient keyed to rank and seniority (`adornment-body-codes.md:80`) |
| **Not a counting system** | the hand shapes what is being said, as a Neapolitan speaker holds up four fingers while saying "per quattro persone" (S192) | finger counting as a patterned counting technology, with its own digits and direction (`numerals-and-measures.md:107`) |

Designer's map (**H** — a heuristic, not a law): one conventionalization line, from co-speech gesture at the speech end, through emblems whose gloss a word can replace (the Neapolitan "piccole" gesture is one) (S192), to sign languages at the far end (S194; `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64`); decide per form how conventional it is, whether a word replaces it, and who may use it, because the typical failure is a world whose gesture is either body-language wallpaper or a second full language, with nothing in between.

## 3. Gesture types and what each carries

The six rows are working categories a designer picks from, and they are loadings, not boxes: one gesture can be iconic and deictic at once (S196, §5; S197, §2). Co-speech gesture as such is not conventionalized and systematic the way a sign language is (`sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64`), so each row says how far a community fixes it.

| Type | What it carries | How conventional it is | Example | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Iconic | A depiction of the referent's concrete shape or action: iconic gestures ‘bear a close formal relationship to the semantic content of the speech’ (S197, §2) | None: improvised per utterance (S197, §2) | ‘An oscillating downward motion starting high to convey the wavy nature of a road descending a mountain’ (S198, Table 3) | Decide which concrete referents your speakers can draw by hand and path; the typical failure is a culture whose nouns are all abstractions yet everyone pantomimes |
| Metaphoric | An abstract idea through a physical source domain — size, container, path: the gesture ‘presents an abstract idea rather than a concrete object or event’ (S197, §2) | Low: the mapping is shared, the drawing improvised (S197, §2) | ‘The hands with palms facing one another held outward at the width of the body’ to symbolize the size of an idea (S198, Table 3); a cupped palm as if holding a discursive object (S196, §1) | Fix one source domain per family of abstractions and keep it stable; the typical failure is two communities mapping the same abstract to opposite images |
| Deictic (pointing) | Reference to a point in space or time: McNeill limits the deictic to pointing, and ‘deixis in gesture is commonly thought of just in terms of pointing’, ‘especially with the index finger or open hand in many cultures’ (S197, §2) | Medium: the pointing anatomy is conventional, the referent is inferred (S197, §2) | ‘A pointed forefinger marking a position to the left of the interlocutor to establish the reference point for a person or place under discussion’ (S198, Table 3) | Choose the pointing anatomy per community and the frame it points in; the forms and their communities are in §4, the absolute-frame constraint with `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:36` |
| Beat | Rhythm and prosody, not content: the hand ‘moves along with the rhythmical pulsation of speech’, and a beat can overlay the other types (S197, §2) | Lowest: any speech can be beaten (S198, §1) | ‘A clasped fist rising and falling on every word or syllable’ (S198, Table 3) | Set your language's stress, then let the beat mark it; the typical failure is a beat on every syllable, which spends the emphasis |
| Emblem | A fixed dictionary meaning in a conventional form: Efron's emblems are ‘conventionalized body movements, which have fixed meanings in the culture they are used in’ (S197, §2) | Highest: it can stand alone as a whole utterance (S196, §1; S199) | ‘Emblems such as the “OK” sign have independent status as symbolic forms’ (S196, §1) — a gloss local to the sampled speakers; see §12 | Hand the emblem lexicon and its geography to `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:79`; finger counting is body code too, at `numerals-and-measures.md:107`; the typical failure is one emblem with two glosses in a single scene |
| Interactive or recurrent (palm-up open hand) | The interaction, not the topic: Bavelas et al.'s four roles, as reported by Williamson et al., are ‘citing the other's previous contribution; seeking agreement, understanding, or help; the delivery of new versus shared information; and events around the speaking turn’ (S198, §1); the palm-up epistemic groups absence of knowledge, ability or concern, uncertainty, interrogatives, hypotheticality, obviousness and exclamatives (S199, "Discussions of palm-ups in gesture") | Medium-high: the form is widespread, the glosses local; for Müller ‘this family is rooted in practical actions of giving and receiving objects’ (S199) | an outstretched palm-up with co-occurring speech, ‘appearing to give reassurance to the confederate’ (S198, Table 3); in Syuba (Tibeto-Burman, Nepal) ‘the emblematic form of the gesture is produced without speech to ask, “What are you doing?”’ (S199) | Decide whether your culture has a quotable palm-up and which glosses it licenses; the typical failure is one gloss in every scene, since ‘form is not a foolproof guide to meaning’ (S199) |

Two rules follow. First, decide per community which gestures are dictionary items and which are improvised — that split is the conventionality column, and it is what a scene can rely on (S196, §1; S197, §2).
Second, write the type as a load, not a label: ‘most gestures are multifaceted—iconicity is combined with deixis, deixis with metaphoricity, and so forth’ (S196, §5), and the palm-up family already splits into presentational and epistemic variants (S199, Abstract).

## 4. Pointing

| Pointing form | Community where it is the default | What it signals | Design consequence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Index finger | US undergraduates: 450 of 496 points (91%), and 15 of 16 preferred manual pointing (S200, p. 8, Table 2) | singles out one referent: “a speaker singles out an object which is to be attended to as a particular individual object” (Kendon 2004, 205, as reported in S201, p. 146) | a default your culture borrows, not a law: where index pointing is claimed to be absent, “other forms of pointing (e.g., lip-pointing, open hand pointing, middle finger pointing) may be dominant” (S202, p. 89) |
| Whole hand (flat hand, fingers together) | Northamptonshire (England) and Campania (Italy), in Kendon’s index-hand and flat-hand data (S201, p. 145); Arrernte (central Australia) keep a “wide hand point” among six handshapes (S201, p. 146) | a referent linked to the topic rather than the topic itself: “the object being indicated is not itself the primary focus or topic of the discourse but is something that is linked to the topic” (Kendon 2004, 208, as reported in S201, p. 146) | give the flat hand a job the index finger does not do; a variant with no contrast of its own is a redundancy (**H**) |
| Lip pointing | Lao speakers in Laos (Lao is also spoken by some 20 million people in Thailand); among the Kuna of San Blas, Panama, ‘hand and finger pointing occurs infrequently’ and lip-pointing is ‘more common by far’ (Sherzer, 1983, p. 169, as reported in S203, p. 207); also recorded among Jahai (Aslian, Mon-Khmer) speakers in peninsular Malaysia and among Colombian Spanish speakers (S203, pp. 186, 188, 191) | it acts as a gaze-switch, “a handy way of ‘switching on’ the deictic function of gaze” (S203, p. 203), and “only occurs when the identity or location of the referent is the focus of the speaker’s utterance” (S203, p. 195) | fix whether it is casual or formal: in Laos lip-pointing is ‘low’ register or ‘sub-standard’ (S203, p. 205), while among the Navajo it is the preferred option to index-finger pointing, which is considered impolite (the source prints “inpolite”) (S203, p. 206) |
| Chin or head pointing | Yupno speakers in the village of Gua, upper Yupno valley, Papua New Guinea: 104 of 598 points (17%) were head-pointing, against 11 of 496 (2%) for US participants (S200, pp. 4, 8) | the same referent job made with the head, chin and eyes — a lip-point raises the head and chin, “the latter visibly jutting out”, and orients gaze to the referent (S203, p. 192) | name the combination, not the label: the labels lip point, head point and eye point are vague and “can be performed in different ways (and are usually combined)” (S202, p. 91) |
| Nose pointing | Yupno and Enga (Papua New Guinea) (S200, p. 4, Table 1) | a facial point: 252 of 598 Yupno points (42%) were nose points, against 0 of 496 for US participants (S200, p. 8, Table 2) | do not read a form’s presence in a repertoire as its being that community’s default: the Yupno pointed non-manually more often than manually, but the difference was not significant (S200, p. 8) |
| Eye gaze | Arrernte (central Australia) (S204, publisher abstract): the speaker shifts the eye “noticeably within the socket toward a particular referent”, with typically no accompanying head movement (Wilkins 2003, 187, as reported in S201, p. 147) | exclusion: eye pointing “is used to exclude third parties from being privy to what or who is being talked about” (S201, p. 147) | decide whether gaze alone is addressable or only a support for a head or lip point; in the Lao data the addressee’s gaze follows the speaker’s (S203, p. 194) |

One restriction cuts across every form: in Ghana pointing by the left hand is considered a taboo, and there is “a politeness convention to place the left hand on the lower back, as if to hide it from the interlocutor” (S205).
The taboo is not absolute: “right-handed pointing may involve an anatomically staining position when indicating a leftward direction across the body”, pointing with both hands together “does not violate the taboo”, left-handed pointing still occurs with the verbalization of LEFT, and some left-handed gestures are so reduced in form that Ghanaians do not count them as pointing for the taboo’s purposes (S205). Design consequence (**H**): a handedness rule constrains the pointing system rather than forbidding it — decide the banned form, the substitute it forces and the escape hatch.

Decide the community and its taboos first, then the canonical form, then the variants, then the focus rules: a pointing form means what it means against the other forms available in that community, and “It is possible to think of there being a kind of ‘grammar of pointing’” (S202, p. 89).

Typical failure (**H**): a world with one pointing form that does everything, so a reader cannot tell which referent the speaker picked out of the discourse; and a second failure, treating lip-pointing as a manual stand-in for when the hands are full, when observation “quickly reveals” the opposite — people finger point when their hands are full, and lip point when their hands are free (S202, p. 90). Status **B** for the community and count cells, **H** for the design-consequence cells (`../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`); a cell whose community the opened page does not name carries `(to-verify)`. The neighbouring registers keep their own material and are cited one-way: gesture as body code and emblem geography at `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:77-93`, co-speech gesture as the boundary row of the manual modality at `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64` and `:161`, finger counting at `numerals-and-measures.md:107`, and absolute-frame pointing at `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:6` and `:36`.

## 5. Gesture in space and time

| Frame or time line the language keeps | Where the hands go | Design consequence (the designer's own heuristic, **H**) |
|---|---|---|
| Absolute cardinals (Guugu Yimithirr, Hopevale, Cape York) | Pointing keeps true bearings — "If one is talking about going to the beach and the beach is east, one points east" (S187); the frame survives a change of language: "they gesture freely in a way that presupposes absolute orientation" (S206); the language side is carried by `myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:36` | Fix the bearing set before writing an instruction: a hand meaning "to my left" is a contradiction in this world |
| The sky as a clock (Guugu Yimithirr) | "GY speakers frequently point to the part of the sky where the sun would be visible at a certain hour" (S207) | Give the culture a horizon and a sun path, or the time-of-day gesture has nothing to point at |
| Topographic frame (Tzeltal, Chiapas; Tzotzil, Zinacantán) | Uphill and downhill carry the load (`myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:39`); Tzotzil speakers "also display precise bodily orientations to space" (S207) | Keep the local axis local: the same phrase sends hands to different slopes in two valleys, and a gesture can be finer than the words for it |
| Static time line (Aymara, Andes) | "The space behind the speaker is the future, and the space in front of the speaker is the past" (S208); the language-side claim sits at `linguistics/semantics-pragmatics.md:118` | Choose one time line and keep it in every scene: a gesture that puts the past behind contradicts the words beside it |
| Topographic time line (Yupno, Papua New Guinea) | "the past is construed as downhill, the present as co-located with the speaker, and the future as uphill" (S209), in a language that "makes extensive use of allocentric topographic (uphill/downhill) terms" (S209) | Let the valley write the timeline: a time line that ignores the terrain reads as foreign speech |
| Horizontal time line from script direction (English, Hebrew) | "English speakers represented earlier time-points by pointing to the left of a reference point, whereas Hebrew speakers pointed to the right of the reference point" (S210); "Written languages appear to organize their readers’ attention in a vector from where text usually begins to where it usually ends" (S210) | Decide whether the script has a direction: if it does, one gesture reads mirrored to readers of the other script |

The frame the language keeps decides where the hands go, and the failure is a hand that points in the writer's frame — a relative "to my left" inside a world of absolute bearings, an uphill hand on a flat map. Bilingualism opens the seam: "fluency in Aymara relates to frontward gestures when referring to the past, whereas fluency in Spanish relates to backward gestures" (S208), so a mixed community needs a rule for which frame a scene is in.

The gesture space moves with the setting as well as the time line: indoors, "Yupno participants primarily produced past-category gestures pointing toward the houses’ entryways, and future-category gestures pointing away from them" (S209). Keep the claim bounded — "the whole system of absolute orientation, the associated gesture and interaction system, is much more than merely a linguistic phenomenon" (S206) — so a culture whose speakers cannot orient is a different culture, and its hands point relative (**H**, `verification/calibration-registry.md:38`; `linguistics/linguistic-anthropology.md:49`).

## 6. Language shapes gesture

| How the language packages an event | What the language does | What the speakers' hands do | Design consequence (lane heuristic) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Manner and path in one clause | "English typically encodes Manner in a verb and Trajectory in a preposition or a verb particle"; "All of them used one clause to encode Manner and Trajectory" (S211) | one gesture that shows both, with "Manner and Trajectory are simultaneously realized" (S211) | Fix the clause count before drawing any gesture |
| Manner and path in two clauses | "Japanese and Turkish typically express the two pieces of information with two separate clauses" (S212) | "Compared to English speakers, Turkish and Japanese speakers were more likely to have Manner Only and Trajectory Only gestures" (S211) | Two clauses mean two gestures, not one sweep |
| Path in a satellite, manner in the main verb (satellite-framed) | "Speakers of satellite-framed languages such as English express the path in a so-called satellite, like up, and manner in the main verb" (S213) | "the gestural representations of the same events differ across languages when they are encoded by different syntactic frames" (S213) | Name the framing type in the design record |
| Path in the main verb, manner outside it (verb-framed) | "the verb root at once expresses both the fact of Motion and the Path"; Manner or Cause then goes in "an independent, usually adverbial or gerundive type constituent" (S214) | the same event is gestured differently in a satellite-framed and a verb-framed language (S213) | Name the framing type in the design record |
| A word the language does not have | English has the intransitive verb swing; Japanese and Turkish "do not have any equivalent intransitive verb" (S212) | Japanese and Turkish speakers drew "straight gestures" that did not show the arc trajectory, English speakers "arc gestures" that did (S212) | A missing lexical item quietly deletes the gesture for it |
| The same language, two clause choices | "English speakers were more likely to express manner and path in a single gesture when they described manner and path within a clause" (S212) | the hands follow the clause the speaker chose this time, not a fixed habit (S212) | Let a scene change the clause and the gesture changes with it |
| The same element left out in both languages | English and Turkish speakers who said only manner or only path gestured alike (S213) | "gestural information was found to fit the semantic encoding of the event rather than compensate" (S213) | Do not use gesture to carry what the lexicon refuses to carry |
| What the sentence never said | the direction of the event was never verbalized (S211) | "the same Trajectory Only gestures encoded the direction of the event that was never verbalized" (S211) | Check the hands against the clause before adding a gesture |

**Design consequence (lane heuristic).** Decide the lexicalization pattern first — at the syntax-skeleton and core-lexicon stages (`conlang-methodology.md:76`, `:82`) — and the speakers' hands follow it; the typical failure is a gesture system that contradicts its own language: a two-clause event drawn as one English-style sweep, or a scene whose hands carry a path the lexicon has no word for.

## 7. Quotable gestures and emblem repertoires

- **Boundary**: the inventory of emblems, their geography and their collision examples are `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:79,:92-93`; this section owns only how a repertoire is documented and specified, and who may use it.
- **What a documented repertoire is**: Brookes's repertoire of quotable gestures among black urban South Africans in Gauteng province falls into three gestural types — lexical, holophrastic and concept (S215) — and its published dictionary gives every entry an English gloss, the local term, a language label (that dictionary prints Is = Iscamtho, S = Sotho, Z = Zulu) and a body description (S215); the entries reach greeting, money, eating, silence and insult (S215).
- **How one is documented**: observations and video of spontaneous talk, elicitation interviews and a decoding test (S215); in the wider literature, procedural inventories with a stated decoding threshold, observational inventories, and emblem dictionaries that are “usually light on methods” (S190).
- **Two older lineages**: de Jorio's 1832 Naples treatise states its aim as “un saggio della mimica de' Napoletani” and prints 21 plates (S216); Kendon's recordings near Salerno separate two gestures that carry emblem status from two that only mark discourse structure (S217); Payrató's emblem has autonomy from speech, communicative purpose, illocutionary force, a semantic core and a social nature, and he calls emblematization a process beside lexicalization and grammaticalization (S218).
- **Yes and no that differ**: the head-movement inversion is a body-code collision owned at `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:92`; this entry adds only the opened study and its calibration row (§12, S219).

| Repertoire size | Domains it must cover | Who may use which |
|---|---|---|
| a household set: a few dozen entries, greeting and insult (design frame, **H**) | insult emblems seem especially widely attested (S190) | everyone, and outsiders pick them up by contact, because emblems are used without speech and travel across language barriers (S190) |
| a town repertoire: 30 or more entries, the survey's own cut-off (S190) | plus money, eating and silence — the no-speech domains (S215) | by age, place and social network, and the reading can change with the performer (S190) |
| one register per trade or rite (design frame, **H**) | whatever a silence rule pushes into the hand (design frame, **H**) | specialists and initiates only (design frame, **H**) |

- **Failure**: a repertoire that lists only insults, or that gives forms without the contexts of use that would tell a reader who may use them (DR-GES-04), is the usual failure, and the dictionaries show it — they are “usually light on methods” (S190); and a head movement that means yes in one town and no in the next is the cheapest way to lose a treaty (design frame, **H**; the inversion at `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:92`).

## 8. Social control: taboos, politeness, hand and status

Gesture is a register with rules: a culture's hands, targets, audiences and spaces are all licensed, and its politeness is visible in who may move and in front of whom (design frame, **H**).

| control | attested case | design consequence |
|---|---|---|
| the hand in giving and pointing | in Ghana "a set of conventions restrict the use of the left hand", and "Virtually all Ghanaians consider giving, receiving and eating with the left hand to be rude" (S220, p. 61); of the 26 speakers who gave route directions, all acknowledged the general left-hand taboo and 16 also the taboo on direction-indicating left-hand gestures (S220, p. 62); the corpus behind the study is Ewe route-description recordings in Ghana, non-elicited, with speech and gesture together (S221); one community reports that "Giving, receiving, eating, and drinking with the left hand are considered rude by virtually all members of the community" (S222, p. 22) | take the culture's hand polarity and its institutions from `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:99` one-way, add the pointing hand, then design the substitute forms the taboo forces — the reported respect position and the bi-manual or right-hand forms (S220, p. 62) (design frame, **H**) |
| pointing at people, and at what is feared | a peer-reviewed cross-cultural study reports taboos "against left hand pointing, index-finger pointing, or pointing at people" and records that not pointing at people is "a taboo common, e.g., in Europe, the Americas, Japan", and that such taboos "are explicitly represented in folk theories of conduct and politeness" (S223, pp. 700, 708); the MPI field manual lists as elicitation items that "in Laos it is said to be rude to point at people, but departures are common and do not usually attract sanction" and that "one should not point at a rainbow for fear of getting a bent finger as a result" (S202, p. 94); one community adds a place-restricted taboo: left-handed residents "dare not to point their left hands at their father’s house" (S222, p. 22); pointing at a rainbow is reported as "a foolhardy act" in Hungary, China, Mexico and Gabon, with the consequences "getting jaundice, losing an eye, being struck by lightning, or simply disappearing" (S224) | give each taboo a body: what is pointed at, who may not point, and which rung of `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:76` backs it — and decide whether it is enforced or merely stated, since the manual's own question "Are pointing taboos actually followed or merely stated as rules?" is the design test (S202, p. 94) (design frame, **H**) |
| gesture rate and size by status and setting | with a socially distant superior, 14 Korean and 14 Catalan participants "curtail gestures when interacting with a socially distant superior", producing fewer gestures, "reduced their gesture space" and less manner encoding (S225); a study of Korean televised drama finds deference to status superiors marked by "suppression of gestures and touching" versus "frequent gestures and touching behaviors" with status equals (S226; screen data, not field observation); by setting and community, "Taiwanese mothers produced gestures at a much higher rate (about three times more) than American mothers" and Mediterranean gesture is "more expansive, elevated, and on the lateral (transversal) plane" (S212, pp. 27–28) | budget gesture volume, size and space per status pair and per setting, and make the hierarchy visible in the amount of body allowed; a single uniform rate erases rank (design frame, **H**) |
| gesture inside oaths and rites | the lane's binding-speech entry already owns the oath machinery and its channel rows, including the reddened ring on which oaths are sworn (`../myth-symbol/binding-speech-complex.md:108`, `:127`); a community's own rite rule excludes the left hand: "the left hand has no role to play in cultural and customary rites in the community when it comes to ritual performances" (S222, p. 22) | decide the bodily act of the oath (which hand, which object) in the same design pass as the hand polarity; the gesture is the visible edge of the binding speech, so it cannot be left to the words alone (design frame, **H**) |
| who may gesture to whom | the hand rule is addressee-graded: exchanging money "with a trader or elderly person with the left hand is considered disrespectful and rude", and giving a gift with the left hand to one's own child "indicates that you suspect the child not to be your own biological child" (S222, p. 22); the manual asks whether it is "impolite to point in the company of certain people" (S202, p. 94); in the screen corpus, status superiors receive "suppression of gestures and touching" while status equals receive frequent gesture and touch (S226) | write the permission matrix by addressee (elder, trader, kin, superior, equal) before writing the gesture inventory; a rule that ignores who is watching has no social meaning (design frame, **H**) |

Status: the case column rests on the sources opened on 2026-10-22 (tiers A and B; the K&E 2001 article itself is paywalled, so its study is reported here through the MPI's own annual report of the study and the MPI archive record of its Ewe corpus); the design-consequence column is the lane's own heuristic (**H**, `../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`); a cell whose community, sanction or data source the opened page does not name carries `(to-verify)`.
One-way pointers, not restated: the emblem and posture gradients stay with `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:79-93`, the co-speech-gesture boundary row with `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64` and `:161`, finger counting with `numerals-and-measures.md:107`, pointing in absolute-direction systems with `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:6` and `:36`, and the taboo-and-sanction ladder with `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:76`.

## 9. Acquisition and learning

A gesture register is learned in a fixed order — before speech, beside speech and across languages — and each stage fixes one thing a designer must decide for the fiction (design frame, **H**).

| stage | what the opened source shows | design consequence in the fiction |
|---|---|---|
| preverbal pointing, around 12 months | index-finger pointing emerges "around 12 months of age" (S223, p. 699), and in seven field sites — Rossel Island (Yélı̂ Dnye), Bali, Kyoto, Montaro Valley (Peru), the Tzeltal and Yucatec Maya areas of Mexico, and Nova Scotia (S223, Table 1, p. 702) — "by 10–14 months of age, infants and their caregivers pointed in all cultures in the same basic situation with similar frequencies" (S223, p. 698), while the majority of each site's infants began "with the index finger between 10 and 14 months of age, with no significant differences between cultures" (S223, p. 705) | the child in the fiction points before it can name what it points at: give the culture a pointing repertoire its adults already use (§4), so a child's first request has a form an adult can read; the failure is a child whose first act of communication is a spoken word in a culture whose adults expect a point |
| gesture leads speech | children produced a gesture for an object "3.0 months" on average before the word for it (S227, p. 369), and gesture-plus-word pairs carrying two different elements — pointing at a bird while saying "nap" — predicted the onset of two-word speech, while pairs repeating the same referent did not (S227, pp. 367, 369) | the child's early hand must add information the word does not carry, because a pair that repeats the referent predicts nothing; the failure is a child whose gesture only echoes the word, so the pair adds no meaning and the next stage has nothing to build on |
| adults' pointing form is taught, not given | index-finger pointing "has been described as non-universal among adults but foundational to linguistic communication in ontogeny" (S223, p. 701), and "certain forms of pointing may be learned through explicit teaching" (S223, p. 700) | the newcomer is marked by the hand: decide whether the community teaches its pointing form in households, at school or nowhere, and let the outsider's different form stay visible; the failure is assuming one form everywhere — and the claim that some communities lack index-finger pointing altogether is `(to-verify)`, since the source reports it rests on untraceable hearsay (S223, p. 708) |
| a second-language learner keeps the first language's gestures | adult learners of L2 Dutch showed "a pattern similar to their L1 preferences but different from those of native Dutch speakers" (S228, ¶26), and learners of L2 French showed "both transfer of L1 semantic representations into the L2 and beginning shifts towards target representations" (S228, ¶29); heritage speakers of Turkish in the Netherlands kept "able to maintain gesture rate for each language" (S229, p. 422), with no rate transfer between the high-gesture Turkish and low-gesture Dutch patterns (S229, p. 423) | the translator in the fiction carries one community's hands across two languages: decide whether the translator's L1 gestures persist in the L2 and write the mismatch into the scene; the failure is a newcomer or translator who instantly adopts the target community's gestures, so nobody can be read as a stranger in the hands |

Status: the developmental and transfer findings are **B** — bounded to the sampled sites, ages and tasks, and Liszkowski's own caution is that "any claims about universality must be treated with caution" (S223, p. 708) — and the design column is the lane's own heuristic (**H**, `../verification/calibration-registry.md:35-39`); §4 owns the pointing forms, the emblem and body-code register stays at `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:77-93`, the co-speech-gesture boundary row at `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64` and `:161`, finger counting at `numerals-and-measures.md:107`, and the absolute-frame pointing case at `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:6` and `:36`.

## 10. Decision table and build order

| If your culture… | then its gesture system needs… | and check |
|---|---|---|
| frames space absolutely or by landmark — uphill, sunward, riverward | a pointing default that lands in that frame rather than in the speaker's body | §4, §5; `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:44` (frame table, one-way); the prescriptions in this table are the lane's design heuristic (**H**, `../verification/calibration-registry.md:38`) |
| packages motion in its words | hands that follow that packaging: decide what the language already says in words before deciding what the hands add | §6; S211 (Turkish, Japanese and English speakers gesture the same cartoon differently) |
| keeps a closed set of ritual or taboo gestures | a quotable repertoire with a taboo list each taboo placed on the sanction gradient at `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:76` | §7, §8; `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:75`, `:76`; pointing taboos are reported at S223 |
| ranks people by age, office or standing | gesture size and rate scaled by rank (posture and distance from `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:82`), and a rule for who may gesture to whom | §8; `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:82` (gradient table, one-way) |
| has a manual language of its own | a hard boundary: co-speech gesture and a manual system must not be merged into one register | §3; `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64` (the boundary row, one-way) |
| marks time on a line or in a spoken convention | a gesture time line set by the same convention the language uses | §5 |

1. Fix the language's frame of reference and its event framing first; the hands follow them. *Artifact: gesture-brief sheet (frame, framing pattern, pointing default).*
2. Choose the pointing form and the gaze rules: the index finger is not the default everywhere (S200), so write who may point at whom and at what. *Artifact: pointing-and-gaze sheet with the taboo list.*
3. Build the quotable repertoire: every gesture glossed, the domain it covers, and each taboo's rung on the `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:76` gradient. *Artifact: quotable-gesture table with glosses.*
4. Set gesture rate, size and space by status pair and setting (§8); take the posture and distance gradients from `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:80-91` one-way and add only the gesture-size column. *Artifact: gesture-size column keyed to the body-code gradient table.*
5. Write the learning path and the misread table: infants pointed before they spoke in all seven sites S223 sampled, so a child, a newcomer and a translator each fail differently. *Artifact: acquisition-and-error table plus one scripted scene.*

|---|---|---|---|
| DR-GES-01 | Decide what gesture is in this culture: part of the utterance, or a separate manual system | a signed system, a silence rule, or a rite with fixed hand forms | a "universal body language" paste with no boundary — the §3 error |
| DR-GES-02 | Set the frame of reference before choosing a pointing default | the language has uphill, sunward or riverward terms | hands that contradict the culture's own words for place |

## 11. NON-CANON worked micro-example

**NON-CANON.** The Vellmark people of the invented Vellmark uplands, their language, their gestures and their numbers (the upland terraces are nine) are invented for this worked example; nothing here describes a real community, and canon belongs to lore-shokunin. The language fixes space absolutely — uphill and downhill, never the speaker's left — so the frame register and its costs live at `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:36`, and co-speech gesture stays on the speech side of the manual boundary at `sign-languages-manual-modality.md:64`; it also marks motion in the verb rather than in an adverb, so, like Turkish and Japanese speakers, whose separate manner and path clauses come with more Trajectory Only and Manner Only gestures (S211; §6), a Vellmark speaker's hand draws the path on its own, apart from the manner (the S-type/V-type contrast in Talmy's typology is one of the language characteristics McNeill names gesticular differences as tracing to, S230; "verb-framed" is this entry's own label for the invented language, not the source's term). Walking the §10 decision table: §3 gives the Vellmark hand three jobs — the three quotable gestures below, the pointing form, and the accompanying motion; §4 takes the chin-and-lip point, since the vector of that point is set by the gaze and the chin raise is nearly always there, a design choice (**H**): the absolute frame does not require it, since Guugu Yimithirr speakers point with the hand in true bearings (S187); §5 puts the time line on the vertical, past uphill and future downhill, because a vertical time axis is attested and its direction follows the language's own metaphor rather than a fixed human one (S231), with the lane's own caution on vertical time metaphors at `semantics-pragmatics.md:118`; §6 keeps path in the verb, so the hand draws the path alone, in true uphill or downhill bearings, never as one English-style sweep blending path and manner; §7 fixes three quotable gestures, each with one stable form and one verbal gloss, culturally specific and translatable rather than universal (S230), the wider emblem taxonomy being owned at `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:79`; §8 sets one taboo — the promise gesture is not made toward an elder, and the sanction is social rather than legal (the sanction gradient lives at `politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:76`); §9 gives one learner's error — the beginner who says the past while the hand marks the future, so that gesture and words disagree, which is the mismatch the child-learning literature reports (design inference from S232); the failure a designer must design against is the one §10 makes visible: a relative pointing habit or a borrowed time axis inside an absolute-frame language, where the gesture contradicts the words.

| Vellmark gesture (invented) | Gloss (English) | Domain | Who may use it, and the sanction |
|---|---|---|---|
| palm turned up, held out flat | "give it to me" | exchange | anyone, to an equal or a junior (design frame, **H**) |
| index finger to the lips, then to the listener | "I say this to you only" | secrecy | adults (design frame, **H**) |
| closed fist at the shoulder, twice | "I swear it" — the promise gesture of the §8 taboo | oath | never toward an elder; the sanction is social, not legal (`politeness-taboo-euphemism.md:76`) |

| Claim | Status | What to say instead |
|---|---|---|
| Gesture is a universal body language | R | co-speech gesture turns up wherever researchers have looked — "there is no report of a culture that lacks speech-accompanying gestures" (S212) — but the forms and the readings are conventional: "Emblems vary across cultures" (S212), and the emblems a community uses differ in inventory and in what they mean (S189). Typical failure: one gesture glossary that every outsider reads |
| Pointing with the index finger is universal | R | the index finger is the onset form, not the adult repertoire: "by 10–14 months of age, infants and their caregivers pointed in all cultures in the same basic situation with similar frequencies" (S223), while "index-finger pointing has been described as non-universal among adults" (S223) and for Naples the review records that "at least five different types of hand shapes are used for pointing" (S212). Fix your community's point forms, focus rules and taboos (§4); the absolute-frame case stays with `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:36`, its source record with `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md:6`. Typical failure: one finger that means one thing everywhere |
| Nodding means yes everywhere | R | an opened source reports the mapping reversed in Bulgaria — "in Bulgaria, this response pattern is reversed" (S219): horizontal head movement means yes there, vertical means no. Typical failure: a scene in which every nod agrees |
| The thumbs-up or the OK ring means the same everywhere | R | the reading is conventional, not global: one community's everyday emblem is “grounds for fisticuffs in another” (S189), and inventories differ in size (S189). Give each community its own emblem list and its own collisions (§7); the emblem register and the geography stay with `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md:79`. Typical failure: a shared gesture dictionary no community would recognise |
| Italians gesture more than anyone | C | the one measured comparison gives Italian narrators "significantly more gestures per 100 words" than Swedish ones (S234), but the same paper calls the wider rate evidence "somewhat patchy and often contradictory" (S234), Efron's two immigrant groups "differed very little in gesture frequency" (S234), and rate evidence is "rare in the literature" (S212). Treat rate, space and function as per-community dials a designer sets (§10); the primer's own line is "Gesture is not just for Italians" (S189). Typical failure: a national character written out of a stereotype |
| 93% of communication is nonverbal | R | what the 1967 experiments measured was attitude from a single spoken word — "The vocal communications of attitude were superimposed on a neutral word" (S235) — and the author limits the equation to feelings and attitudes: "Unless a communicator is talking about their feelings or attitudes, these equations are not applicable" (S236); the formula then spread as "an “urban legend” about communication" (S237). Never quote a percentage of meaning; say what the gesture adds to the line (§1). Typical failure: a voice-over that explains a character in percentages |
| The Aymara future-behind finding is a general law | B | one community, one method: "Speech and gestural data were collected through videotaped ethnographic interviews with Aymara speakers from the Andes highlands of Northern Chile" (S208) with thirty speakers aged 38 to 84, and the direction tracks fluency — "fluency in Aymara relates to past-front/future-behind gestures, whereas fluency in Spanish relates to past-behind/future-front gestures" (S208). Write it as one community's convention with a stated method (§5); the language side stays with `semantics-pragmatics.md:118`. Typical failure: a whole people rewired in time, asserted without method |
| Mirror neurons explain gesture | C | the reviewed evidence is narrower: there is "strong evidence from patient, brain-stimulation, and brain-imaging studies that mirror-neuron brain areas play a causal role in copying of body movement topography" (S238), while these areas "contribute to low-level processing of observed actions" but "not to high-level action interpretation" (S238). Design who learns which gesture and from whom (§9); do not make a neural mechanism the reason a gesture exists. Typical failure: a gesture justified by neuroscience instead of by a community's habit |

**Living-practice flag.** Gesture repertoires belong to named communities and living speakers — Aymara-speaking communities of the Chilean Andes, Italian narrators in Naples and Swedish narrators in Lund, Bulgarian participants, and the field sites of Rossel Island (Papua New Guinea), Bali, Kyoto, the Montaro Valley (Peru), Tzeltal and Yucatec Maya communities, and Nova Scotia (S223, S219, S234, S208). ## 14. Scope

Reference method and lane policy for the linguistics partition. This entry owns co-speech gesture, pointing and quotable gestures as a conlang and worldbuilding method: what a gesture carries beside the words, what each gesture type does, pointing in space and time, what a language packages into its speakers' hands, emblem repertoires, the social control of gesture, and how gesture is learned and carried across languages.
It hands the emblem taxonomy, the posture and bow gradients and the proxemic bands to `../culture-material/adornment-body-codes.md` (`:79`, `:82`); the manual modality, homesign and contact-sign boundary to `sign-languages-manual-modality.md` (`:64`, `:161`); finger counting to `numerals-and-measures.md` (`:107`); frames of reference, cardinal directions and the frame table to `../myth-symbol/direction-symbolism.md` (`:36`, `:44`); gesture animation in a game to the game-dev lanes; and canon to lore-shokunin.
It names communities and languages precisely, forbids no practice, and rules on no clinical, legal or heritage-policy question; a fictional culture borrows mechanisms, never a community's identity.

Handoff: lore-shokunin owns whether a gesture, a pointing form or an emblem exists in a world, who may use it and what it means there; this entry ends at the design record, the calibration bank and the worked example, and never decides canon.
