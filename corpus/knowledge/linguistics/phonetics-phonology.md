## 1. Two levels: phonetics vs phonology

- **Phonetics** = physical speech sounds; **phonology** = the system a language
  builds from them. A *phoneme* is a contrast category (minimal pair /pæt/ vs
  /bæt/); an *allophone* is a contextual variant of one phoneme ([pʰɪn] vs
  [spɪn] — aspiration is not contrastive in English).
- The IPA classifies consonants by **place** (bilabial → glottal, ~11 columns)
  × **manner** (plosive, affricate, fricative, nasal, trill, tap, lateral…) ×
  laryngeal state (voiceless, voiced, aspirated, ejective, implosive, click),
  and vowels by **height, backness, rounding** (IPA 1999).
- Sounds group into **natural classes** via distinctive features (Jakobson,
  Fant & Halle 1952; Chomsky & Halle 1968); rules target classes, never grab-
  bags — an unnatural set is a design smell.
- **Apply:** give each culture a phoneme *chart*, not a word list, and decide
  early which contrasts it *hears*: speakers mis-hear foreign sounds through
  their own grid (Japanese r/l merger).

## 2. Inventory design (comparative data)

- WALS anchors (Maddieson 2013): modal consonant class **19–25** (~35% of 562
  languages); **5–6 vowel qualities** modal (~45%); CV(C)-type "simple"
  syllable systems ~54% of 534.
- Extremes: Rotokas **11 phonemes** (Firchow & Firchow 1969); Pirahã 8
  consonants + 3 vowels in men's register (Everett 1986); Hawaiian 8+5
  (Elbert & Pukui 1979); Ubykh ~80 consonants, 2–3 vowels (Vogt 1963); !Xóõ
  80+, mostly clicks (Traill 1985); Kabardian 2 vowels on Kuipers' (1960)
  analysis.
- **Economy/symmetry**: grids fill systematically; contrasts sit where
  acoustics are robust. **Adaptive dispersion**: vowel systems spread to even
  perceptual spacing — /i a u/ for 3 vowels, triangular /i e a o u/ for 5
  (Liljencrants & Lindblom 1972; Schwartz et al. 1997).
- Inventory size is **not** a complexity/progress scalar: tiny (Pirahã) and
  huge (!Xóõ) inventories coexist in small hunter-gatherer societies;
  complexity migrates to phonotactics/morphophonology (Everett 2005; Nevins
  et al. 2009).
- Decision table: isolated small oral culture → small/mid inventory, heavy
  phonotactic work; trade hub → mid/large + loan-adaptation alternations;
  priestly castes → layered registers; nonhuman anatomy → consult §7 first.
- **Apply:** size the inventory from contact history, not coolness; every
  exotic phoneme must sit in a series of siblings, as clicks sit in !Xóõ's
  series.

## 3. Phonotactics: what sequences are legal

- Universals of shape: onsets easier than codas; codas restricted (often to
  sonorants) or banned; complex codas rarest (WALS "Syllable Structure").
  **Sonority scale** obstruents < nasals < liquids < glides < vowels governs
  clusters (Clements 1990); "s + stop" (English *stop*) is the standard
  exception.
- Extremes for flavor: Nuxálk words with no vowels, e.g. /xłp̓x̣ʷłtłpłłskʷc̓/
  (Newman 1947); Tashlhiyt Berber utterances of pure syllabic consonants (Dell
  & Elmedlaoui 1985); Georgian multi-consonant onsets.
- **Weight**: many languages count mora (Japanese) or syllable weight (light
  CV vs heavy CVV/CVC), driving stress and verse structure (Hayes 1995).
- Decision table: CV(C) + fixed stress → chant-like, smooth; CCC onsets →
  aggressive texture; mora-timed open syllables → loanwords broken by
  epenthetic vowels (cf. Japanese).
- **Apply:** write the syllable template (e.g. `(C)(C)V(V)(C)`) *first* and
  generate 100 words. Three identical CV syllables per word = template, not
  phonotactics; add weight, clusters, or coda restrictions.

## 4. Markedness and implicational universals

- **Markedness** (Trubetzkoy 1939; Jakobson 1941/1968) ranks values as
  typologically expected vs unexpected. Implications: voiced fricatives imply
  voiceless; front rounded vowels imply front unrounded and back rounded; mid
  vowels imply high; ejectives imply plain stops (Greenberg 1963; Maddieson
  1984; Eckman 1976 — L2 difficulty tracks markedness distance).
- Acquisition orders track the same scale: nasals/stops early, /m/, [i], [a] as
  anchors (Jakobson 1941/1968) — good for fictional child speech and
  reconstruction strata.
- **Calibration:** markedness is a statistical tendency, not law or universal
  difficulty; "unmarked" is relative to the system, and strong "all
  languages…" claims have counterexamples (2-vowel Kabardian vs 3+-vowel
  reanalyses).
- **Apply:** use implications as a generator — pick exotic options first
  (ejectives, front rounded vowels), add everything they entail; break
  implications only *deliberately*, as an alien/ancient register flag.

## 5. Allophony: the texture layer

- Real patterns to copy: English /t/ → [tʰ] onset, [ɾ] intervocalic, [ʔ] coda;
  Japanese /h/ → [ɸ] _u, [ç] _i, with [ɡ]→[ŋ] for many speakers (Vance 1987);
  Spanish /b d ɡ/ → [β ð ɣ] between vowels (Hualde 2005); Korean three-way stop
  contrast carried by VOT and f0 (Cho, Jun & Ladefoged 2002).
- Rule orderings (feeding/bleeding/counterfeeding; Kiparsky 1968) create opaque
  alternations — the line between a language that looks *generated* and one
  that looks *lived in*. **Phonologization** (Blevins 2004): yesterday's
  phonetic bias is today's rule; allophony is frozen sound change.
- Optimality framing (Prince & Smolensky 2004): systems trade off markedness
  vs faithfulness — distinctness, ease, contrast. Useful as a pressure
  checklist.
- **Apply:** 5–8 rules (voice assimilation, palatalization before front
  vowels, unstressed reduction, nasalization, coda weakening) give most of
  the texture at near-zero cost; derive them from plausible drift so they
  have a history.

## 6. Prosody: stress, pitch accent, tone, intonation

- Typology (Hyman 2006): stress accent, pitch accent, tone (register vs
  contour; Yip 2002). Tone is not exotic: ~42% of WALS languages lack it, ~33%
  simple tone, ~25% complex; 2-tone systems most common, 5+ rare.
- Areal tendencies: Africa → level register-tone with downdrift/downstep
  (Welmers 1973); East/Southeast Asia + Mesoamerica → contour tones.
  **Tonogenesis**: loss of laryngeal codas reinterpreted as low/high pitch
  (Haudricourt 1954; Thurgood 2002).
- **Discredited:** the stress-/syllable-/mora-timed rhythm-class acoustic
  dichotomy (Pike 1945) — weak, gradient correlates (Ramus, Nespor & Mehler
  1999; Arvaniti 2012). Keep it as folk texture only.
- All languages have intonation above word prosody; stress languages still
  reduce vowels and lengthen phrase-finally.
- **Apply:** prosody follows communicative ecology — long-distance speech →
  pitch prominence; liturgy → register tone + fixed weight; bureaucracy →
  stress + reduction; verse meter on the real weight system.

## 7. Environment, anatomy, society → inventory (calibrated)

Solid couplings:
- Whistled speech: mountains + long-distance herding → whistled registers
  (Silbo Gomero, Kuşköy, Chinantec; Meyer 2008).
- Drum language: tone + gongs → tone-and-rhythm coding of set phrases
  (Kele/Congo; Carrington 1949); needs tonal lexicon + redundancy.
- Sign-language phonology works without acoustics — handshape, location,
  movement carry contrast (Sandler & Lillo-Martin 2006); deaf cultures
  need this layer.

Contested — seeds only, flag downstream:
- Ejectives ↔ altitude (Everett 2013): mechanism argued, sampling criticized;
  not established.
- Tone ↔ dry climate (desiccation; Everett, Blasi & Roberts 2015):
  cross-correlation; contested — commentaries object on mechanism and on
  contact confounds (Hammarström 2016; Collins 2016, 2017; Donohue 2016), and
  a diachronic Bantu model finds no effect once geography and phylogeny are
  controlled (Hartmann et al. 2024), though raw global correlations
  (Maddieson & Benedict 2023) and Chinese voice data (Liang et al. 2023)
  still fit; flavor, never law.
- Post-Neolithic bite → labiodental f/v (Blasi et al. 2019): influential;
  contested — causal modelling is claimed to refute it (Tarasov & Uyeda
  2020, answered by Blasi et al. 2020), the inventory statistics are
  challenged (Berthommier & Boë 2019), while a word-list follow-up does
  support the association (Chen & Everett 2020). Soft *era* marker:
  pre-agricultural cultures skew away from f/v.
- Genes → tone bias (Dediu & Ladd 2007): probabilistic bias, not
  determinism; contested.
- Small isolated societies accumulate complexity (Trudgill 2011; Lupyan &
  Dale 2010): tendencies reported, robustness debated.
- "Cold = no nasals": no solid source — climate work touching nasality is
  about nasal-cavity anatomy (Noback et al. 2011) or the sonority/temperature
  association (Maddieson 2018), neither of which licenses a nasal-inventory
  law.

Anatomy for fictional speakers:
- Human two-tube vocal tract enables quantal [i a u]; the old "Neandertals
  could not say [i]" claim (Lieberman & Crelin 1971) was rebutted — their
  vowel space ≈ ours (Boë et al. 2002). No "archaic = vowelless" tropes.
- Most mammals: one-tube tract, poor formant control (Lieberman, Klatt &
  Wilson 1969; Fitch 2000) → almost-human species should have restricted
  vowel analogues unless given articulators.
- Bird syrinx = two independent sound sources (Suthers 1990) → polyphonic
  phonation is grounded; odontocete clicks come from nasal bursae (Cranford
  et al. 1996); elephant calls are infrasonic (Poole et al. 1988).
- Vocal learning is rare (humans, songbirds, cetaceans, bats, elephants;
  Janik & Slater 1997) → without it: poor imitation, iconic systems.
- **Apply:** write the speaker's articulatory spec sheet (sound sources,
  resonator, hearing range, control), derive available contrasts, then
  apply §2–§6; hearing range sets the channel and whisper register.

## 8. Sound symbolism (couples: symbol, myth)

- Core finding: statistical biases, not per-language dictionaries. Size–sound:
  high front vowels ↔ small, low/back ↔ large (Sapir 1929; Ultan 1978; Blasi
  et al. 2016; Haynie et al. 2014). **Frequency code**: high pitch/small tract
  ↔ smallness/submission, low ↔ dominance (Ohala 1994).
- **Bouba/kiki**: robust across most cultures/scripts (Ćwiek et al. 2022) but
  **not universal** (Bremner et al. 2013, Namibia); Köhler (1929),
  Ramachandran & Hubbard (2001).
- **Apply:** name flavor at population scale (diminutives leaning /i/), not a
  meaning key; best uses: divine names, taboo coinages, myth etiologies.

## 9. Conlang pitfalls checklist

1. Kitchen-sink inventory: exotic phonemes with no series or economy (§2).
2. English-orthography thinking: letters as phonemes, no allophony (§1, §5).
3. No phonotactics: flat CVCV or random jams; no weight; arbitrary stress (§3).
4. Uniform phoneme frequencies: use is Zipf-skewed (Zipf 1935) (§5).
5. Tone as decoration: contour pitch, no tonogenesis, no intonation (§6).
6. Markedness inversion as politics: size ≠ sophistication (§2).
7. Sound-symbolism dictionary-itis: bias ≠ per-language meaning code (§8).
8. Pop-science determinism: contested claims pasted unflagged (§7).
9. One pronunciation per language: no child/elder/liturgical registers (§7).
10. Achronic allophony: no sound-change origin; derive from drift (§5).
11. Human anatomy for aliens: [i a u] assumed for beaks and syrinxes (§7).
12. Inventory as complexity gauge: complexity lives elsewhere (§2).
