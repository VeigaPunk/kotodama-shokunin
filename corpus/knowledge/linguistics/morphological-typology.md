## 1. The two axes, not four boxes

The classical "four types" are actually four stops on **two independent gradients** (Sapir 1921; Comrie 1981):

1. **Index of synthesis** — how many morphemes a typical word packs (how much meaning per word).
2. **Degree of fusion** — how tightly bound and how transparent those morphemes are (one-morpheme-one-form vs. portmanteau forms merging several meanings).

History: F. Schlegel (1808) split inflectional vs. affixing languages; A.W. Schlegel (1818) added the isolating type (tripartite split); Humboldt (1836) added "polysynthetic". Sapir (1921) decoupled synthesis from fusion and stripped the moral ranking off the scheme. Use the gradients; the four labels are convenient handles on them.

## 2. The four types at a glance

| Type | Mechanism | Exemplars | Synthesis index* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Isolating (analytic) | One morpheme ≈ one word; grammar carried by word order and particles | Mandarin, Vietnamese, Yoruba | 1.0–2.0 |
| Agglutinative | Words built by stacking transparent affixes; one affix ≈ one function | Turkish, Japanese, Finnish, Hungarian, Quechua, Swahili, Tamil | ~2.0–3.5 |
| Fusional (inflecting) | Portmanteau endings; one form expones several categories; stem classes and alternations | Latin, Russian, Sanskrit, Arabic (partly), Georgian | ~2.0–3.0 |
| Polysynthetic | Whole clauses in one verb; argument pronouns + derivations + often incorporated nouns | Inuktitut, Mohawk, Chukchi, Yimas, Nuu-chah-nulth, Mapudungun | 3.0–5.0+ |

\* Greenberg (1960: 193) measured morphemes-per-word on running text: Annamite (Vietnamese) 1.06, Persian 1.52, English 1.68, Old English 2.12, Yakut 2.17, Swahili 2.55, Sanskrit 2.59, Eskimo 3.72. Rule-of-thumb bands: <2.0 analytic, 2.0–3.0 synthetic, >3.0 polysynthetic. Note English sits mid-gradient, not in a box: Old English was synthetic, Modern English is drifting analytic.

## 3. Decision table: what each typology commits you to (worldbuilder's cost sheet)

| Type | Grammar lives in… | Word order | Arguments/pronouns | Lexicon grows by | Worldbuilder failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Isolating | Word order, particles, serial verbs, context | Rigid, grammar-bearing | Obligatory full NPs or bare particles | Compounding, collocation, tone | Assuming "no grammar" — isolating languages have intense grammar, just in syntax |
| Agglutinative | Case/agreement suffix chains, derivational stacks | Free-ish (case licenses it) | Droppable (marked on verb/noun) | Affix productivity: thousands of forms from one root | Making it a soup of glued particles with no harmony or allomorphy texture |
| Fusional | Inflectional paradigms, agreement concord, declension classes | Free-ish | Cross-referenced by agreement | Irregular paradigms as fossils; stems + classes | Uniform regular paradigms — real fusional languages are full of syncretism and alternations |
| Polysynthetic | The verb as mini-clause; head-marking | Near-free, discourse-driven | Usually absent as separate words | Verbal derivation and incorporation | Long words that are just isolating sentences with hyphens — no incorporation, no argument absorption |

Corollaries (Comrie 1981; Nichols 1986, 1992; WALS 23A): fusional and agglutinative languages lean **dependent-marking** (case on nouns); polysynthetic languages lean **head-marking** (arguments cross-referenced on the verb); isolating languages often mark neither, so word order and function words carry everything. Marking locus is a second design dial that should be set *with* typology, not left random.

## 4. Morphological processes catalog

| Process | What it does | Real exemplars | Hook for a fictional culture |
|---|---|---|---|
| Suffixing/prefixing | Attach before/after root | Turkish -ler/-im/-den; Bantu prefixes | Suffixing is globally dominant (WALS 26A); a prefixing language reads as exotic |
| Infixing | Affix inside the root | Tagalog -um-: sulat → sumulat 'wrote'; Bontoc fikas → fumikas 'to be strong' | Infixes feel "magical" — meaning threaded *into* the holy word |
| Circumfixing | Discontinuous affix wraps root | German ge-lieb-t 'loved'; Indonesian ke-adil-an 'justice' | Ring morphemes suit ritual wrapping/enclosure symbolism |
| Reduplication | Repeat all/part of root for plurality, intensity, frequency | Malay orang → orang-orang 'people'; Tagalog bili → bibili 'will buy' | Chant-able doubling; child-speech and diminutive registers |
| Ablaut (apophony) | Root vowel grades alternate | English sing/sang/sung (PIE e~o~zero grades) | Vowel as tense/mood carrier — good for songs-that-change-things |
| Umlaut | Root vowel assimilates historically to a suffix vowel | foot/feet, German Mann/Männer | Residual "haunted" alternations from a lost suffix |
| Consonant mutation | Initial consonant alternates by grammar | Welsh cath → y gath 'the cat'; Irish lenition/eclipsis | Mutation as visible spell-state marker on the same name |
| Root-and-pattern (templatic) | Fixed consonantal root + vowel melody slots into a template | Arabic k-t-b: kataba 'wrote', kitāb 'book', kutub 'books', kātib 'writer', maktab 'office', maktūb 'written/letter' | The natural substrate for true-name magic (see §5) |
| Tone (grammatical) | Pitch alone expones case/gender/TAM | Somali ínan 'son' vs. inán 'daughter' (Saeed 1999); tense/aspect in many Bantu languages | Spoken spell vs. written text divergence: tone invisible in plain script |
| Suppletion | Unrelated forms fill one paradigm | go/went, good/better, person/people | Old ritual verbs keep suppletive "true" forms after regular forms erode |
| Incorporation | Noun root absorbed into the verb | Mohawk ra-wir-a-núhweʔ-s 'he-likes-babies' (Mithun 1984: 853) | Predation verbs, blessing verbs: patient dissolved into the act |
| Subtraction | Segment deleted to inflect | Rare but attested: Tohono O'odham (Papago) perfective truncation (Zepeda 1983); Lardil nominative truncation (Hale 1973); Alabama/Koasati pluractional disfixes (Hardy & Montler 1988; Broadwell 1993) | Cheap "reversal" motif: undo a sound, undo a thing |

## 5. Root-and-pattern deep dive (the word-spirit engine)

- Best attested in **Afro-Asiatic**: Semitic (Arabic, Hebrew, Akkadian), Berber, Cushitic. Outside that family the argued case is **Yokuts** (Yawelmani/Chukchansi, California — templatic affixes that reshape the root: Archangeli 1983; Guekguezian 2017); elsewhere it is rare and contested, so do not treat it as a universal option.
- The **root is a consonantal skeleton** (usually 3 consonants) with an abstract meaning field (k-t-b = writing); **patterns** (vowel melodies + affixal slots) force it into word classes. One root yields dozens of predictable words; dictionaries are ordered *by root*, not alphabetically by word — a real cultural artifact of scribal practice you can import.
- Coupling to myth: a consonantal "spirit" that must be dressed in a vocalic "flesh" to be speakable is a ready-made metaphysics. Mispronounce the melody and you summon the root without its pattern — a mechanical, checkable magic system, not vibes.
- Era coupling: template systems resist sound change in the melody slot (the paradigm keeps the pattern audible), so a temple register can preserve vowels that everyday speech has reduced — schism between liturgical and street pronunciation is historically plausible.

## 6. Incorporation and the Polysynthesis Parameter

Mithun (1984) gives a four-stage functional cline, each implying the ones before:

1. **Type I — lexical compounding**: N+V forms a new verb stem (ink-writing 'writes with ink'); no syntax touched.
2. **Type II — case manipulation**: incorporation strips the noun of its argument role, demoting it (instrumental/body-part idioms).
3. **Type III — discourse manipulation**: incorporated noun marks *given/old* information; a full noun signals *new* referent. The choice is an information-structure device.
4. **Type IV — classificatory**: verb incorporates a generic noun, with a full noun supplying the specific one ('I meat-eat it' — the fish).

Baker (1996) formalizes polysynthesis: the **Polysynthesis Parameter** — every phrasal head must be morphologically expressed on the verb; Mohawk-style languages then show no free pronouns, no articles, verb-as-sentence. Use Mithun for *function*, Baker for *constraint*. Practical consequences for a culture: discourse tracks animacy and novelty rather than word order; oath and curse verbs are complete micro-sentences; "naming" is a derived-verb industry, not a noun list (Sapir 1911 began this debate; it still frames the field).

## 7. Cascade into grammar

- **Isolating**: grammar budget moves to word order + particles → serial verb constructions, aspect particles, and (cross-linguistically frequent) classifiers arise to make countable the uncountable (Aikhenvald 2000). Rigid order means poetry gets its music from parallelism, not inflection.
- **Agglutinative**: one-function-one-form chains → enormous derivational productivity (Turkish, Finnish, Quechua); vowel harmony commonly rides along (Turkish, Hungarian, Finnish); morphological transparency makes finite-state-style regularity the default — irregularity is a marked, learnable exception, so irregular forms can be culturally prestigious (archaic, liturgical).
- **Fusional**: portmanteau exponence forces declension/conjugation classes and syncretism (Latin puellīs = dative *and* ablative plural); rich agreement licenses free word order, and free word order feeds information-structure-fronting rhetoric. WALS 22 measures how many categories can stack on one verb — its maxima in the WALS sample are Koasati and Wichita at 12–13 categories per word (North America), with 10–11 for Abkhaz, Ingush, Alamblak, Imonda, Upper Chinook, Lakhota and Yagua — i.e. the Caucasus plus North America and New Guinea (WALS 22A).
- **Polysynthetic**: WALS 26A confirms global suffixing dominance — a polysynthetic language that prefixes its person markers (like many Iroquoian) is a deliberate rarity; evidentials and switch-reference are frequent riders on the verb, so *how you know* and *who did what to whom* are grammatical, hence inescapable, hence cultural.

## 8. Cascade into lexicon

- Isolating → homophony pressure from short roots; two classic pressure valves: **compounding** and **tonogenesis** — Vietnamese lost final consonants, and compensation re-purposed pitch as contrast (Haudricourt 1954). If your analytic culture speaks a tonal language, its puns, taboo-substitutions, and name-avoidance systems get tonal too.
- Agglutinative → dictionaries of roots + affix inventory; one root radiates hundreds of dictionary words; word-coinage is a lay skill, so lexical innovation is fast and loans integrate via suffix slots.
- Fusional → irregular paradigms are fossils of sound change applied to formerly regular fusions; lexicon stratifies by age of borrowing (each loan era has its own declension class — excellent for an empire with historical strata).
- Polysynthetic → the verb is the dictionary: activities, instruments, and even many "nouns" are derived verbs; translation of scripture or law is constrained because one European word may need a full clause — realistic translation-friction plots.
- Semitic-type → word families cluster visibly (kātib/maktab/maktūb), so folk etymology across the family is constant; scribes organize knowledge by root — encyclopedias, indexes, and magical correspondences all inherit root-organization.

## 9. Diachrony: the typology wheel (era coupling)

Givón (1971): "today's morphology is yesterday's syntax." The wheel (Hopper & Traugott 2003):

1. Discourse strings of independent words grammaticalize into auxiliaries/serials (**analytic →**).
2. Auxiliaries phonologically erode into agglutinated affixes (**→ agglutinative**).
3. Continued erosion fuses affixes into portmanteau endings; paradigms go opaque (**→ fusional**).
4. Opaque inflection is abandoned for periphrasis; endings drop (**→ analytic**); loop to 1.

Worked real case: Old English (2.12 morphemes/word, case endings) → Middle English collapse of case under sound change → Modern English (1.68, periphrastic *did/have/will*). Middle Chinese → Mandarin repeats the same wheel. **For the lane**: set each culture's language at a wheel position consistent with its attested era and contact history; a "young" colonial language should be analytic-with-agglutinating-futures, not fusional. The wheel also generates registers: liturgy preserves yesterday's stage; the street runs ahead of the script.

## 10. Calibration: contested and discredited

- **The 19th-century ranking is dead.** Isolating→agglutinative→fusional as a ladder of "progress" (implied in F. Schlegel 1808) is discredited evolutionism; Sapir (1921) already rejected the value ranking. Never let a fictional culture's "primitiveness" ride on typology.
- **No pure types.** Every attested language mixes processes; Greenberg's own numbers show gradient scores, not kinds. Assigning a fictional language a single clean type is a starting position, not a fact.
- **"Agglutinative = regular = easy to learn"** is folk belief; agglutinative languages have their own opacity (allomorphy, harmony, boundary fuzziness) (Haspelmath & Sims 2010).
- **Equicomplexity is contested.** The comforting claim that all languages are "equally complex, just differently distributed" is empirically disputed — McWhorter (2001) argues measurable overall complexity differences exist (creoles simplest). Write complexity consciously; do not lean on automatic compensation.
- **Typology ≠ worldview.** Strong claims that morphological type determines thought (popularized Humboldtian/Whorfian readings) lack robust support; weak, usage-based effects are defensible. In-world *beliefs* about language shaping spirit are fine — that is the culture's ideology, not the narrator's.
- **Isolating ≠ thoughtless/simple-minded**, **polysynthetic ≠ "primitive long words"**: both remain common exoticist errors in fiction; both are calibrations this lane should actively correct in review.

## 11. Application protocol (kotodama workflow)

1. **Place the language** on both axes: target synthesis index (give a number) + wheel position (era-consistent).
2. **Pick 3–5 processes** from the §4 catalog; at least one should be non-affixal (tone, ablaut, mutation, pattern) to give the language audible texture.
3. **Derive grammar consequences mechanically** from §3 and §7 (marking locus, order rigidity, argument expression); if two choices conflict (e.g., rigid order + rich free-scope case), resolve and note why.
4. **Derive lexicon rules** from §8 (how new words are minted, how dictionaries/registers are organized, what loans do).
5. **Write one full paradigm** (8–12 forms) and one sentence family (declarative / negative / past / imperative / oath) — the paradigm is the deliverable's proof of life.
6. **Write the incantation register note**: which morphology marks ritual speech (archaic fusion, reduplication, mutation, tone) and what laypeople mispronounce — this is the myth-symbol coupling point.
7. **Hand off to lore-shokunin** as reference knowledge and method only; canon decisions (which culture speaks what, what magic actually does) stay with lore-shokunin. Flag any contested claim carried forward with its `to-verify` marker intact.
