## 1. Semantic fields: what the lexicon must split

- Trier (1931): the **field**, not the word, is the unit — words define each other jointly; his case study traced the medieval German intellectual-vocabulary field (wîsheit / kunst / list) reshaping c. 1200–1300 as courtly culture changed. Lehrer (1974) made field structure testable (cooking terms, substitution classes, gaps). Sense-relation toolkit: hyponymy, meronymy, antonymy types (Cruse 1986; Lyons 1977).
- Boas (1911, preface) is still the best worldbuilding sentence in the literature: every language groups all experience, but *chooses differently* — the point is never "a language has no word for X" (you can always paraphrase) but "which distinctions the grammar makes obligatory". Obligation is constant rent; elaboration is occasional luxury.
- **Lexical elaboration** = many near-synonyms on one dimension (rice terminologies, herd vocabularies). The "Eskimo snow words" figure is a documented exaggeration chain — English compounds snow terms just as freely (Martin 1986; Pullum 1991). Don't build a culture on word-count flexes.
- **Obligatory marking** = every noun/verb forced through a category (shape, animacy, source of knowledge, relative age of the addressed). Stronger lever: it taxes every utterance, not just specialist speech.
- Folk taxonomy ranks (Berlin 1992): unique-beginner → life form → **generic** (largest, shortest names, most culturally salient) → specific/varietal. Depth at the generic level follows folk-biological engagement. Fields can cut on culture-specific dimensions: Conklin (1955) — Hanunóo basic color categories cross hue with succulence-vs-dryness and lightness-vs-darkness.
- Color: Berlin & Kay (1969) implicational sequence (black/white → red → green/yellow → blue → brown → others), refined by Kay & Maffi (1999); method and sampling contested (Saunders & van Brakel 1997). Use as a *constraint sketch*, not law.
- Kin terminologies: Murdock (1949) types — Eskimo (nuclear-family focused), Hawaiian (generational only), Iroquois (bifurcate merging), Omaha (lumps via mother's lineage), Crow (mirror via father's), Sudanese (descriptive, bureaucratic). Each encodes an alliance logic — the cheapest "deep culture" import available.
- NSM (Wierzbicka 1996): ~60 semantic primes (I, YOU, GOOD, BIG, BECAUSE…) as a minimal paraphrase language; use to *test* whether an invented keyword does definable work (list itself has shifted across editions; universality debated).

| Pressure | Gives you | Mechanism |
|---|---|---|
| Subsistence salience (rice, herds, tides) | elaborated fields, fine verb sets | optional vocabulary, genre-linked |
| Ritual/political ranking | obligatory honorific/address distinctions | grammar: constant tax |
| Folk biology, medicine | generic-level taxonomy depth (Berlin) | naming customs |
| Law, testimony | source-of-knowledge marking | see §4 |
| Commerce, counting | numeral systems + classifiers | see §3 |

- Budget: max ~2 obligatory systems + 2–3 elaboration fields per culture, or the language reads as a novelty shop.

## 2. Polysemy networks and metaphor paths

- Prototypes (Rosch 1975): categories center on best examples; membership gradients. Radial categories (Lakoff 1987): polysemy hangs off a central sense with conventionalized extensions.
- Conceptual Metaphor Theory (Lakoff & Johnson 1980): systematic mappings — TIME IS SPACE, ARGUMENT IS WAR, KNOWING IS SEEING — grounded in embodied experience; metonymy organizes within a frame (part-for-whole, producer-for-product, place-for-institution: Radden & Kövecses 1999).
- Recurrent, largely one-way extension paths worth mining:

| Path | Examples | Source |
|---|---|---|
| perception → cognition | "see" → understand; "hear" → obey | Sweetser 1990 |
| space → time | before/after; "ahead of schedule" | Sweetser 1990 |
| body part → object/terrain part | foot of the mountain; head of a nail | Ullmann 1962 |
| content → epistemic (subjectification) | temporal "while" → concessive; epistemic "must" | Traugott 1989 |
| noun → measure → classifier/affix | "head (of cattle)"; "going to" → future | Hopper & Traugott 2003 |
| amelioration / pejoration | knight (boy → noble); silly (blessed → foolish) | Ullmann 1962 |

- Change operators (Ullmann 1962): metaphor, metonymy, broadening, narrowing, pejoration, amelioration — apply to every etymon you invent; grammaticalization ladders (Hopper & Traugott 2003) convert them into morphology over eras.
- Calibrations: (a) Sandra & Rice (1995) showed proposed polysemy networks (English "over") are not psycholinguistically recoverable as claimed — networks are analyst artifacts until tested; Tyler & Evans (2003) tightened the method. (b) Haser (2005) dismantles much of cognitive semantics' evidential base (circularity, thin cross-linguistic sampling). (c) Lakoff's (1987) "women, fire, and dangerous things" romanticizes Dixon's (1972) Dyirbal noun classes — the balan class unites myth/function-linked *subsets*, not one semantic essence; don't import "mythic logic in grammar" wholesale.
- Fiction method: give each key verb/noun a 3–4 step path (literal → social → mental); draw path material from the culture's own life (a delta culture: silt, mooring, soundings); audit against metaphor monocultures (everything-is-honor) — real languages mix domains.

## 3. Classifier systems: grammar counting the world

- Royen (1929) claimed all languages classify nouns (to-verify) — refuted (Aikhenvald 2000). Typology (Aikhenvald 2000; Senft 2000):

| Device | Locus | Real examples | Encodes |
|---|---|---|---|
| noun class / gender | agreement | French le/la; Swahili's ~16–18 Bantu classes; Dyirbal 4 | animacy, sex, function, arbitrary residue |
| numeral classifier | with numbers | Mandarin 本/张/只/个; Japanese 人/匹/頭/枚/本; Thai; Vietnamese | shape (1D/2D/3D), animacy, size, arrangement |
| possessive classifier | possession | Oceanic: general vs edible vs drinkable vs precious | owner's relation to the owned |
| verbal classifier | verb stem/agreement | Athabaskan handling verbs; Mayan noun classifiers (Jacaltec; Craig 1986) | shape/manipulability of patient |
| locative classifier | place expressions | rare; see Aikhenvald 2000 | figure shape at rest |

- Recurrent semantics (Adams & Conklin 1973): 1D/2D/3D shape, rigid/flexible, size, animacy, function; humans almost always split out. Denny (1976): classifiers encode manipulability — "what you do with it". Large inventories exist: Kilivila (Trobriands) has well over 100 numeral classifiers (Senft 1996).
- Lucy (1992): Yucatec Maya (numeral classifiers, no plural) vs English — classification habits spill into nonlinguistic sorting tasks; modest, well-controlled relativity evidence.
- Design procedure: (1) pick at most 2 devices (a language with both noun gender and numeral classifiers is rare — check Aikhenvald 2000 for mixed systems before doing it); (2) inventory 5–15 classifiers, each motivated by cultural salience (nets, canoes, honor-things, body-spirits); (3) always include a neutral/general fallback (Mandarin 个 gè); (4) run the **collision test**: new inventions (gunpowder, books, ghosts) get assimilated by extension — the assimilation choices are where values show; (5) classifier choice itself can carry respect or contempt (person-classifiers split by status in several systems).
- Fiction note: counting swords with the "long-thin thing" classifier but reputations with the "precious cargo" classifier tells the reader the value system in grammar, not exposition.

## 4. Evidentiality and mirativity: grammar of where you know from

- Evidentials mark information **source**; miratives mark **unexpectedness** (DeLancey 1997). Their overlap is debated (DeLancey 2001 vs Lazard 1999) — both readings can share one marker (Turkish -mIş: "apparently… / why, it…!"). Evidential ≠ epistemic modality (certainty): Palmer (1986) keeps them separate.
- Willett (1988) hierarchy: attested (visual > non-visual sensory) > inferred (from results > general) > reported (secondhand > thirdhand > folklore).
- Canonical systems: Tuyuca — five obligatory verbal suffixes: visual, non-visual, inferred, reported, assumed (Barnes 1984); Cusco Quechua -mi / -si / -chá (direct / hearsay / conjecture; Aikhenvald 2004); Korean retrospective -te (firsthand experience not witnessed at the moment; Sohn 1999); Turkish -mIš inferential-perfect doubling as mirative (Aksu-Koç 1988).
- Matses (Panoan) dream reports take experienced-past evidentials: dreaming itself is treated as a kind of subconscious visual experience, so dream events are coded as experienced even though the dream content is not credited as real (Fleck 2007; Aikhenvald 2004: 345). Direct myth hook.

| Inventory | Model | Cultural effect |
|---|---|---|
| none | English "apparently, reportedly" | gossip lives in adverbs; testimony is lawyer-work |
| 2 (witnessed vs not) | common Amazonian pattern | a hard witness line — perjury becomes a grammar error |
| 3 (+ inferred) | Quechua-type | expertise class: who may *infer* for others |
| 4–5 (+ assumed, folklore) | Tuyuca-type | rumor stratified by transmission distance; prophecy needs a lane |
| optional but rich | Turkish-type | evidential choice still social, just not forced |

- Futures cannot be witnessed: systems route them through prediction/inference — a built-in humility clause for forecasters. Cultures where dreams are evidentially "experienced" get different novels: dream testimony is admissible.
- Calibrations: obligatory marking does **not** mean constant conscious vigilance — it becomes cheap habit (Slobin 1996 "thinking for speaking"); and evidentials encode source, not truth — asserting a witnessed source for hearsay is grammaticalized lying: courts, feuds, and stage magicians all inherit this.

## 5. Implicature and speech acts: meaning beyond the said

- Grice (1975): Cooperative Principle + maxims (Quantity, Quality, Relation, Manner). Openly flouting a maxim generates implicature (irony, understatement); covertly violating it is deception. Design lever: **which maxim does politeness sacrifice by default?** (Quantity-flouting humility formulas; Manner-flouting euphemism cultures; Relation-flouting ritual circumlocution.)
- Generalized implicatures are default inferences with cancellation licences (Horn 1984 Q/R principles; Levinson 2000). Relevance theory (Sperber & Wilson 1995) reframes it as effect-per-effort optimization — a liturgical culture tolerant of effortful speech is simply a different relevance economy.
- Speech acts: Austin (1962) performatives and felicity conditions (who may speak, in what venue, with what precedent); Searle (1976) five classes:

| Class | Act | Institutional form in a world |
|---|---|---|
| assertives | claim, report | testimony bound by evidentials (§4) |
| directives | order, request | petition registers; who may direct whom |
| commissives | promise, vow | oath formulae with self-curse clauses |
| expressives | thank, condole, envy | mourning etiquette; envy taboos |
| declarations | sentence, christen, excommunicate | the power of naming — couples to naming-and-address |

- A culture where words bind absolutely (no idle promise) has different contract law, children's games, and literature. Conventional implicature: Japanese honorifics add non-truth-conditional, non-cancellable social content (Potts 2005) — you can state facts while the honorifics grovel or sneer.
- Politeness: Brown & Levinson (1987) — positive/negative face, face-threatening acts, on/off-record strategies. Calibration: the universality claim is contested — Matsumoto (1988): Japanese politeness *presupposes* interdependence rather than defending autonomy; Gu (1990): Chinese ideal-face differs; Hu (1944): face = mianzi (social standing lent and borrowed) vs lian (moral character) — a two-word system ready to copy.
- Register engineering: Joos (1961) five clocks (frozen/formal/consultative/casual/intimate); Javanese ngoko vs krama paired vocabularies, jointly computed by relative status (Geertz 1960); Japanese sonkeigo (elevating the other) vs kenjōgo (humbling self) verb morphology (Harada 1976); Dyirbal mother-in-law avoidance register (Jalnguy) swaps out the everyday lexicon near prohibited kin (Dixon 1972).

| Mechanism | Load | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| T/V pronouns | low | binary status display; political eras visible in drift |
| verb morphology (keigo) | high | every clause pays; scribes/courtiers specialize |
| paired lexicon (krama) | very high | doubling the dictionary; translation between in-laws |
| particles | low | softness displayed per utterance |
| kin-term address (Vietnamese anh/chị/em) | medium | every conversation re-entered into kinship |
| avoidance register (Jalnguy) | medium | geography of kinship: rooms become untranslatable |

- Pick 2–3 mechanisms and enforce consequences in every dyad (see §7 step 4).

## 6. Deixis: the anchored words

- Bühler (1934): the deictic **origo** — the I-here-now center. Taxonomy (Levinson 1983): person, spatial, temporal, discourse, social deixis. Silverstein (1976): "shifters" are where grammar and social structure interlock — a pronoun is a tiny institution. Deixis in practice is culture-saturated (Hanks 1990, Maya).
- Social deixis: T/V pronouns track power, then drift to solidarity (Brown & Gilman 1960); revolutionary tutoiement; German du/Sie; Spanish usted < *vuestra merced* "your mercy" — a worn-down honorific. Era lever: plot political change as pronoun drift; costume change and pronoun change co-occur.
- Frames of reference (Levinson 2003): intrinsic ("bow-ward of the hull"), relative ("left of me"), absolute ("there's an ant on your south-west leg" — Guugu Yimithirr uses cardinal directions for all description, and speakers maintain continuous orientation). Absolute frames ride on topography: river-up/down, mountain/sea, windward/leeward. They change narrative voice: no "left of the hero".
- Temporal deixis is flexible: Aymara puts the known past in front, the unknown future behind (Núñez & Sweetser 2006); Mandarin vertical time metaphors (up = earlier/later) — Boroditsky (2001) vs non-replication Chen (2007); effects are small. Treat as flavor, not cognition-forcing.
- Deictic projection (Fillmore 1997): historical present; "come" toward the letter-writer's center; narrative center shifting onto characters — free texture for chronicles, letters, and in-world documents.
- Deixis spec for a culture: (a) what can be origo — speaker only, or also institutions ("here at the law-court" as formal center); (b) spatial frame per domain (navigation absolute, gossip relative); (c) temporal anchors of documents; (d) honorific reference — titles instead of pronouns, name-avoidance (couples to myth: name-taboo).

## 7. Method: designing the meanings a culture actually needs

1. **Life audit**: list institutions, subsistence, ritual calendar, law. Tag each with its meaning-work: *distinguish* (fields, §1), *address* (social deixis, §5–6), *verify* (evidentials, §4), *bind* (commissives/declarations, §5), *avoid* (taboo).
2. **Obligations**: max 2 obligatory grammatical systems, each institutionally motivated (testimony court → evidentials; guild accounting → classifiers).
3. **Elaborations**: 2–3 deep fields (Berlin-level generic richness where folk biology matters; Murdock kin-type where alliance matters).
4. **Politeness stack**: choose mechanics (§5 table); write default behavior for stranger-meeting, insult, petition to a superior, apology. If the scenes can't be written in prose, the system is underdesigned.
5. **Keyword mining**: 3–5 cultural keywords with NSM-style explications (Wierzbicka 1996/1997) — testable paraphrase, not romance; beware nihonjinron-style over-reading (amae: Doi 1973 is the source; the mystique is not).
6. **Taboo chains**: taboo → euphemism → semantic drift → re-tabooing ("euphemism treadmill", Pinker 1994; Allan & Burridge 2006) — this manufactures era strata: yesterday's slang is today's liturgy.
7. **Metaphor audit**: one 3-step path per key domain (§2 table); check for single-dome monoculture.
8. **Deixis spec**: run §6 (a)–(d); verify against your maps and documents — letters and chronicles must visibly use the chosen anchors.

Worked micro-example — delta mercantile culture: obligatory possessive classifiers (provisioning as social fact) + 3-term evidentials (harbor courts weigh cargo claims); politeness = paired lexicon (dock-register vs cabin talk) + kin-term address; absolute river-frame deixis (upriver/downriver replaces left/right); keywords *silt* "settled gain", *soundings* "measured trust"; taboo on naming the drowned → "gone to the deep" → respectful → obligatory → ancestor term (four eras in one euphemism).

Calibration, plainly: strong linguistic relativity (language determines thought) is dead — Whorf's Hopi claims specifically refuted (Malotki 1983); "Eskimo snow words" is a documented hoax chain (Martin 1986; Pullum 1991); Pirahã number/grammar exceptionalism is contested (Everett 2005 vs Frank et al. 2008); color-term universals survive but method-battered (Saunders & van Brakel 1997); universalist overreach fails too (Evans & Levinson 2009). What survives: obligatory marking shapes *habitual attention while speaking* (Slobin 1996; Lucy 1992; Gumperz & Levinson 1996). Design dose: relativity as texture and friction, never as mind-control.
