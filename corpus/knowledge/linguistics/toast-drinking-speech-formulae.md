## 2. The toast formula: a speech act that needs a cup and a company

A **toast** is a formulaic speech act whose fulfilment is shared. That is the whole of it, and everything else in this entry is a consequence of the word *shared*. The act has an addressee, a content, a moment, and a bodily uptake that the company performs at the same time; remove any one of the four and what is left is either a greeting, a speech, or a private intention.

The design consequence is that a toast is not a sentence. It is a **sequence** in which a sentence is only one part (design, not sourced):

1. **The standing occasion.** The toast is placed in something that is already happening — a meal, a gathering, a rite, a departure. A toast with no occasion is a speech that happens to mention a cup.
2. **The cup.** The vessel is filled before the words, and the words are addressed to the company over a vessel that is about to be drunk. The cup is what makes the act collective: what is promised or wished is promised *to the people who drink with you*.
3. **The named addressee.** Somebody is named, or everybody is named. A toast that addresses no one in particular has no receiver, and a wish with no receiver is a mood.
4. **The content.** The wish, the claim, the pledge, the memory — the part that says what is wanted or what is owed.
5. **The uptake.** The drink itself is the uptake: the moment the company drinks is the moment the words take effect. Silence is available to the company, and a formula that forbids silence has lost the feature that makes it a speech act.
6. **The permitted response.** What the company or a single guest may say back — a return wish, a nod, silence, a refusal.

This slot order is a proposal, not a finding (design, not sourced). It is deliberately close to the performative-slot anatomy of `linguistics/oath-blessing-curse-formulae.md`, and the difference is deliberate: the toast's distinguishing slots are the **cup** and the **shared uptake**, and its ordinary slots — addressee, content, uptake, response — are borrowed from the binding-utterance tradition, where the sanction clause has no drinking counterpart at all.

The opened material names this pairing of speech with drinking directly, and the naming is the design gift: a toast joins the mouth's two uses, communication and consumption, so that agreement of mind and community of body become one act rather than two (S1001).

The same account sets out the choreography that makes the act collective: the toast is made standing, around a table, with the glasses touched or merely lifted and a look made (or a gaze into space), before the company drinks together (S1001).

The reply slot has an attested obligation behind it. In an English translation of a legendary toast exchange the corpus can open, the king is told that the lady's words "offered to drink your health" (S1002).

In the same passage the interpreter tells the king what his own answer must be, and that answer is a fixed formula rather than a free reply (S1002).

Three design rules follow from the slot list, and they are the ones a writer gets wrong most often (design, not sourced):

- **The occasion precedes the words.** If you cannot say where the toast happens and who is present, you do not have a toast yet; you have a line of dialogue.
- **The content must be drinkable.** A toast whose content could be delivered equally well by a messenger has missed its medium; the wish should be something the company can drink *to*.
- **The response is part of the formula.** A culture that has a return-wish has made reciprocity structural, and a culture that has only silence has made the toast a monologue over a cup.

## 3. Health-drinking: the cup as the vehicle of a wished state

**Health-drinking** is the most widespread of the toast contents and the one that makes the cup's role plain: the drink is the vehicle of a state the company wishes for its members. The formula takes the shape of a wish, and the wish is carried by the act of drinking together rather than asserted as a proposition.

The design question this section answers is what a health-wish may name. The opened material shows that the wished-for content is not generic: what the cup is drunk *to* is a specific state, a specific person, or a specific relation, and the specificity is what makes the formula usable in a scene (design, not sourced). A workable inventory of drink-contents, with the design question each raises:

| drink-content | what the cup is drunk to | what the formula asserts | design question |
|---|---|---|---|
| the health of the company | a bodily state the drinkers share | that all present may be well | is health a collective property or a personal one? |
| the health of one named person | a state owed to one drinker | that this person is cared for | who may name a person's health without impropriety? |
| a relation | a bond between two parties | that the bond holds | is the relation public enough to be toasted? |
| a dead person or an absent one | a state extended past death | that the dead are still addressed | is the absent drinker present in the order or outside it? |
| a sovereign or office | a state owed by the company upward | that the ruler's standing is acknowledged | does the toast flatter, or does it report? |
| the occasion itself | the success of the gathering | that the event has been well begun | is the wish for the event or for the people in it? |

Three cautions follow from the table, and they are the ones that keep the formula from becoming a wish-granting machine (design, not sourced):

- **A health-wish is a request, not a cause.** It asks for a state; it does not produce one. A design that lets the toast *cause* health has moved from speech act to magic, and it has moved into another entry's territory.
- **The addressee of the wish is not always present.** Wishing health to the dead is a different act from wishing it to the living, and the difference belongs to the funerary offering economy, which is owned elsewhere (`myth-symbol/death-afterlife-eschatology.md`). This entry covers the living-table case and points the other way.
- **The health of the company is a collective claim.** A wish for *all of us to be well* is not the same speech act as a wish for *you to be well*, and a scene that needs one but is written as the other reads as impersonal.

The health-drinking formula has an opening in the record that shows its two halves: the lady's phrase, and the answer the king is told he must give in return (S1002).

The same materials show that the cup is not merely handed over but **prepared**. In the district drinking rite of the record, the host washes his hands, rinses the cup and raises it (S1003).

And the rite in that record is reciprocal in gesture as well as in drink: the company bows when the cup is received, and bows again when it is presented in return (S1003).

The nearest existing material in the corpus is the eating-side health wish, which the cuisine entry owns as table etiquette (`culture-material/cuisine-food-lexicon.md` §6). It is the **analogue**, not the same thing: the eating formula runs at the meal, the drinking formula runs at the cup, and a culture that has both will normally say them in a fixed relation to one another — which is a design decision the culture owns, not this entry.

## 4. Libation and the pledge: the god's share and the promise made wet

Two different acts share the word **libation**, and a designer must choose between them before writing a line, because they have different addressees and different sanctions.

**The deity-ward libation** is an offering. Something is poured out for a power, a spirit, an ancestor or a god, and what is poured is not drunk by the company. The cup is inverted rather than raised. This is the votive edge of the drinking rite, and the conditional-vow grammar of `linguistics/petitionary-and-votive-formulae.md` applies to it: the pledge is a debt the culture can collect on, and the god's share is the sign that the debt has been opened.

**The peer-ward pledge** is a promise made wet. Here the cup is raised and drunk, and the promise is made to the people at the table — an oath whose witness is the company and whose token is the shared drink. The distinction is not decorative: in the deity-ward case the liquid leaves the company for someone else, and in the peer-ward case the liquid enters the company and binds them to one another.

The record of the Greek drinking-party puts the deity's share first and makes it a distinct act: after the company has supped, "libations were offered, and after a hymn had been sung to the god," and only then were they about to commence drinking (S996).

A second ancient account names a cup that is handed round for a god rather than for the company, and separates the god's portion from the wine the company drinks: a law was made that after the solid food was removed "a taste of the unmixed wine should be served round as a sort of sample of the power of the Good Deity" while the rest of the wine was mixed (S998).

The same account links the carrying of that cup to the removal of the tables, though it makes the link through a story about a tyrant rather than through a description of the rite (S998).

The record of the district drinking rite in China gives the shared vessel its social shape: the chief of the district had the vessel of liquor placed between the room and the door, host and guests sharing it between them (S1003).

The implement-level anchor in the corpus is Ainu, where the libation stick and the lacquered cup are used in prayer communication, and where the corpus insists on distinguishing the handling, the speech and the offered drink (`civilizations/ainu.md` §). That row is an exemplar, not a template: it shows what has to be specified (an implement, a manner of use, and a content), not what the rite must be.

The design consequences for a fictional rite (design, not sourced):

1. **Decide the direction of the liquid.** Out for a power, in for the company, or both in sequence — the sequence is itself a strong convention and a good source of scene tension.
2. **Decide whether the pledge is spoken, signed, or embodied.** A rite in which the words are spoken and the liquid is poured separately has two acts; a rite in which the pouring *is* the swearing has one, and the two do different work.
3. **Decide who witnesses.** A pledge witnessed by the cup alone is deniable; a pledge witnessed by named companions who must answer for it is not. The witness question is the binding entry's, and this entry only supplies the cup as a possible witness.
4. **Decide what happens to the unpoured remainder.** A libation rite in which the cup is emptied entirely leaves nothing for the company to drink, and the occasion loses its social half; a rite in which a measure is set aside for the power and the rest is drunk keeps both halves and is the more common shape to design for.

## 5. The toastmaster's office and the order of a toast sequence

A **toast sequence** is an ordered set of toasts with an order of precedence, and in any culture where the sequence is regular there is an office that runs it. The **toastmaster** is that office: a person whose job is to know the order, to call the turns, to say the words, and to keep the sequence from tangling.

The office is worth designing carefully, because it is where a drinking rite acquires a **politics** (design, not sourced). A toastmaster decides, in effect:

- who is called on, and in what order (rank, age, sex, office, relationship, or the order in which people arrived);
- which themes are named and which are not;
- how long each toast may run;
- what is done when a turn is refused or skipped;
- when the sequence is closed.

That is a large amount of social information for one job to hold. Three design options (design, not sourced):

| option | what the office is | what it buys | what it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| the host's own | the host, or the host's senior, calls the turns | continuity with the meal and the household; no separate rank | the host cannot also be a guest; the sequence cannot outrank the host |
| a separate officer | a designated drinker, chosen or appointed | the sequence can outrank the host; a fixed vocabulary and a fixed order | the office needs a recruitment rule, a training and a cost |
| rotating by the guests | whoever was toasted last, or the one with the smallest debt, calls the next | the sequence is self-renewing and the debt chain drives it | the order drifts; a rude guest can stall it; no one owns the close |

The office in its present form is younger than its reputation: the opened chapter finds the toastmaster and contemporary toast-making "conspicuously absent" from Georgian courtly literature and early travellers' accounts, and its words for the office "are not attested before the 19th century" (S999). In the contemporary Georgian banquet the chapter describes, in principle, at every occasion where wine is consumed, even if only two men are present, one of them is selected to be the *tamada*, and the ideal holder of the office is knowledgeable, witty, articulate and a good drinker (S999).

The same chapter makes the office the source of the sequence's order: each round of drinking begins with a toast on a particular topic declaimed by the toastmaster, after which he — and only he — drinks (S999).

And the reply rule is a rule about turns, not about speeches: after the toastmaster finishes, the other guests give toasts of their own one after the other on the same theme, and then each of them drinks (S999).

The order within the opening phase of that banquet is reported as rigid rather than improvised, and the sequence can run to a great many rounds (S999).

The emptied vessel is part of the rule in that account too: ideally each drinker should drink all the wine in his vessel in a single draught, and in practice only the toastmaster is obliged to keep to it (S999).

An older poem shows the order of the cup distributed by rank rather than by a chair. The queen in that hall hands the cup first to a named heir and warden, then carries it to younger and older everywhere, and it comes at last to a named guest who answers when it reaches him (S1000).

A Norse feast record shows the same order worked out in cups rather than speeches: a goblet is emptied first for victory and power to the king, then goblets for peace and a good season, then the goblet whose name carries the oath, and then a goblet emptied to the memory of departed friends (S1006).

The **order of a toast sequence** itself has a shape that recurs, and a designer can vary each of its parts independently (design, not sourced):

1. **The opening.** The host or the toastmaster opens, before any drinking, and the opening names the occasion and the company.
2. **The god's share.** If there is one, it comes early and it is not drunk.
3. **The official round.** Themed, ordered, and usually long. The theme names the host, the occasion, an absent person or an institution.
4. **The personal round.** Guests reply in turn, and the reply usually quotes the theme.
5. **The memory or the boast.** The most dangerous and most entertaining turn, and the one with its own rule about who may speak.
6. **The penalty or the forfeit.** Where refusal carries a cost, the cost has a slot in the sequence.
7. **The close.** A final cup, often the shortest toast in the sequence.

Three rules govern the order of a sequence, each paired with the failure it prevents (design, not sourced):

- **Every turn has a caller.** A sequence without a caller is a free-for-all, and the reader cannot tell whether the disorder is intentional.
- **The reply-toast must quote the theme.** This is what makes the sequence a sequence rather than a list of speeches, and it is what tells a reader that the second toast is a response.
- **The order must be checkable.** If a reader cannot work out why the third guest spoke before the fourth, the order will feel arbitrary; the designer should therefore fix a rank and let the reader infer it.

## 6. The drinking oath and the boast-cup

Two turns of a drinking sequence carry an unusual weight, and they are worth separating because they fail differently.

### 6.1 The drinking oath

A **drinking oath** is a solemn promise made over a cup: the words are sworn, the drink is the token, and the company is the witness. What makes it an oath rather than a toast is not the word but the **sanction** — the promise has a stated cost if it is broken, and that cost is what the drinking ritualizes.

The slot grammar of such an utterance is the binding entry's, and this entry does not restate it (`linguistics/oath-blessing-curse-formulae.md` §2, §6): performative verb, witness invocation, assertion, sanction clause, self-imprecation, uptake. What the drinking frame adds is (design, not sourced):

- the **cup as the third party** — the drink may be named as the witness, which is a stronger claim than naming a bystander, because the cup is consumed;
- the **shared uptake** — the company drinks at the moment the oath is sworn, which makes every witness a participant in the act;
- the **roundness** — the oath is sworn round the table, in an order, which makes the sequence itself part of the obligation;
- the **irreversibility** — the cup is emptied, so the oath cannot be withdrawn once the drink is gone.

The oath over a cup has a plainly recorded instance. At the funeral feast described in the Old Norse saga, the man who had made the feast and was to inherit stands up against the cup called the *bragafull*, strengthens the oath and drinks from the cup; only then is he led to the seat that was his father's (S995).

The edition's own note on that goblet describes it as the cup over which vows were made, and it is the goblet emptied after the ones for victory and for peace (S1006).

The saga's feast record also names the turn that carries memory rather than promise, a goblet emptied to the memory of departed friends (S1006).

The Greek drinking-party record shows guests pledging to one another in the course of the drinking: one answers with the words "Quite right (said Callias), and we will pledge you" (S997).

The cup's movement is what carries the round in that account, and the speed of the circuit is a matter of regulation: the cup-bearers were to push the cups round, quickening the pace each circuit (S997).

The design rule that follows is about **weight**: a drinking oath with no stated cost is a toast, and a toast with a stated cost and a solemn formula is an oath. A writer who wants both — a warm, celebratory sequence that also carries an obligation — should put the oath in one position in the sequence and let the rest of the sequence stay light, because a sequence in which every toast has a penalty is a court, not a feast.

### 6.2 The boast-cup

The **boast-cup** is the turn in which a speaker claims something about themselves and stakes the claim on the cup. The boast's own machinery — framing, claim, warrant, stake, and the audience that tests it — belongs to `linguistics/verbal-duelling-ritual-insult.md` §6 and is not restated here. What this entry owns is the drinking frame that the boast is mounted on:

| feature of the drinking frame | what it adds to the boast | what it costs |
|---|---|---|
| the cup as the stake | the wager is drinkable, and the loser drinks the consequence | the boast can no longer be purely verbal; a wrong claim is now a physical debt |
| the round as the audience | the hearers are already seated in a hierarchy, so a boast is aimed at a known order | the boast cannot be freely aimed; it must respect the order of turns |
| the deadline of the round | a boast with a stated span can be judged in front of witnesses | a boast that fails is a scene-ending failure, not a joke |
| the fullness of the cup | the claim's truth is tested by a body, not an argument | a culture that cannot afford the consequences cannot run this turn |

The Norse feast record gives the memory-turn its own named cup, emptied to the memory of departed friends (S1006), and the same record's oath-cup shows the other pole of the range: a turn that binds rather than recalls (S1006). Reading the two as one machine with two contents — one looking backwards, one looking forwards, both settled by drinking rather than by argument — is this entry's proposal, not the source's statement (inference). The design question the pairing raises for an invented rite is whether the boast-cup is **permitted** or **assigned**, because the two produce very different scenes: a permitted boast-cup invites everyone to claim, while an assigned one produces a single, appointed speaker whose failure is a scheduled event (design, not sourced).

## 7. Refusal, reciprocity and the penalty cup

A drinking rite in which every cup is drunk is a rite with no speech act in it, because the uptake is the act. The **refusal** is therefore a first-class slot, and it must be designed as one.

What a refusal asserts is the interesting part (design, not sourced):

1. **The cup is not accepted** — the act is declined, so the collective uptake does not happen.
2. **The speaker is not available for the sequence** — the turn is passed, and the order must have a mechanism for that.
3. **A statement is made instead** — a refusal can carry content: illness, rank, a religious reason, an objection to the theme, a prior debt.
4. **A debt is opened** — and this is where refusal becomes dangerous, because the company has just invested in a collective act that one person has broken.

That fourth point is the design hinge. A refusal with no cost is a shrug, and the reader will not believe that a culture builds an elaborate rite around an act that any guest can decline for free; a refusal with an enormous cost is a trap the reader will not believe either. Three workable shapes (design, not sourced):

- **the substitute** — a refusal may be answered by another cup, another drink, or another act that stands in (design, not sourced);
- **the forfeit** — the refuser pays a stated cost, in drink, in service, or in status, and the payment is part of the sequence (design, not sourced);
- **the deferred debt** — the refusal is accepted but recorded, and the company may collect later (design, not sourced).

The penalty cup is the institutionalized version of the forfeit: a named, standing, pre-declared cost for a named kind of refusal, already built into the rite before anybody refuses anything. Its design properties (design, not sourced):

- it is **pre-declared** (the audience knows the tariff before the first cup is poured);
- it is **proportional** (the cost matches the offence, and the scale is legible without adjudication);
- it is **non-humiliating** or **humiliating on purpose** (a culture that punishes refusal with mockery has made the penalty social; one that punishes with service has made it material);
- it is **collectable** (somebody's job is to notice and to apply it).

The reciprocity chain is the other half of this section, and it is the mechanism that makes a drinking rite **social** rather than merely **ceremonial**. In a reciprocal rite, each toast creates an obligation to return one, and the company is a ledger (design, not sourced). That has three consequences worth designing for:

- the round can be **tracked** (who owes whom), and a scene can turn on the ledger;
- the debt can be **played for** — a boast-cup is a bet placed into a chain of debts;
- the debt can be **forgiven**, which is an act in its own right and often the emotional peak of the sequence.

The record of toasting in Georgian Britain shows that a refusal could be principled and costless, and that the etiquette charged the man who **ran** the toast rather than the man who declined it: the toastmaster had to down a glass at one go after every toast, and refusing to do so was a breach (S1004).

In the same account, toasts were taken seriously, and a toast could express a man's deeply held principles and his denominational, national or local identity (S1004).

And the rule of the refusal is stated as a rule of conscience: no man must drink a toast if it goes against his principles, and refusing a toast was a mark of manliness and independence of mind (S1004).

The Greek drinking-party record shows the other end of the range, where the company agrees that each will drink only as much as he pleases and that there is to be no compulsion (S996).

The overlap with the hospitality law must be kept clean: the **shared cup as a bond-opening marker, the host's owed package, the breach ladder and the penalty for breaking it** belong to `myth-symbol/guest-right-hospitality-law.md`. This entry owns the words and the ritual slot; that entry owns what the cup legally does and what breaking the rite costs in law.

## 8. The stages of a toast sequence

The table below sorts the stages of a sequence against the openings the sources record and against the options this entry proposes. It is a working frame, and it does not claim that any two traditions resemble each other. Cells tagged *(design)* are proposals of this entry. An untagged cell rests on a source only where a line of the grounding list underneath quotes it; every other untagged cell is this entry's working frame (design, not sourced).

| stage | office and who speaks | what the stage carries | cup action | refusal and exit option |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| opening | the host, or the toastmaster | the occasion named and the company welcomed | cup filled and raised; no drinking yet | a late arrival is not a refuser; the opening may not be declined |
| the god's share | the toastmaster, or a priest | the pledge, or the naming of the power that receives the first pour | the first pour goes out and is not drunk | a non-participant may pour in silence and take no part *(design)* |
| the official round | the toastmaster, in the order of rank | the theme, the host, the absent, the institution | drinking on the theme's last word | a shallow drink is possible; speaking over the toast is not |
| the personal round | any guest, called in turn | the reply that quotes the theme | a sip and a return of the wish | a turn may be passed forward; passing is recorded *(design)* |
| the memory or the boast | a qualified speaker | the memory-turn, or the boast with its warrant | the full cup, drunk to the end | refusal here asserts enmity and is the most expensive turn |
| the penalty cup | the wronged party, or a standing office | the named fault and the pre-declared tariff | drunk in one, with no reply | a refusal converts the penalty into a feud *(design)* |
| the close | the toastmaster | the shortest toast of the sequence | the last cup | the sequence is closed and ordinary speech resumes |

## 9. Decision procedure: a fictional drinking rite whose words stay tied to its hosts, guests, cups and occasions

Here is the procedure itself, offered as a set of steps and nothing more (design, not sourced).

**Before a single line is written (design, not sourced):**

1. Fix the **occasion**: what is happening, who is present, how many, and how long the rite has.
2. Fix the **occasion's owner**: the household, the guild, the ship, the wedding party — the body that the rite belongs to.
3. Fix the **cup**: what is in it, who fills it, who pours it, and whether the same cup or several cups are in play.
4. Fix the **office**: who calls the turns, and how that person is chosen.
5. Fix the **order**: the sequence of stages and the precedence rule within each round.
6. Fix the **themes**: what the official round may name, and what it must not.
7. Fix the **reply rule**: what a guest may say back, and how a reply must quote the theme.
8. Fix the **refusal rule**: what a refusal asserts, what it may be answered with, and what it costs.
9. Fix the **boast rule**: whether the boast-cup is permitted to anyone, assigned to one speaker, or barred.
10. Fix the **close**: the last cup and the signal that ordinary speech resumes.
11. Fix the **vocabulary**: the fixed formulae — the call, the reply, the refusal, the penalty notice, the close — so the sequence is legible as a set piece and not as a series of improvised speeches.
12. Fix the **non-cup neighbours**: which of the meal, the greeting, the parting gift and the funerary offering the rite borrows from, and where the line is drawn.

**While drafting (design, not sourced):**

13. Give every toast a **named addressee**; a toast addressed to nobody is a mood.
14. Make the content **drinkable** — something the company can drink *to*, not a fact that needs a messenger.
15. Make the reply **quote the theme**; that is what makes the sequence a sequence.
16. Keep the turns **even in length** unless rank says otherwise; a sequence where one toast is four times the others reads as a monologue.
17. Let the office **interrupt**; a toastmaster with no power to stop a turn is a narrator.
18. Put the **cost of refusal** in the text, not in the author's head; if the reader cannot see the tariff, the refusal has no weight.
19. Give the sequence a **ledger** if the rite is reciprocal; the debts are what the later turns are about.
20. Write the **cup actions** as stage directions, not as dialogue; the drinking is half the speech act.

**Before the rite is called finished (design, not sourced):**

21. Run every scene past the failure modes of §10 — the toast with no addressee, the cup with no order, the oath-cup confused with the toast, and the refusal with no cost come first.
22. Check that the rite's speech stays **tied to its hosts, guests, cups and occasions**: a toast that could be delivered at any table by any people on any day is a formula without a rite.
23. Confirm that no step above repeats a neighbouring entry's material: the cup goes to the vessel lexicon, the meal to the cuisine entry, the bond and the breach to the guest-right entry, the binding slots to the oath entry, the boast slots to the duelling entry, the vow to the petitionary entry, the room to the furniture entry, the dead to the death-afterlife entry.
24. Check that the entry has **settled no canon**: the people, the rite, the words and the cup are the lore layer's.
25. Record the non-canon status and the handoff in writing.

**A worked micro-example (NON-CANON).** A river-ferry guild on a tidal crossing holds a **first-crossing rite** on the night the new channel is opened. The cup is a shallow, unglazed, double-handled beaker that the guild's senior ferryman fills from the new channel in front of the assembled crews; each crew answers with one short toast naming its own crossing, and the sequence closes when the last crew has been called and the senior ferryman drinks last and sets the cup upside down on the sluice stone. The order of the calling is the order the crews were chartered, so the oldest charter answers first and the newest crew answers last — a precedence the reader can check. One toast in the personal round is permitted to be a **memory-turn**, and one is permitted to be a **boast-turn** with a warrant: a claim that a crew crossed in a flood last spring, backed by the name of the crossing-keeper who logged it. A crew that does not drink when called may send a substitute up to the ladder with a stated reason; the reason is spoken aloud and recorded in the guild's roll, and the roll is what the guild reads at the next spring tide when it settles who crossed first (design, not sourced).

## 10. Error patterns: drinking rites that fail as speech acts

The rules above make these failures checkable, because each one is a missing or broken slot (design, not sourced):

**1. The toast with no addressee.** The company is wished well, but no person and no body is named as the recipient of the wish. The test: ask who receives it. If the answer is *everyone in general*, there is no receiver and the line reads as mood-setting.

**2. The cup with no order.** There is a cup, there are toasts, and there is no precedence and no caller. The test: ask who speaks next and why. If the order is arbitrary, the sequence is a list of speeches.

**3. The oath-cup confused with the toast.** A solemn formula with a stated penalty is described as a toast, or a warm wish is described as an oath. The test: ask whether a cost was stated for breaking it. A promise with a cost is an oath; a promise without one is a toast; a text that uses the words interchangeably has no way to make the reader feel the difference.

**4. The refusal with no cost.** A guest declines the cup and nothing happens. The test: ask what the refusal costs and who collects it. If the answer is *nothing at all*, the whole sequence loses its stakes, because the reader learns that any turn can be dropped.

**5. The reply that ignores the theme.** The personal round consists of guests who each deliver an unrelated speech. The test: ask whether the reply could be moved to a different feast unchanged. If yes, the sequence is a party programme, not a rite.

**6. The toast with no occasion.** The words are warm and the cup is raised, but nothing in the scene says why now. The test: ask what has just happened that makes this the moment for these words.

**7. The office with no power.** The toastmaster calls the turns but cannot stop one, cannot shorten one and cannot close the sequence. The test: ask what the toastmaster does when a guest will not stop talking. If the answer is "nothing", the office is a label.

**8. The penalty that arrives from nowhere.** A forfeit is imposed in the middle of a sequence that never announced it. The test: ask whether the audience could have known the tariff in advance. A surprise penalty is a plot device, not a rite.

**9. The drinking that is not shown.** The text says the toast was made and does not say what happened to the cup. The test: ask what the characters physically did at the moment the words took effect. If the answer is *they drank*, the uptake is missing and the speech act has no completion.

**10. The libation with no direction.** Liquid is poured out or raised, and the text never says to whom. The test: ask where the liquid goes. Out for a power and in for the company are different rites with different addressees.

**11. The sequence with no close.** The toasts continue and the scene never returns to ordinary speech. The test: ask what physically happens once the sequence is over. A rite with no release leaks.

**12. The rite that floats free of its people.** The formula is generic enough to be delivered by any speaker to any audience in any world, so the reader learns nothing about the household, the guild, the ship or the family that holds it. The test: ask what this rite says about *these* people that no other rite would say.

## 11. Checklist

A short list to run before a drinking rite is used in a scene (design, not sourced):

- [ ] Is there an **occasion** named, and is it the reason for the words?
- [ ] Is the **cup** specified — what is in it, who fills it, and what happens to what is left?
- [ ] Is there a **toastmaster or an equivalent office**, and does that person have the power to call, to shorten and to close?
- [ ] Is the **order** legible, and does the reader know why the third guest spoke before the fourth?
- [ ] Does every toast have a **named addressee**?
- [ ] Is the **content drinkable** — a wish, a claim, a memory, a pledge, not a fact for a messenger?
- [ ] Does the **reply** quote the theme?
- [ ] Is there a **fixed vocabulary** — a call, a reply, a refusal, a penalty notice, a close?
- [ ] Is the **refusal** defined: what it asserts, what answers it, what it costs, and who collects the cost?
- [ ] Is the **oath distinguished from the toast** by a stated sanction?
- [ ] Is the **libation's direction** stated — out for a power, in for the company?
- [ ] Does the sequence **close**, and is the signal for ordinary speech visible?
- [ ] Is the rite **tied to its own hosts, guests, cups and occasions** rather than generic?
- [ ] Are the **neighbour boundaries** respected: no cup-naming, no meal-grammar, no binding-slot re-derivation, no boast-slot re-derivation, no bond or breach law?

## 12. What the sources reach, and what is handed to the world bible

**The reach of the sources.** Each opened source grounds this entry only as far as the passage captured from it, and each reports drinking-speech in its own setting. No source surveys toasting across the world, so nothing in this entry is offered as a universal grammar of the toast (design, not sourced).

**Calibration.** What one tradition does stays a fact about that tradition and becomes a *design option* only where a rite is being invented. The slot list of §2, the rules of §5, §7 and §9, the failure modes of §10 and the checklist of §11 are working apparatus of this entry; no claim is made that real cultures share them (design, not sourced). A source that records a particular formulaic expression is reported as that source's record and is not widened into a type.

**Where description stops.** A toast names a genre; it is not a transcript of what anyone said at any table. The corpus's calibration of recorded invective and praise modes is applied here to the whole entry: a formula that is recorded stays a record, and a practice that is described never becomes a universal (design, not sourced).

**Canon and rights are outside the door.** No sentence here settles what happens inside a fictional world, and none rules on whether a real community's drinking rite may be taken up by another; the first question is the canon layer's, the second a rights process (design, not sourced).

**The package lore-shokunin receives.** A surface, never a canon: the toast slot list (§2), the inventory of drink-contents (§3), the split between libation and pledge (§4), the office and the sequence stages (§5, §8), the oath and boast-cup frames (§6), the refusal and penalty machinery (§7), the build procedure (§9), the failure modes (§10), the checklist (§11). The world bible supplies the people, the rite, the cup, the formulae and the language, and rules on whether any drinking rite in the fiction is lawful, sacred or merely customary (design, not sourced).

## 13. Inference ledger

Each item below is a reading this entry takes from an opened source without that source putting it in the same form. In the body the same readings carry the tag *inference* or *design, not sourced*, and a reader who refuses every one of them still keeps every cited claim intact (design, not sourced).

- That the toast's distinguishing slots are the cup and the shared uptake, and that its ordinary slots are borrowed from the binding-utterance tradition (inference).
- That the order of a toast sequence is a form of social ranking that a reader can check, and that a designer should therefore fix a precedence rule (inference).
- That a refusal in a collective rite is a speech act with four distinct assertions, of which the fourth (an opened debt) is the one that gives refusal its weight (inference).
- That the reciprocal debt chain is what makes a drinking rite social rather than merely ceremonial, and that a rite with no ledger has no later turns to be about (inference).
- That the boast-cup and the memory-turn are the same machine with two contents, and that the design question is whether the turn is permitted or assigned (inference).
- That a drinking oath without a stated cost is a toast, and that the difference is the whole difference (inference).
- That a libation whose direction is unstated is the commonest drafting failure in a drinking scene, because the direction decides the addressee (inference).
