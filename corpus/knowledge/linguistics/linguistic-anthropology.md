## 1. Ethnography of communication: the SPEAKING grid

Founding move (Hymes 1962; Gumperz & Hymes 1972): the unit of analysis is the **speech event** (a prayer, a bargain, an initiation speech), not the sentence. A culture is partly constituted by its inventory of named speech events; a fictional culture feels real once it has native genre names (the oath-swearing, the formal refusal, the funeral praise).

The SPEAKING grid (Hymes 1974) — eight components, each a worldbuilding lever:

| Letter | Component | Ethnographer asks | Fictional lever |
|---|---|---|---|
| S | Setting, Scene | When, where; physical vs psychological framing | Dawn temple oath vs tavern banter: same words, different meaning by venue |
| P | Participants | Speaker, addressee, audience, overhearers | Audience alone can force a register switch (see avoidance registers, 5.2) |
| E | Ends | Goals and outcomes | Ritual speech whose end is magical efficacy, not information (Tambiah 1968) |
| A | Act sequence | Form and content, order | Formulaic openers, fixed turn order, parallel couplets |
| K | Key | Tone, manner, spirit | Mock-serious, liturgical, ironic; key failure is a faux pas or an insult |
| I | Instrumentalities | Channel and codes/registers | Chanted vs spoken vs written; argot vs standard vs liturgical code |
| N | Norms | Interaction and interpretation norms | Who may interrupt; what silence means (Bauman 1983 on Quaker silence) |
| G | Genre | Category of event | Catechism, threat, riddle, praise poem; give each an in-culture name |

Worked method (Saville-Troike 2003): for a fictional culture, fill the grid for 6-10 named speech events before inventing vocabulary. A genre inventory plus norms does more ethnographic work than any lexicon.

Extension (Gumperz 1982): conversational code-switching is a **contextualization cue** — the switch itself signals stance ("we speak Noldori at home, Aurellic at court"). A code switch is content, not decoration.

## 2. Linguistic relativity, calibrated

| Version | Claim | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Strong (popular "Whorfianism") | Language determines thought; no word means no concept | Discredited; also a strawman of Whorf, whose own claims concerned habitual thought (Whorf 1956; Pinker 1994; Pullum 1991) |
| Sapir/Whorf as written | Language is a "guide to social reality"; shared habits shape habitual attention (Sapir 1929, Language 5) | Reasonable but hard to falsify; standard careful reading (Lucy 1992) |
| Neo-Whorfian (weak) | Language biases perception, categorization, memory in measurable ways | Real but modest effects, replicated in specific domains (Lucy 1992; Gumperz & Levinson 1996; Levinson 2003); contested as a grand theory (Casasanto 2008; McWhorter 2014) |

Evidence ledger (domains where effects survive best; pop-science versions survive worse):

| Domain | Finding | Key source |
|---|---|---|
| Color | Universal focal colors exist, yet lexical categories speed discrimination across named boundaries (Russian blues; Kay & Kempton 1984, American Anthropologist 86) | Berlin & Kay 1969; Kay et al. 2009; Winawer et al. 2007 (PNAS 104) |
| Space | Communities using absolute (cardinal) frames, e.g. Guugu Yimithirr, maintain constant dead-reckoning orientation (Levinson 2003) | Haviland 1979; Levinson 2003 |
| Number | Piraha, lacking exact numerals, fail exact-number tasks; language as a cognitive technology | Gordon 2004 (Science 306); Frank et al. 2008 (Cognition 108); Everett 2005 (Current Anthropology 46) |
| Gender | Grammatical gender shifts object associations (a "bridge" feminine vs masculine gets different adjectives) | Boroditsky, Schmidt & Phillips 2003 |
| Time | Vertical time metaphors in Mandarin (Boroditsky 2001, Cognitive Psychology 43); Pormpuraaw speakers map time east-to-west | Boroditsky & Gaby 2010 (Psychological Science 21) |
| Agency | Agentive (English) vs non-agentive (Spanish) event framing shifts who is blamed and remembered as culprit | Fausey & Boroditsky 2010 (Psychonomic Bulletin & Review 17) |

Calibration dial for fiction: level 0 no effect (universal cognition); level 1 attention bias (default, always defensible); level 2 habitual metaphor transfer (allow one or two per culture); level 3 trained perception such as absolute orientation — plausible only in small face-to-face societies that practice it daily from childhood, and lost within a generation of displacement (a ready-made story beat).

Best single middle concept: Slobin's "thinking for speaking" (1996, in Gumperz & Levinson 1996) — language shapes cognition on-line, during the act of speaking. Portable to any scene of negotiation, prayer, or testimony.

Standing warnings: the "Eskimo snow words" claim is documented folklore built on a fabricated number (Pullum 1991, The Great Eskimo Vocabulary Hoax); "no word for X, therefore cannot think X" is always false — all languages can paraphrase any proposition, so the interesting question is what is habitual, not what is possible (Pinker 1994). Deutscher (2010) is the sound popular synthesis; McWhorter (2014) the sound anti-hype corrective.

## 3. Language ideology

Definition (Silverstein 1979, in Clyne, Hanks & Hofbauer (eds.), The Elements): "sets of beliefs about language articulated by users as a rationalization or justification of perceived language structure and use." Ideology does the social work: it makes one way of speaking feel natural, superior, sacred, or shameful (Woolard & Schieffelin 1994, Annual Review of Anthropology 23; Kroskrity 2000).

Irvine & Gal's three semiotic processes (2000, in Kroskrity (ed.), Regimes of Language) — the sharpest worldbuilding tools in this file:

- **Iconicity**: linguistic features are read as icons of group essence ("lilting speech means a gentle people").
- **Erasure**: ideology deletes what does not fit (a "pure peasant nation" erases its bilingual trading cities).
- **Fractal recursivity**: one opposition is re-projected at other scales (civilized/barbaric becomes capital/province becomes quarter/quarter becomes master's speech/servant's speech).

How features acquire social meaning: **indexical order** — features index class, gender, stance at a first order; when folk ideology names them they become second-order stereotype indexicals ("the posh R", "the dropped H") (Silverstein 2003, Language in Society 32). **Enregisterment** — a repertoire becomes a recognizable register of a social persona (Agha 2005, Journal of Linguistic Anthropology 15; Agha 2007). Print, schooling, and broadcast accelerate enregisterment, so era choice changes what "an accent" can even mean.

Standard language ideology (Lippi-Green 1997): the belief in one correct standard, policed by a "complaint tradition" of anxiety literature (Milroy & Milroy 1999) and "verbal hygiene" reform movements (Cameron 1995). Bourdieu (1991): a linguistic market prices ways of speaking; linguistic capital converts to economic and political power.

| Ideology type | Signature behaviors | Conflict seeds |
|---|---|---|
| Purity/purism | Loanword purges, etymological folklore, language academies (Academie francaise 1635; Real Academia Espanola 1713) | Reformers vs poets; vernacular Bible vs altar Latin |
| Sacred-language ideology | Liturgy frozen in an old code; vernacular worship treated as heresy | Translation riots; "the god speaks archaic only" |
| One-language-one-nation | Erasure of minorities, dialect shaming, toponym renaming | Secessionist counter-standardization |
| Accent meritocracy | Accent discrimination read as class truth (Lippi-Green 1997) | Passing, elocution schools, revenge plots |
| Prescriptive style norms | "Proper women's speech" manuals, swear gaps by gender | Register policing as plot engine |

## 4. Register ecology: diglossia, politeness, speech levels

**Diglossia** (Ferguson 1959, Word 15): two varieties of one language in stable functional distribution. High: codified, literary, usually no native speakers, learned in school, used for sermon, lecture, news. Low: home, market, intimacy. Classic pairs: Classical Arabic/colloquial Arabic; Katharevousa/Dhimotiki Greek; Standard German/Swiss German; French/Haitian Creole.

Fishman's matrix (1967, Journal of Social Issues 23): diglossia without bilingualism is a stable elite; bilingualism without diglossia is unstable and conflict-prone; both together is stable (Switzerland-style); neither is monolingual simplicity. Locate any fictional polity in this 2x2 and the instability falls out of the placement.

**Power and solidarity** (Brown & Gilman 1960, in Sebeok (ed.), Style in Language): T/V pronouns originally encode power asymmetry (superior gives T, receives V); after revolutions the drift is toward mutual-solidarity T. Pronoun history is political history: a fictional revolution should visibly change pronoun etiquette.

**Politeness** (Brown & Levinson 1987): face wants (positive: approval; negative: autonomy) and strategies from bald on-record through positive and negative politeness to off-record hints. The universality claim is contested — treat as a strong tendency, not a law.

**Speech levels** (Geertz 1966, in Bright (ed.), Sociolinguistics, on Javanese ngoko/krama/madya; cf. Japanese keigo): status is grammaticalized — every utterance encodes relative rank, and speakers compute level per sentence per relationship. Craft effect: one mis-leveled sentence is a plot-sized insult, with no exotic vocabulary required.

Quick distinctions: dialect = region/group variety; register = situation-coded variety; style = degree of formality within a code; code = the language or distinct system being selected. Special languages (Section 5) are usually registers or codes with an ideology attached.

## 5. Special languages: ritual registers, avoidance registers, anti-languages

### 5.1 Priestly and ritual registers

Structural signature: archaism + formula + parallelism + performative force, often with deliberate incomprehensibility.

- Archaism equals authority: a liturgical code older than the vernacular (Church Latin, Vedic Sanskrit, Quranic Arabic) stays unchanging while the street drifts; "true religion speaks as it always spoke."
- Parallelism: semantic pairs and couplets carry ritual weight (Fox (ed.) 1988, To Speak in Pairs, on eastern Indonesia; Sherzer 1983 on Kuna chant; Kuipers 1990 on Weyewa ritual speech).
- Words as acts: mantra efficacy does not depend on comprehension or paraphrase (Tambiah 1968, Man n.s. 3, on Sinhalese ritual formulae); Staal (1979, Numen 26; 1989, Rules Without Meaning) argues ritual is rule-governed and mantra strings are often syntactically meaningless — form outlives meaning, which is exactly why frozen registers feel numinous.
- Phatic communion (Malinowski 1923, in Ogden & Richards, The Meaning of Meaning): talk that binds rather than informs; Trobriand garden magic (Malinowski 1935, Coral Gardens and Their Magic) as words doing agronomy.
- Silence as the highest register: Quaker worship values silence over fluency, speech only under divine impulse (Bauman 1983, Let Your Words Be Few) — a counter-model where prestige goes to not-speaking.
- Secret and mystic codes: Hildegard of Bingen's Lingua Ignota (c. 1150 glossary in the Riesencodex) as an invented holy vocabulary; the medieval "language of the birds" motif; nushu, the Jiangyong women's script (attested, documented in the 20th century; linguistic study in Chiang 1995); Balaibalan, a constructed sacred language of the 16th-century Ḥurūfī-Sufi milieu, attested by a single dictionary manuscript and attributed either to Fazlallāh Astarābādī's circle or to Muhyî-i Gülşenî (Bausani 1954; Encyclopaedia Iranica).

### 5.2 Avoidance registers ("mother-in-law languages")

The trigger is audience, not addressee: certain taboo relatives (parents-in-law, cross-cousins) may not be near everyday speech, so the speaker switches style while saying the same things — a SPEAKING Participants effect.

- Dyirbal (Dixon 1972, The Dyirbal Language of North Queensland): everyday Guwal vs avoidance Jalnguy — same grammar and phonology, entirely different lexicon; Jalnguy is a semantic superset (one generic term covers several everyday terms), so vagueness is respect.
- Guugu Yimithirr (Haviland 1979, in Shopen (ed.), Languages and Their Speakers): avoidance style used in the presence of the mother-in-law and for sacred topics.
- Lardil Damin (Hale 1982, "The Logic of Damin Kinship Terminology"; phonology in Hale & Nash 1997): ceremonial register whose phonology Hale calls a drastic departure from Lardil's — clicks (elsewhere confined to Africa) plus sounds found in no other Australian language — built on ~150–200 invented roots; reported learnable in weeks because the root stock is small and combinatory.
- Widespread across Aboriginal Australia (Dixon 1980, The Languages of Australia, survey chapter) — the register encodes the kinship diagram, so it couples directly to any kinship file.

Craft conversion: an avoidance register is audible etiquette — the mother-in-law's arrival changes the whole room's speech. Structural recipe: reuse the grammar, swap the lexicon, make avoidance terms more generic, trigger on presence of taboo persons, extend to sacred topics and post-death name taboos.

### 5.3 Thieves' argot and anti-languages

Halliday (1976, American Anthropologist 78, "Anti-languages"): the language of an anti-society (underworld, prison, itinerant trades). Mechanisms:

- Relexicalization: new words in the domains that matter to the group (cons, weapons, police).
- Overlexicalization: many synonyms in salient domains (dozens of cant terms for "gallows" or "prostitute" in early modern cant lists).
- Metaphor and deformation: backslang, rhyming substitution, phonological disguising (Pig Latin, French verlan, Cockney rhyming slang).
- Resocialization: learning the argot IS induction into the anti-society (Maurer 1940, The Big Con, on grifter cant).
- Turnover: argot leaks and decays as it becomes fashionable; constant renewal is a realism detail, and leaked argot losing its secrecy is a plot beat.

Documented corpus to draw on: Harman 1566, A Caveat for Common Cursitors (first English cant list); B.E., c.1698, New Dictionary of the Terms Ancient and Modern of the Canting Crew; Grose 1785, Classical Dictionary of the Vulgar Tongue (the dictionary tradition is traced in Coleman 2004); Spanish germanía, printed cant vocabulary from 1609 (Juan Hidalgo, Romances de germanía de varios autores, Barcelona); Lunfardo, late 19th-century Buenos Aires, preserved in tango lyrics; Polari (fairground, naval, gay men's code; Baker 2002); Grypsera in Polish prisons (Halliday 1976).

### 5.4 Decision table: which special language fits your culture

| Type | Social trigger | Mechanism | Decay/renewal | Signals | Real exemplar |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Liturgical register | Sacred office, calendar | Archaic frozen code | None by design; schism if reformed | Orthodoxy, hierarchy | Church Latin |
| Ritual formula set | Rites of passage, magic | Fixed strings, parallelism | Copyist drift only | Efficacy, lineage | Vedic mantra (Staal 1989) |
| Avoidance register | Presence of taboo kin; sacred topics | Lexicon swap toward generic terms | Stable while kinship system holds | Respect, boundary | Dyirbal Jalnguy (Dixon 1972) |
| Ceremonial invented register | Initiation, secret rites | Invented roots plus exotic phonemes | Slow, deliberately guarded | Initiate status | Lardil Damin (Hale 1982) |
| Argot / anti-language | Criminal or persecuted group | Re-/overlexicalization, deformation | Fast turnover | Insider vs police | Cant, Polari (Halliday 1976; Baker 2002) |
| Ludling (word game) | Play, child culture | Phonological rule applied live | Learned in minutes | Wit, brief secrecy | Verlan, Pig Latin |
| Honorific speech levels | Any ranked dyad | Grammaticalized status morphology | Stable; shifts with politics | Rank, refinement | Javanese krama (Geertz 1966) |

## 6. Applying this to a fictional culture: checklist and calibration flags

1. List 6-10 named speech events and fill the SPEAKING grid for each (Section 1); name the genres in-culture.
2. Place the polity in Fishman's diglossia/bilingualism 2x2 (Section 4) and let the placement predict its instability.
3. Choose at most one or two relativity axes, set the dial 0-3 (Section 2), and write the daily cultural practice that trains the skill — or the displacement that erodes it.
4. Pick 1-3 language ideologies (Section 3) and run Irvine & Gal's three processes over the society's fault lines; give one linguistic feature a second-order stereotype name.
5. Design the register ecology: High/Low diglossia, honorific levels, T/V rules — and date them to the era (print freezes standards, broadcast spreads enregisterment, revolution flips pronouns).
6. Place special languages with explicit triggers using the Section 5.4 table: ritual formulas, an avoidance lexicon for taboo kin, an argot for any anti-society, ludlings for children.
7. Couple to costume: registers license dress and gesture (oath regalia, mourning-silence garb, initiate masks); a speech event without costume flags feels unfinished.
8. Couple to myth: ritual speech needs an origin story (the god's first word, the bird that taught the ancestors); the ideology explains why the old code must not change.
9. Couple to era: writing standardizes and empowers prescriptivism; printing creates the complaint tradition; radio and school enregister accents at national scale.
10. Handoff discipline: deliver systems and method as reference material (this file's job); do not decide canon facts about the shared world — flag contested scope for lore-shokunin instead.

Calibration flags — state these plainly whenever this file is used:

- Strong Whorfianism ("no word, no thought") is discredited; use weak, habit-level effects only (Section 2).
- "X words for Y" statistics are folklore (Pullum 1991); an invented one in-world is a character's ideology, not the world's truth — which is itself a usable device.
- Politeness universals (Brown & Levinson 1987) are contested; treat as a strong tendency.
- The Piraha numeral findings (Gordon 2004; Frank et al. 2008) are debated in scope (see Everett 2005 and later critiques); phrase claims narrowly.
- Damin is thinly documented: cite Hale & Nash 1997 for the phonology, Hale 1982 for the lexicon and kinship use, and attribute the exotic-sound claims rather than asserting them.
- Balaibalan rests on a single dictionary attestation whose authorship is genuinely unsettled (Fazlallāh Astarābādī's circle vs. Muhyî-i Gülşenî — Bausani 1954; Encyclopaedia Iranica): quote the attestation, not a creator.
- Pormpuraaw time-mapping claims rest on small samples; use as texture, not as physics.
