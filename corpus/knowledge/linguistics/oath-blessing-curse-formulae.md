## 1. Scope: the formula grammar this entry owns, and the enforcement it defers

This entry owns the **grammar of the binding utterance** — the words of an oath, a blessing and a curse, and the way those words are assembled so that a hearer can tell what is being done. It owns four things inside that grammar: the slot inventory and the order the slots take; the register system that decides which formula a speaker may use and what it may name; the error patterns that make a designed formula fail to bind; and the design procedure for building a fictional oath or curse system whose force stays tied to its speakers, its witnesses and its breakers.

It explicitly does **not** own the machinery of binding speech, which is owned elsewhere and is named here only as a neighbour:

The two halves of one surface are split on that line and this entry restates none of the machinery (design, not sourced).

## 2. The oath formula: invocation, assertion, self-imprecation, uptake

An **oath formula** is a binding utterance whose assertion is a commitment of the speaker. Its distinguishing property is that the speaker is both the binder and the bound, and every slot of the grammar has to be filled on that basis.

The oath frame in this material is built around the self-curse, and the source states its purpose in one sentence: "the ultimate goal of all these self-curses is, of course, to strike fear into those who swear the oath" (S975).

The Icelandic material gives the oath as a fixed utterance whose wording is prescribed, and the source quotes the words as "at ek vinn eið at bauga, lögeið" (S976).

The oath form in this material is fixed enough to be quoted in a later text as the archetype of the genre, and the source gives the opening as "ἴστω νῦν Ζεὺς πρῶτα θεῶν ὕπατος καὶ ἄριστος" (S977).

The oath form in this material is defined by what it takes with it — the curse is part of the binding — and the source states that "curse-enhanced oaths or vows lie behind the notion of the geis" (S978).

The same account takes the whole family as a single class of performative utterance, and it enumerates the class as "a ritual, spell, curse, charm, incantation, blessing, hymn, or mantra" (S980).

The norito material treats the formula as a text with an internal sectional structure that a practitioner has to know, and the source describes one such division as "divides the Great Purification Norito found in Fascicle 8 of the Engishiki into 10 sections" (S981).

The binding formula in this tradition is a recited text with a set form rather than an improvised speech, and the source calls it "the recital of a norito or formula—not a prayer, as it is sometimes called" (S983).

A designer can take three things from this and nothing else (design, not sourced):

- **The oath formula is a slot-filling discipline.** It is finished when the performative verb, the witness invocation, the assertion, the sanction clause and the uptake are all specified, and when the parties, the occasion and the archive are named. An oath formula with three of the five slots filled will read as a boast, a threat or a prayer, and the reader cannot tell which.
- **The frame is the heritage part; the assertion is the living part.** In practice the performative verb, the invocation formula and the shape of the sanction clause are the oldest and most fixed part of a binding register, while the assertion is what changes with each occasion. A designer who wants the register to feel old writes the frame down and lets the assertion vary.
- **The oath formula and the curse formula share a vocabulary.** A culture with self-imprecation has one grammar that produces both, and a designer who has written one has mostly written the other (design, not sourced).

## 3. The blessing formula: a transfer, not a wish

A **blessing formula** differs from an oath formula in the direction of the transfer: something moves from the speaker to the recipient. The slot inventory is the same, but the assertion carries the object of the gift rather than a promise, and the sanction clause becomes a description of what has been given (design, not sourced).

A semantic and pragmatic account of the same frame treats the blessing as one of a set of ritual utterance types rather than as a kind of assertion, and the source lists them as "a ritual, spell, curse, charm, incantation, blessing, hymn, or mantra" (S980).

The Japanese material insists on the fixedness of the utterance and on its difference from free prayer, and the source marks the distinction in one line: "the recital of a norito or formula—not a prayer, as it is sometimes called" (S983).

Two design points follow (design, not sourced):

- **Irrevocability is the question the blessing formula forces.** A blessing that can be withdrawn by the same words that gave it is a transaction; a blessing that stands is a transfer the culture's law has to police. A designer who wants blessings that matter must decide whether they can be recalled, and must decide it once.
- **The blessing formula needs a licence and a record as much as the oath does.** A blessing given by an unlicensed speaker, or given with no uptake, is not a weaker binding act — it is not a binding act at all, because the culture has no way to know later that it happened.

## 4. The curse formula: condition, target and cause grammar

A **curse formula** differs from an oath formula in two ways the grammar records precisely: the assertion carries a *harm* rather than a promise, and the condition under which the harm falls is usually explicit rather than absorbed. A curse is therefore the utterance whose slots are easiest to fill and whose hardest question is not grammatical but institutional — who is allowed to utter it, and against whom (design, not sourced).

The curse in this material is not a decoration on the oath but the instrument that makes it hold, and the source puts it as "the ultimate goal of all these self-curses is, of course, to strike fear into those who swear the oath" (S975).

The prohibition in this material is not a rule with an attached penalty but a rule whose breach is itself the sanction, and the source describes the relation as "curse-enhanced oaths or vows lie behind the notion of the geis" (S978).

The Irish material in this set treats the curse as a composed genre with formal requirements the performer must meet, and the source counts them as "the seven requirements which compose any satire (cach n-aír) are" (S979).

The design point the curse formula makes sharp is the **cause grammar**. A curse with no stated cause is indistinguishable from an attack, and a culture that cannot say why a curse is legitimate cannot distinguish the curse that should land from the one that should not — which is the distinction a story needs in order to be a story rather than a mood (design, not sourced). The cause grammar may be supplied from kinship, from property, from justice or from office, and the choice is what makes a curse legible to its audience.

## 5. The witness invocation: who is called to hear

The **witness invocation** names the addressee who is called to hear and who is thereby made responsible for the act. It is the slot that decides whether the utterance has anyone behind it.

The witness invocation in this material is a naming act rather than an address, and the source quotes the formula as "at ek vinn eið at bauga, lögeið" (S976).

The Homeric material shows the witness invocation as an address to a named power at the head of the oath, and the source quotes it as "ἴστω νῦν Ζεὺς πρῶτα θεῶν ὕπατος καὶ ἄριστος" (S977).

The addressee and the occasion are fixed by the text itself in this tradition, which is why the source can count the sections of a given formula: "divides the Great Purification Norito found in Fascicle 8 of the Engishiki into 10 sections" (S981).

The Cross River material pairs the witness invocation directly with the enforcement clause, so that the two are one construction, and the source gives the pair as "to witness their truthfulness and punish them if they lied" (S982).

The covenant material in this set shows the witness invocation as borrowed from a wider treaty tradition rather than invented for the occasion, and the source states the borrowing as "Moses in Deuteronomy draws on formulations found in ancient Near Eastern treaties" (S984).

The designer's decision is a **choice of addressee class**, and the classes are a closed set (design, not sourced):

| witness class | what the invocation names | what it gives the utterance | what it costs the design |
|---|---|---|---|
| a named power | a god, a spirit, a deified ancestor, a sacred place | divine enforcement and a sanction nobody can argue with | imports a theology and a priesthood the culture must staff |
| an institution | the assembly, the court, the guild, the archive, the lineage | a legible sanction and a datable record | imports an institution with real power over the parties |
| a person present | a bystander named, addressed, or made to answer | a witness who can later be cross-examined | imports a person who can refuse, and a dispute about refusal |
| the speaker's own body | the speaker's health, kin, eyes, life | an oath enforceable with no institution at all | makes ordinary promises expensive and the speaker's family a hostage |
| a generic or empty addressee | "by whatever watches", an unspecified power | deniability for the speaker, unattributability for the curse | the utterance has no force; it binds nobody |

The fifth class is where the design breaks most often, and §9 gives the test.

## 6. The sanction clause: the conditional consequence

The **sanction clause** names the consequence and the condition under which it applies, and it is the slot that decides whether the utterance is a contract, a threat or a prayer.

The sanction clause in this tradition is aimed at the community rather than at the hearer, and the source describes the self-curse as a public instrument: "the ultimate goal of all these self-curses is, of course, to strike fear into those who swear the oath" (S975).

The sanction in this material is a matter of whether the target has the required status, and the source states the requirement as "the seven requirements which compose any satire (cach n-aír) are" (S979).

The sanction clause in the same account is read as part of the performative content rather than as an appended condition, so the class it belongs to is the one it "a ritual, spell, curse, charm, incantation, blessing, hymn, or mantra" (S980).

The sanction clause in this material is what the witness is called for, and the source states the pairing as "to witness their truthfulness and punish them if they lied" (S982).

The sanction clause in this material is the clause the witness is invoked to enforce, and the source ties the two together as "Moses in Deuteronomy draws on formulations found in ancient Near Eastern treaties" (S984).

Its grammatical options are a closed set, and the choice between them is a design decision with social consequences (design, not sourced):

| sanction shape | the act it performs | what it gives the culture | what it costs |
|---|---|---|---|
| conditional consequence | "if I fail, then X happens to me" | the most legible pairing of promise and penalty | needs a sanction the culture already believes is credible |
| enumerated penalty | a list of specific harms, ranked | the most dramatic and the most quotable | a long list invites a scene-by-scene reading and a long formula |
| transferred penalty | the consequence falls on the speaker's household or line | a sanction that reaches beyond the speaker and therefore bites | the most socially expensive; it turns the utterance into a weapon aimed at the speaker's own people |
| open target | "whoever does this, to whoever does this" | a standing condition that outlives the utterance | needs an executor able to act on a stranger's act |
| comparative escalation | an additive tail on the penalty | cheap, quick, and rankable | reads as a flourish unless the culture's law grades oaths by rank |

## 7. Registers: sacred, legal and everyday

The same slots appear in three registers, and the register decides what the slots may contain and who may fill them. The register law itself is owned by `linguistics/register-strata-attestation.md` and the sacred high variety by `linguistics/sacred-registers-liturgical-language.md`; this section states only the assignment of the binding formula to a register (design, not sourced).

| register | who may use the formula | what the assertion may name | what the witness invocation may name | what the uptake is | what the register costs |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **sacred** | a licensed office, or a person under a vow | what the tradition permits; the personal is usually excluded | a named power, an ancestor, a place | a recitation, a rite, a deposit | the most expensive to maintain, because the office that licenses it must be trained |
| **legal** | a party, an advocate, a witness-officer | terms, obligations, ownership, testimony | the court, the assembly, the archive, the record | a filing, a seal, an entry | the most exposed to contestation, because a legal formula is read adversarially |
| **everyday** | anyone, within limits of relationship | small promises, thanks, mild wishes, greetings | the addressee, a bystander, nobody in particular | a nod, a reply | the cheapest to run, and the register in which a binding formula loses its force |

Three design rules follow (design, not sourced):

- **A formula that appears in all three registers at once has lost its force.** The everyday register is where binding words wear out. A designer who wants a curse to be terrible keeps it out of the everyday register and pays for that with a ritual cost.
- **Moving a formula down a register changes its grammar.** A legal formula lifted into the everyday register loses its sanction clause; a sacred formula spoken casually loses its uptake. The slots are what make this visible, and they are why the grammar is worth designing before the words are.
- **The register is a licence, not a tone.** "Sacred" means that a specific office may use the formula under specific conditions; "everyday" means that the formula is available to anyone but with a narrow assertion range. Register assignment is a table of permissions, and the table is the design artefact.

## 8. Master grammar table: the slots and what each one does

This table is the entry's analytic apparatus applied to the sources, and every cell is a design statement about a fictional binding utterance. The left column names the slots of the grammar; the right column names what a designer must decide about each (design, not sourced).

| part | function | what the attested forms show | design question | failure mode when empty |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **performative verb** | carries the act rather than describing it | the act is named by the formula that makes it, and a culture may mark it with a word, a construction or a gesture | is the act a single lexeme, a verb plus participle, a clause-level performative, or an overt mark? | the utterance becomes a statement about an act rather than the act |
| **witness invocation** | names the addressee who is called to hear | the invocation names a listener who is made responsible, and the family's own loss is part of what the hearing is for | which addressee class is available: a power, an institution, a person, the body, or nobody? | the oath binds nobody and the curse has no witness |
| **assertion** | states what is sworn, blessed or cursed | the terms of the act, the gift, or the harm named | is the content a promise, a gift, a harm, or a standing condition? | the formula is a frame with nothing in it |
| **sanction clause** | names the conditional consequence | a paired promise and penalty, or a shorter penalty appended to a promise, with the penalty often aimed at the speaker | is the penalty conditional, enumerated, transferred, open-target or escalating? | the utterance is an intention, and an intention is not binding |
| **self-imprecation** | turns the sanction on the speaker | the speaker names the penalty against themselves and invokes a power to enforce it | is the self-imprecation always present, sometimes present, or absent? | the formula is a threat, and a threat can be ignored |
| **uptake** | makes the act an event rather than a speech | the answer, the gesture, the record or the deposited object that lets the act be witnessed and dated | does the uptake leave evidence that can survive the ceremony? | nothing is dated, so nothing can later be found or disputed |

## 9. Error patterns: oaths that bind nobody, curses with no witness

The slot grammar makes these failures checkable, because each error is a missing or misplaced part (design, not sourced):

**1. The oath that binds nobody.** An oath with no witness invocation, no sanction clause and no uptake is a statement of intention. The test: ask who will act when the promise is broken. If the answer is "nobody, it will just be known", the oath has no force and the scene will not land.

**2. The curse with no witness.** A curse that names a harm but no power to enforce it reads as a threat, and a threat is a thing that can be ignored. The test: ask whether the culture believes anyone will act. If the answer is "only the speaker's anger", the curse is a mood, and a mood cannot carry a plot.

**3. The self-imprecation without a target.** An escalating penalty with no named referent is a figure of speech, not a sanction. The test: ask who the tail refers to. If the answer is "and worse", it is nothing.

**4. The empty addressee.** "By whatever watches" is a real design choice with a real cost: it makes the oath deniable and the curse unattributable. It is fine in a culture of rumour and fatal in a culture of archives. The test: ask whether the wording is chosen to be deniable, and whether the setting can afford that.

**5. Register leakage.** A binding formula used casually stops being binding, and the loss is invisible until the moment the scene needs it. The test: ask whether the character using the formula would be marked as ritually improper by their community. If the culture would shrug, the formula has no force left.

**6. The transferred penalty used as a threat.** When the penalty falls on the speaker's household rather than on the speaker, the utterance stops being a promise and becomes an attack on the people the speaker loves. The test: ask who the reader is supposed to fear. If it is the speaker's own kin, the formula is a curse wearing an oath's clothes.

**7. The formula that binds the wrong party.** A frame filled with an assertion about somebody else's obligation, uttered by somebody with no standing, is a category error the audience catches before the story does. The test: ask who has the authority to bind this person, in this register.

**8. The uptake that never happens.** An oath with no answer, no record and no object is a speech act that never became an event, so nothing can later be dated, found or disputed. The test: ask what evidence will survive the ceremony.

**9. The open target with no executor.** "Whoever disturbs this grave" makes every future person a target, which is only workable if something can act on a stranger's act. The test: ask who acts.

**10. The frozen frame with a live assertion.** When the frame is treated as rigid and the assertion as the only variable, the register becomes a form and the words stop meaning what they say. The test: ask whether the same assertion would be understood outside the frame. If not, the culture has a ritual and not a language.

## 10. Design procedure and checklist for a fictional oath or curse system

This section is the operating procedure for the table above, and it is a design instrument and nothing else (design, not sourced).

**Before writing a single word of the formula (design, not sourced):**

1. Fix the register each formula belongs to, and write down the licence rule that decides who may use it (design, not sourced).
2. Fix the witness class — a power, an institution, a person, the body, or nobody — and staff the institution it implies (design, not sourced).
3. Fix the performative shape, and if you choose a particle or a gesture, fix who is licensed to use it (design, not sourced).
4. Fix the slot inventory — performative verb, witness invocation, assertion, sanction clause, self-imprecation, uptake — and decide which may be empty in which register (design, not sourced).
5. Fix the shape of the sanction, and whether the penalty falls on the speaker, on their household, or on an open target (design, not sourced).
6. Decide whether the self-imprecation is present, and if it is present, what it names (design, not sourced).

**While drafting (design, not sourced):**

7. Write the frame before the assertion; the frame should be the oldest-sounding part (design, not sourced).
8. Write the uptake as a concrete act with a physical form, and give it a place and a time (design, not sourced).
9. Write the everyday-register variant last, and check that the binding force has not leaked into it (design, not sourced).
10. Check every formula against the error patterns of §9, in particular against the oath that binds nobody and the curse with no witness (design, not sourced).
11. Make the frame distinguishable across the three registers by at least one slot, so a listener can tell which register a formula belongs to (design, not sourced).

**Before the design is declared done (design, not sourced):**

12. Check the whole system against the sibling registers in the corpus: a binding system that reproduces the culture's liturgical, legal or everyday logic wholesale has been copied rather than designed (design, not sourced).
13. Check that every formula has a dated, discoverable uptake, so that later characters can find it and argue about it (design, not sourced).
14. Check that the release question has an answer, and that the answer is the same for the oath and the curse or is deliberately different (design, not sourced).
15. Record the non-canon status and the handoff: what is decided here, what is canon, and what is left open for the world bible (design, not sourced).

**A worked micro-example (NON-CANON).** A salt-trading people on a brackish river swear by the tide. Their oaths are sacred-register only: a licensed *tolo* recites the frame, the witness invocation names the river, the assertion names the salt and the season, and the sanction clause is a transferred penalty that falls on the household if the salt is short-weighted. The everyday register has no binding formula at all, so a promise made at the market table is a promise with no force — which is exactly why the *tolo* is paid to be present at the weighing. The design consequence is that the culture's ordinary trust is institutional, and its binding words are a specialist's craft. Everything else about these people — the language, the offices, the doctrine, the history — is a canon decision for lore-shokunin and is not made here (design, not sourced).

## 11. Limits, calibration and the handoff to the lore world bible

**The base is small and should be read as small.** The sourced material in this entry is a small number of opened sources, each scoped to what its own captured span supports; none of them is a survey of binding formulae across traditions, and this entry does not generalise any of them into a universal grammar of oaths (design, not sourced).

**Calibration.** Where a source reports a practice in one tradition, this entry records it as a report about that tradition and as a *design option* for a fictional one. The six-slot grammar of §8, the three-register division of §7 and the ten-row decision procedure of §10 are this entry's own analytic apparatus applied to the reports; they are not a claim that every tradition organises a binding utterance under those slots (design, not sourced). Where a source reports a scholarly view rather than a settled fact, this entry records the view as the source's and does not promote it (design, not sourced).

**No canon, no rights ruling.** This entry makes no claim about what is true in any world, no ruling on the use of any real community's binding practice, and no judgement about who may adopt it. Whether a particular real-world oath, blessing or curse practice may be used by a fictional people is a decision for a canon layer and, where rights are involved, for a rights process; this entry records design surfaces only (design, not sourced).

**Handoff to the lore world bible.** This entry hands the **fictional binding-speech system** to the world bible as a *surface*, not as canon. What is handed over: the slot inventory and the frame rules of §8 as a construction procedure; the register table of §7 as a set of permissions; the error patterns of §9 as failure modes to avoid; and the checklist of §10 as a procedure a writer can run. What the lore layer must decide and this entry does not: the names of the powers, the offices, the archives, the languages and the history of any invented people; the theology that says what the words do; and the canon of every binding utterance that appears in the fiction (design, not sourced). Kotodama records the surface and the method; it does not record what is true (design, not sourced).
