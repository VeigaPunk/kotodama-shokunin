## 2. Five bathing regimes — a comparison table to fill per culture

These are not a progress ladder; each answers a different question (public sociability? ritual purity? domestic necessity? therapeutic heat?) with different infrastructure.

| Regime | Sequence | Heat and water | Staff | Social function | Purity relation |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Roman thermae / balnea | undress (*apodyterium*) → warm (*tepidarium*) → hot (*caldarium*) → steam (*sudatorium/laconicum*) → cold plunge (*frigidarium*) → exercise/rest | hypocaust under floors and wall flues; aqueduct or cistern supply; oil + strigil, **not soap** | attendants, masseurs, bath-sandals guards; often slaves or freed staff | the daily social institution — business, patronage, gossip, display (Seneca *Ep.* 56 complains of the noise) | none required: thermae are *hygiene and leisure*, not ritual |
| Islamic hammam | undress room → warm room → hot room (steam) → washing and massage → cool room → rest | hypocaust-derived underfloor heating, domes with star-shaped light holes; water carried or piped | bath attendant (*tellak*), washer, keeper; the bath as a funded institution (*waqf*) | neighbourhood sociability, celebration (wedding baths), a literary topos | **adjacent but distinct**: *wuḍūʾ* (ablution before prayer) and *ghusl* (full washing) are ritual acts that may happen at home; the hammam is the social venue |
| East Asian sentō / onsen | wash at the tap-line or with a bucket → soak in the hot pool (→ rinse, cool) | fuel-heated public bath (Edo-era towns; firewood and ash economy) or volcanic hot spring; wood-fired or geothermal | the bathhouse operator and family; the water-carrier/hauler where there is no piped supply | the neighbourhood bath as daily institution; the spring resort as therapy and travel | springs carry emic therapeutic doctrine (bathing cures); the soak is pleasure and health, not law |
| Sweat-lodge family (Mesoamerican *temazcalli*, North American sweat lodge, Finnish sauna, Russian banya) | heat the room (stones/stove) → steam → cool plunge or rest | heated stones and steam; the stove (Finnish *kiuas*), the besom (*venik*), the birch whisk (*vihta*) | a keeper who manages heat and steam; in Mesoamerica the midwife for birth use | health, birth, mourning, sociability — a hot room at life-crises | often **ritually marked**; some practices are restricted and permission-bound (see §9) |
| Domestic / personal washing | basin, ewer, sponge, cloth; the bath-tub for the wealthy | heated in the kitchen or on the hearth; carried from well, cistern or river | household labor; in cities the professional water-carrier | privacy and modesty; the bath as luxury or as drudgery | washing as private devotion (ablution at home) |

- **Design lever — pick the regime by the question your culture asks of water.** A culture whose washing is *ritual* builds immersion (still water, a plunge, a river); one whose washing is *social* builds a steam room and a lounge; one whose washing is *hygiene* builds plumbing. Two regimes coexist easily (public bath + private ablution); three need institutions to explain the redundancy.
- **The fuel line is the hidden cost.** Every hot bath burns something: wood, charcoal, dung, peat, geothermal heat. The bath's fuel demand is a landscape institution (who owns the woodlot; what the bath costs in winter) and is often more socially consequential than the bathing itself.

## 3. The bath sequence as designed space

- The universal shape is a **gradient**: cold → warm → hot → cool, with an undress/rest stage at each end. Fill one row per culture; the empty cells are the design work.

| Stage | What it must do | What it demands | Failure if omitted |
|---|---|---|---|
| undress / store | hold clothes and valuables safely; establish who may be present | lockers, hooks, a guarded room; a nudity norm | theft, embarrassment, a scene that breaks the culture's own rules |
| warm | adapt the body; social buffer | a moderately heated room | shock; a scene with no transition |
| hot / steam | open pores, sweat, the core of the institution | heat source (hypocaust, stove, stones), steam water, ventilation | smoke, burns, collapse — real bath-house hazards |
| cool / plunge | close the sequence; the contrast that makes the heat feel like something | cold water (pool, river, snow), a towel | the bath reads as incomplete to anyone raised in the culture |
| rest / dress / socialize | the room where the institution actually lives | benches, drink, time | a bath that is only plumbing, not a society |

- **Architecture carries the story.** The hammam's dome with pierced star openings (light and steam in one device), the Roman vaulted hall, the Japanese bathhouse's painted backdrop (the famous Mount Fuji mural convention, standard by the late Meiji/Taishō period — **to-verify**), the sauna's windowless dark: each is a different answer to "what should a person look at while hot?".
- **The staff make it an institution.** Attendant, masseur, washer, fuel-carrier, water-carrier, towel-keeper. Give at least two a title and a ladder (couples `craft-artisan-lexicon.md`) or the bath is furniture.

## 4. Purity vs hygiene — the load-bearing distinction

- **They are different systems and must not be merged.** Ritual purity is about *status* (who or what is fit for a sacred act); hygiene is about *contamination* (what makes a body sick). Mary Douglas's *Purity and Danger* (1966) is the standard statement that purity rules are classificatory rather than hygienic; Milgrom's reading of Leviticus is the main rival account — the corpus already runs this exact calibration pair for dietary law (`corpus/knowledge/culture-material/cuisine-food-lexicon.md`), and the same discipline applies to washing.
- **The ritual-immersion family**: the Jewish *mikveh* (a constructed immersion pool with a minimum volume — the 40 *se'ah* rule, Mishnah *Mikvaot* — and rules about what makes water fit); Islamic *ghusl* and *wuḍūʾ*; Christian baptism; river bathing at confluences (the corpus's existing Kumbh Mela and *petirtaan* rows, `corpus/knowledge/myth-symbol/ritual-liminality.md:86`, `corpus/knowledge/civilizations/java-majapahit.md:146`); Vedic bathing before status change (`corpus/knowledge/civilizations/india-vedic.md:126`); the high priest's bathing in Leviticus 16 (`corpus/knowledge/civilizations/israel-ancient-hebrew.md:138`).
- **The leaks are the design work.** A purity bath that also cleans; a hygienic bath that acquires ritual force (the modern shower as a moral act); a purity rule that forbids the very water hygiene needs. Write the leak deliberately and give it an institution (a priest who inspects, a doctor who scoffs) — that is a scene engine, not a footnote.
- **Design rule:** state separately (a) what the culture considers *unclean*, (b) what it considers *unsanitary*, and (c) which institutions own each. If (a) and (b) are identical, the culture is a modern hygiene state wearing period clothes; if they are unrelated, the culture has two bodies of law and therefore two kinds of expert.

## 5. The infrastructure decision — water, heat, drainage, payment

- **Water source:** river (free, unbounded, dangerous, ritualized), well (shared, local, rationed by queue), cistern (rain-fed, private, seasonal), spring (sacred and/or therapeutic), aqueduct (engineered, political, taxed), water-carrier trade (labor turned into a commodity — the medieval and early-modern city's answer). The source decides the *distance* a person travels to wash, and distance decides frequency.
- **Heat:** hypocaust (underfloor flues, fuel-hungry, stone-built), stove (banya/sauna, wood-fired), kitchen-hearth heating of water carried in vessels (the domestic default), solar heating (the poor and the lucky), geothermal (onsen: geology as infrastructure).
- **Drainage:** bath water must go somewhere. Latrines, sewers, cesspits, and the street are all real answers; the Roman *forica* and the sponge-on-a-stick, the night-soil trade, and the urban stench are the material consequences. Without a drain decision, a fictional city has baths that fill with water and never empty.
- **Payment and ownership:** private household; the bath as a **benefaction** (a rich patron builds it for civic prestige — the Roman evergetism pattern); the bath as a **funded charitable institution** (the Islamic *waqf* pattern); the bath as a **taxed service** (admission fee, fuel tax — Vespasian's urine tax on the fullers' collection, Suetonius *Vesp.* 23, is the classic joke with a real economy behind it); the bath as a **public-health service** (municipal baths and wash-houses, §8).
- **Decision table:**

| Question | Options | What it predicts in scenes |
|---|---|---|
| Where is the water? | river / well / cistern / spring / aqueduct / carried | who carries it, how far, how often, who queues |
| What burns? | wood / charcoal / dung / peat / geothermal / nothing | the bath's cost curve; winter closures; a fuel politics |
| Where does it drain? | sewer / cesspit / street / field | disease geography; the smell map of the city |
| Who pays? | household / patron / waqf / fee / tax / state | who may enter, and who is turned away |
| Who is admitted? | everyone / by class / by gender / by purity status / by membership | the bath as an arena of rank and exclusion |

## 6. Who bathes, where, when — privacy, gender, schedule, sociability

- **Privacy and gender regimes** run from fully mixed to strictly separated, and the *rule's form* matters: separate hours, separate buildings, separate rooms, screens, or nothing. Real anchors: Roman mixed bathing with moralizing commentary and at least one imperial ban (**to-verify**, *Historia Augusta*); hammam gender separation by hours or by separate establishments; Japanese mixed bathing, then bakufu-era restrictions and later separated entrances (**to-verify**); monastic and ascetic washing regimes; the *mikveh*'s rules about who may immerse when.
- **Schedule** is an institution: daily (a Roman habit for those with leisure), weekly (the "Saturday bath" pattern, the eve-of-festival bath), seasonal (spring cleaning, the year-end wash), and life-crisis (birth, marriage, death, pilgrimage). The schedule is where a calendar and a bath couple (couples `calendar-festival-systems.md`).
- **Sociability and its dark side.** The bath is a place for business, patronage, courtship, gossip, and status display; it is also a place where the powerless work (attendants, carriers) and where a body is exposed to inspection. Both are real and both are writable; do not write the bath as only one of them.
- **The bath as gift and as display.** Patrons build baths; hosts offer a bath; rulers use baths as a benefaction. The private bath is a sumptuary object (who has hot water at home is a rank question; couples `sumptuary-law-rank-display.md`).

## 7. The kit, its trade, and its vocabulary

- **Soap** is chemistry before it is a commodity: alkali (wood-ash lye, later barilla soda) plus fat or oil → soap by saponification. Name the alkali source and the oil source and the culture's soap is already characterized: olive-oil + soda (the Castile/Marseille family), laurel-oil (Aleppo), animal tallow (northern Europe), and the 19th-century industrial shift when cheap soda (the Leblanc process, 1791 — **to-verify**) made soap a mass product with a marketing apparatus attached (branded soap, ~1880s Britain — **to-verify**).
- **The non-soap kit** matters more in most of history: oil + strigil (Greek and Roman — scrape, don't lather), sand or ash as abrasive, the whisk/besom (*venik*, *vihta*), the loofah and the scrub cloth, the sponge, and the towel (couples `textile-technology.md`). Each item is a trade and a word.
- **The containers** (couples `vessel-container-lexicon.md`): the basin, the ewer, the bucket, the tub, the cauldron; the bath's small-ware is where a culture's pottery and metalware show their cheapest and most-used forms.

## 8. Health, medicine and the hygiene revolution

- **Emic doctrine first.** Bathing as *therapy* is old and cross-cultural: the hot spring as cure (the *onsen*'s therapeutic doctrine; European "taking the waters"), the sweat bath at birth (the *temazcalli*), the steam room as a remedy for colds and joints. Label this as emic doctrine (it is a system of belief about bodies), then let the culture's own practitioners argue.
- **The evidence spine, dated:** Semmelweis's 1847 handwashing intervention in the maternity clinic — a measured mortality drop with no accepted theory to explain it (Semmelweis 1861) — and John Snow's 1854 Broad Street investigation (Snow 1855) are the two cases that separate "washing works" from "why washing works". The germ theory (1860s–80s) arrives *after* the evidence.
- **The public-bath movement.** The 19th-century municipal bath and wash-house (Britain's Public Baths and Wash-houses Acts, 1846/1847 — **to-verify**) is hygiene as *state infrastructure*: the wash-house half is the more consequential (laundry as women's labor and as disease control), and it is where a fictional industrial city's public health story should begin.
- **The "cleanliness revolution" is a dated, uneven process** (Vigarello; Ward — **to-verify**), not a switch. Water supply, soap price, fuel price, housing density and fashion all move independently; a culture can be clean in one register (ritual immersion) and filthy in another (no laundry), and that combination is the historically normal one.

## 9. Calibration — dated myths, restricted practices, and over-claims

- **"Romans were clean"** is true of the *bath-city* and misleading as a universal: thermae were urban, expensive to run, class-stratified, and dependent on fuel and water systems that most of the empire's population did not have. Use the institution, not the slogan.
- **"Medieval people never washed"** is a 19th-century slander with a dated history behind it: public bathhouses, steam baths, hand-washing rituals and the "clean linen" culture all exist in the medieval record, and the *change* in washing habits is a slow early-modern and modern story (Vigarello; Ashenburg — the latter **trade/popular**, flagged). Do not write the "great unwashed Middle Ages" as fact.
- **Mixed-bathing bans and separations are dated legal acts, not timeless customs** (Hadrian's ban, Edo-era restrictions, later municipal rules — all **to-verify** before printing). A culture's gender regime should be given a *date and an enforcer*, not a vibe.

A world can have a perfect calendar and no clock; that gap is what this entry fills.
- **Method statement** (house rule, mirrors the partition): everything below is reference knowledge and craft method. Nothing here decides what is true in any world; the handoff is at §11.
- **Why it is load-bearing for fiction.** A timekeeping device is the cheapest way to make an institution visible: whoever owns the bell owns the day. Scenes that need it are common — the gate that closes at the curfew bell, the trial timed by water, the watch change, the wages paid by the hour, the messenger who arrives "late" against a standard the culture only just invented.

## 2. The five instrument families — resolution, failure mode, archaeological trace

Before the escapement, every culture that measured the day did it with one of five families. They are not a ladder of progress: each answers a different question (daylight? indoors? at night? at sea?) and each fails differently. Fill one row per culture; the empty cells are the design work.

| Family | How it works | Attested forms | Resolution | Fails when | Leaves in the ground |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| SHADOW | a cast shadow reads the sun's altitude/azimuth | gnomon (Babylonian, Egyptian; Herodotus 2.109 has the gnomon and polos arriving from Babylon), obelisk-as-sundial (Pliny NH 36.71–72, the Campus Martius obelisk; the "Horologium Augusti" reconstruction is modern scholarship — **to-verify**), the sundial families Vitruvius 9.8 catalogues (hemicyclium, scaphē, conical, portable diptych dials) | quarter-hour to minutes on a large, well-aligned dial; season-dependent on small ones | night, cloud, and any latitude the dial was not cut for | inscribed stone and bronze dials; alignment marks; foundation sockets |
| FLOW | a measured quantity of water (or sand) passes a marked scale | outflow and inflow clepsydra (Vitruvius 9.8.4–7 describes the float-and-pointer mechanism); the Athenian court water-clock that limited speaking time (Aristotle *Ath. Pol.* 67.2 — subsection **to-verify**); Chinese *lou* 漏 with multi-vessel constant-head systems (Needham vol. 3); Islamic *mīqāt* tables | minutes to tens of minutes; better with a constant-head tank | freezing, evaporation, sediment, temperature/viscosity drift, and an unreplenished head | stone/bronze basins, inscribed scales, float chambers; the wooden parts decay |
| FIRE | a known burn rate of a consumable measures elapsed time | calibrated candles (the Asser candle-lantern story, ch. 104 — legend-grade, §8), oil lamps with graduated reservoirs, incense sticks and seal-stamps (Bedini 1994), rope fuse | coarse: tens of minutes; fine for "how long has this burned" | draft, humidity, fuel quality, and the fact that it is consumed | almost nothing: ash, lamp metal, holder ceramics — the *consumable* is the timekeeper |
| SKY | a star's position or transit marks the night hour | Egyptian diagonal star clocks and Ramesside star clocks (decans; Neugebauer & Parker 1960–69, cited in `celestial-symbolism-calendars.md:83`), the merkhet sighting instrument, the planispheric astrolabe, the nocturnal | tens of minutes at night | cloud; and it needs a trained reader | inscribed tables (coffin lids, almanacs), astrolabe brass; the skill itself leaves nothing |
| WHEEL | an oscillator is counted by an escapement | verge-and-foliot tower clocks (13th–14th c. Europe), pendulum (Huygens 1656), balance spring (Hooke/Huygens 1675), marine chronometer (Harrison, 1735–1761; Longitude Act 1714), quartz (1927), caesium standard (1955/1967 definition) | minute → second → microsecond; the only family that keeps time *unattended* | wear, temperature, and — before the marine chronometer — the sea | bronze/brass gears, dial plates, weights; wooden wheels and cordage decay |

- **Design lever — pick the family by the question, not by era.** A mountain temple with a big south-facing wall and no metals wants SHADOW; a river city with a palace and a court system wants FLOW (a legal timer); a monastery that must wake before dawn wants SKY plus FIRE; a maritime power needs WHEEL (the chronometer is a longitude instrument before it is a watch). Two families per culture is plenty; three reads as technology-collecting unless the culture's institutions explain it.
- **The constant-head rule (FLOW's whole engineering).** A clepsydra measures *elapsed time* only if the driving head is constant; hence the overflow tank, the multi-vessel Chinese system, and the float-and-pointer. A culture that gets this wrong has a clock that runs fast when full and slow when nearly empty — which is itself excellent scene material (the trial that ends early, the watch that drags). Water clocks were institution-grade devices: precise enough to divide a court day, useless for a wage hour.

## 3. Variable hours vs equal hours — the biggest single decision

- **Temporal (unequal) hours.** Rome and Greece divided daylight into twelve hours from sunrise to sunset and night into watches (Roman *vigiliae*, four; Greek *phylakai*). An "hour" therefore lengthened and shortened with the season and the latitude, and a "winter hour" was a different quantity from a "summer hour". The hour was a *position in the day*, not a duration.
- **The equal hour is an institution, not nature.** Equal hours become cheap only when a device can count them (WHEEL), or when an administration must pay by duration. Once wages, contracts, astronomy and navigation need durations, the equal hour wins — but it wins *socially*, and the older regime can persist for centuries beside it.
- **Anchors for the alternative regimes.** Monastic hours name segments rather than measure them (matins, lauds, prime, terce, sext, none, vespers, compline; *Regula Benedicti* cc. 8–18 — chapter division **to-verify**); Islamic prayer times are computed from shadow lengths, which is why the mosque timekeeper (*muwaqqit*) is an astronomical office (King 2004); Edo Japan ran seasonal day/night divisions with adjustable clock faces, a variable-hour mechanical regime (**to-verify** for the specific wadokei claims and dates); Italian hours counted 1–24 from sunset in some regions into the 19th century (**to-verify**). The corpus already holds the label-side precedent for such a system at `corpus/knowledge/civilizations/aksum-ethiopia.md:98-114`.
- **Design lever — choose which quantity your culture can talk about.** A variable-hour culture can say "the third hour" and mean *a place in the day's shape* (dawn, market, heat, dusk) — good for ritual, farming, and scenes that must feel pre-modern. An equal-hour culture can say "three hours" and mean *a duration* — necessary for wage labor, artillery timing, navigation, and factory discipline. Write the culture's idioms from its regime: a variable-hour culture does not "have an hour to spare", it has "until the light fails".
- **The failure to avoid:** mixing regimes silently. If a scene has a temple bell marking variable hours *and* a contract paying by the hour, say who owns the equal-hour device — usually the employer or the state, which is exactly the political point.

## 4. The unit ladder — resolution becomes vocabulary

The instrument's resolution sets the smallest unit the culture can *name*, and the named unit sets what its institutions can *do*. Time units are inherited, not invented ad hoc: the 60-based subdivision of the hour descends from Babylonian sexagesimal astronomy (`corpus/knowledge/linguistics/numerals-and-measures.md:135`), giving *pars minuta prima* → minute and *pars minuta secunda* → second (etymology legs **to-verify**).

| Rung | Device that reaches it | What it enables | What it cannot do |
|---|---|---|---|
| the day (and its named segments) | no device: sun, cockcrow, star, meal | ritual, agriculture, curfew, market opening | anything contractual |
| the hour (variable) | gnomon, sundial, bell | courts, watches, offices, prayer | duration contracts |
| the hour (equal) | clepsydra + bell, early tower clock | wages by the hour, timetables, standard curfew | navigation, science |
| the minute | escapement clock, pendulum | medical dosing, meeting times, precise military orders | longitude at sea |
| the second | pendulum/balance clock, chronometer | astronomy, navigation, physics, industry | — |
| sub-second | quartz, atomic | modern metrology, telecom, geodesy | — |

- **Design lever — do not give a culture units it cannot keep.** A sundial culture with "seconds" is a defect; a culture with a first-class *bell* vocabulary but no minute word is coherent and vivid. Name the smallest unit and the largest unit that device can be trusted for, then let idiom follow ("in a breath", "by the second bell", "before the lamps are lit").
- **Words the instruments generate** (each etymology **to-verify** this round; OED not consulted): *clock* (from a word for **bell**, medieval Latin *clocca* — the machine is named after its bell); *o'clock* (< "of the clock", i.e. by the bell-scale); *watch* (the wake-keeping shift, then the device); *hour* (Greek *hōra* "season, time"); *curfew* (< Old French *couvre-feu* "cover the fire"); *alarm* (Italian *all'arme* "to arms"). The lexical layer is a contact receipt: a culture that borrows the *device word* before the device arrives has a story in its lexicon.
- **The "who says the time" problem.** Once an institution owns a device, its reading becomes the official one: the temple's bell, the state's tower, the employer's clock. In-world disagreements about the correct hour are therefore political, not astronomical — and they are free plot.

## 5. Institutions a time device generates

| Institution | What it is | Real anchor |
|---|---|---|
| bell and tower | public time made audible and visible from a single point | European town clocks; the belfry/campanile as a building type; the tower vocabulary lives in `architecture-settlement-lexicon.md:90` |
| curfew | a legal boundary on the day, enforced by a signal | *couvre-feu*; night as a legal category |
| market and work bells | opening, closing, and the rhythm of labor | town and guild bells; hour-wages (Thompson 1967 — DOI **to-verify**) |
| night watch | a human clock: the watchman calls the hours and raises alarms | Roman *vigiliae*; the town watch; the night-watch institution appears once in the corpus (`grep -ril "night watch"` → 1 file) |
| the divine office | named hours kept by a body of people, not by a machine | *Regula Benedicti*; Islamic prayer times and the *muwaqqit* (King 2004) |
| the timekeeper's office | a paid specialist who owns the device's reading | muwaqqit; the horologer; the Astronomer Royal (Greenwich, 1675) |
| the clockmaker's guild | instrument-making as a closed trade with a masterpiece and a mark | guild ladders and maker's marks in `craft-artisan-lexicon.md`; a signed dial is a signature case there |
| clock diplomacy | a clock given to a foreign court as a prestige object | European clocks at the Ming/Qing and Tokugawa courts (**to-verify**; Needham vol. 4 pt 2 chapter-level) |

- **Design lever — every culture should have exactly one *public* time source and name it.** The tower, the bell, the mosque, the drum, the signal gun. Then decide what happens when a second one appears (a rival temple's bell, a merchant's clock): that is a schism scene with no magic in it.
- **The night is the interesting half.** Instruments that work only in daylight (SHADOW) hand the night to people (watches, vigils) or to fire and sky. A culture's night institutions — curfew, watch, vigil, night market — follow directly from which family it owns.

## 6. Ownership: temple, palace, city, state, employer

- The load-bearing question is not "what does the device measure" but **who owns its reading**. Four stable answers, each with a different fiction:
  - **temple/priesthood** — time is sacred; the bell announces rite, and the calendar is doctrine (couples `calendar-festival-systems.md` on doctrinal intercalation).
  - **palace/court** — time is prestige; clocks are gifts and the ruler's day structures everyone else's (clock diplomacy, §5).
  - **city/commune** — time is civic; the tower clock is paid for by the town and is a point of civic pride and rivalry.
  - **state/employer** — time is labor; the time-book, the fine for lateness, the factory gate (Thompson 1967; the "knocker-upper" as a paid human alarm in industrial Britain — **to-verify** detail).
- **Design lever:** ownership predicts the *scenes* that can exist. A temple-owned bell cannot be moved for a wedding; a city-owned bell can be argued about in council; an employer's clock can be falsified in a strike scene. Pick one and let the conflicts write themselves.

## 7. What a timekeeping device leaves behind — the archaeology and the inference

- **Survival bias is severe.** Metal gears, brass dials, inscribed stone dials, bronze basins and coffin-lid star tables survive; wooden wheels, ropes, candles, incense and the *skill of reading a nocturnal* do not (formation processes; `material-culture-inference-method.md`). A world whose excavators find a gear train but no wooden frame has a genuine interpretive problem — which is exactly what in-world antiquarians should argue about.
- **Ladder tags** (using the partition's canon tags from `material-culture-inference-method.md` §2): an excavated dial or gear train is **HARD** (L1/L3: material, form, technique); "this was a public clock" is **SOFT** (needs a tower, a bell, an inscription); "this clock's reading was the official one" is **DOCTRINE**-grade (institutional claim); "this culture invented the escapement" is **DESIGN**-grade unless the diffusion evidence exists.
- **Emic vs etic naming.** Real terminology is partly ancient (*horologium* covers both sundial and water clock — **to-verify**) and partly modern scholarship ("verge-and-foliot", "tower clock", "star clock" as a type name). Mark which names are in-world words and which are the antiquarians' labels; the same rule as `vessel-container-lexicon.md` §3 applies verbatim here.
- **Archaeoastronomy caution.** A claimed alignment or "sundial" at a site needs converging evidence, not a plausible shadow (the structural-overreading failure mode, `material-culture-inference-method.md` §5). Do not let a fictional dig "prove" a clock from one groove.

## 8. Calibration — legend-grade, contested, and over-claimed

- **Alfred's candle clocks are legend-grade.** The story (Asser, *Life of Alfred* ch. 104) has the king dividing candles to keep the hours; the *device class* (calibrated candles) is real and widely attested, but the specific royal anecdote is hagiography, not horology. Use the practice; mark the anecdote (**to-verify**).
- **"Monks invented the clock to keep the hours" is a contested origin story.** Dohrn-van Rossum's corrective is that civic, market and administrative demand ran alongside (and in places ahead of) the monastic need, and that the bell-tower clock is a civic institution as much as a liturgical one. Write the origin your culture's institutions support — but do not print the monastic-origin story as settled history.
- **"Salisbury 1386 is the oldest clock" is a dating claim, not a fact.** Several English tower clocks compete for the title and the dates rest on documentary readings that scholars dispute (**to-verify** before printing any single date).
- **The Chinese clock tower (Su Song, 1088) is real and sophisticated** — an astronomical tower with an escapement-like mechanism and a documented treatise (Needham vol. 3). Whether the European escapement *derived* from Chinese models (Price's transmission argument) or arose independently is **contested**; present diffusion as a question, not a result.
- **A sundial does not show clock time.** A dial shows *apparent solar time*; a clock keeps *mean time*; the difference (equation of time) reaches roughly ±16 minutes over the year, and local noon differs by longitude as well. A scene that has a character set a clock by a sundial and be exactly right is a defect — the discrepancy is a better scene anyway.
- **Water clocks are not accurate by default.** Constant head, temperature and sediment are engineering problems; a culture with a clepsydra but no overflow tank has an institution-sized error, not a precision instrument.
- **"The clock caused industrial capitalism" is an over-claim.** Mumford's "key machine" and Landes's argument give the clock a large role in time-discipline (Thompson 1967 is the load-bearing source); the causal claim is historiographic and contested — report it as an argument, never as a law of history. The "clockwork universe" is likewise a metaphor-history claim (couples `myth-symbol/cosmology-cosmography.md`).
- **Clocks as "Trojan horses" of influence.** Clock diplomacy at the Chinese and Japanese courts is a real diffusion channel (Cipolla; Needham **to-verify**), but the framing that clocks "opened" those cultures is an overreach; diffusion of an object is not conversion of a culture.
- **Precision numbers are the classic trap.** Every date, accuracy class and error rate in this entry needs a page-level check before it enters a world bible; treat the table in §2 as design ranges, not constants.
