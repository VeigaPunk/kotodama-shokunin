## 1. The gap this entry fills, and the frame: four machines, wired in one direction

The gap is stated in the header as `- gap:`. In short: the culture-material partition owns every other material system a household runs on and none of the one it must run on exactly once per person. This section states the frame that fills it.

- **Four machines.** A mortuary system is DISPOSAL (what the body becomes), ASSEMBLAGE (what is deposited with it, and who decides), MONUMENT (what the living build above it and must keep building), and WORD (the register the whole system speaks in). Design them as one object or the culture will have grave goods and no graves.
- **The wiring is one-directional and it is the entry's master claim.** DISPOSAL constrains the SCHEDULE (cremation completes in hours, exposure takes weeks, secondary burial takes a decomposition interval); the schedule constrains the MOURNING DURATION (you cannot hold mourners in suspension longer than the rite they are waiting on); the duration constrains the MOURNING ECONOMY (who is withdrawn from production, for how long, at whose cost); and the economy generates the ENFORCEMENT LAW (because a state that conscripts labour must legislate mourning or lose the labour). Skip a link and the system fails legibly: a culture with a nine-day funeral and a three-year mourning grade has two different schedules fighting, which is a scene, not a bug.
- **Second claim.** **The grave is the only artifact a culture makes for an audience it will never meet.** Every other artifact is made for a living user who can complain. A grave is made for the dead, the ancestors, the rivals, and the future — none of whom can. That is why the grave register is the culture's most conservative and most rhetorical, and why it is simultaneously the richest inference surface and the most trap-laden one (`corpus/knowledge/culture-material/material-culture-inference-method.md:81`).
- **Third claim.** **Grief has a budget.** In most pre-modern economies a funeral is the largest per-capita expenditure a household will ever make and mourning is the largest labour withdrawal. That single fact generates the whole enforcement layer: funerary sumptuary law (Rome), mourning-grade law (China), shroud law (England), and mourning-dress markets (Europe). A fictional culture that legislates nothing else legislates this.
- **Craft rule.** Name the *payer* before you name the rite. The payer determines the scale, the guest list, the duration, and — usually — the doctrine.

## 2. DISPOSAL — the typology and its commitment column

Pick a disposal technology and you have chosen the schedule, the pollution rules, the monument's form, and half the lexicon. The commitment column is the load-bearing part: each row is a *contract*, not a flavour.

| Disposal technology | What the body becomes | Real exemplars | What it commits you to |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Primary inhumation** (extended or flexed) | earth + a body-shaped void | the default across most of the record | a grave-cut labour demand, a body-orientation convention, and a decomposition interval you must either wait out or hide |
| **Secondary burial / ossuary** | clean bone, curated and gathered | Hertz's double funeral (Borneo; `ritual-liminality.md:24`); Merina *famadihana* (`malagasy-madagascar.md:98`); Toraja *ma'nene* (`ritual-liminality.md:32`); Levantine ossuaries | **two rites and an interval** — a temporary place for the fresh dead and a permanent place for the bone; a bone-handling office; and a mourning clock with two endpoints |
| **Cremation** | heat, smoke, and calcined bone | Rome (where the Twelve Tables barred burning inside the city — Cicero *De Legibus* 2.58); the Hindu *antyeshti*; the modern cremation movement | fuel as a cost line; a pyre or furnace office; an urn or a scattering place; and a **bone-collection rite** — the bone is the surviving object and the words for it become the rite's nouns |
| **Excarnation (exposure)** | carrion birds, sun, weather | the Tibetan *bya gtor*; the Zoroastrian *dakhma* with the *astodan* that collects the bone | a place that must be **out of the settlement and defended against pollution**; a class of persons who may touch the body; and, in the Zoroastrian case, a **second disposal technology in sequence** (exposure, then bone curation) — the cleanest proof that a system can run two technologies |
| **Water burial** | the river, the sea | river deposition at the great river cities; sea burial | the water as an agent that **removes the monument** — the culture must invent a substitute monument or accept that its dead leave no address |
| **Artificial preservation** | a durable image of the person | Egypt (natron desiccation, the canopic set, the seventy-day clock — Herodotus 2.86–88); Chinchorro, c. 7000–3500 BP (Arriaza 1995); Scythian permafrost (`scythian-saka.md:64`); the Inca *mallqui* | an **industrial-scale specialist trade**, a materials supply (natron, resin, linen, cinnabar), and a *transport* problem — a preserved body is a body that can be moved, and a movable ancestor is a political object |
| **Vehicle burial** | a ship, cart or boat as coffin | Oseberg (dendro-dated 834 CE), Gokstad, Borre; Sutton Hoo Mound 1; Ibn Fadlan's 921/922 CE Rus' ship-cremation account | the vehicle is **removed from the economy** — the assemblage's single largest cost, and the reason ship burial is a claim about what a lineage can destroy |
| **Mound / monument over the body** | architecture above the dead | the Japanese Kofun network (`japan-yayoi-kofun.md:46`); the Shang royal tombs (`china-shang-zhou.md:18`); the Borre and Oseberg mounds; Mississippian platform mounds | a **maintenance institution** and a labour levy (see §4); mounds are also the most legible class of monument to a later observer, which is why "mound" becomes an era name |
| **Rock-cut / built tomb** | the tomb as architecture | the Egyptian rock-cut tombs; the Roman catacombs; Petra; Palenque's Temple of the Inscriptions | a **construction guild** and a plan; a built tomb is the only disposal technology whose vocabulary overlaps architecture's, which is why tomb-words enter the built-environment lexicon (`architecture-settlement-lexicon.md:43`) |
| **Figurative substitution** | an image does the work the body or the retainer did | ushabti figurines; *yong* 俑; the Qin terracotta army; paper effigies (*zhizha*) | a **figurine industry** and a doctrine that answers "does the image count?" — the substitution arc at `ritual-liminality.md:61` is a documented sequence, and the Mencian critique of the figurine-maker (*Mengzi* 1A.4, (to-verify)) shows it was argued about at the time |

- **The schedule is a vocabulary.** Every disposal technology comes with a day-count, and the day-counts are the culture's own numbers: Rome's *novendialis* (nine days), the Zoroastrian fourth-day *chahārom*, Jewish *shiva* (seven) and *shloshim* (thirty), the Islamic *iddah* of four months and ten days (Qur'an 2:234), the Hindu *sapindikarana* that converts *preta* into *pitr*, China's mourning grades up to three years, the annual *yahrzeit*. **Give a fictional culture its own day-count and you have given it its own mourning grammar.** Numbers are (to-verify) per the `- recheck:` line.
- **Hertz's interval is the general shape.** Hertz 1907 (carried at `ritual-liminality.md:24`) showed that a funeral is often two rites with a decomposition interval between them, during which the dead is a liminal presence near the living and only after which does it become an ancestor with a place and an inheritance. **The interval is the single most useful design variable in this entry**: it is a *plot budget* (what happens while the dead is neither gone nor settled?), a *pollution window* (what may not be done?), and an *inheritance delay* (who holds the estate meanwhile?).
- **The two-technology rule.** A system that runs exposure and bone-curation, or cremation and urn-deposition, is not confused — it is *sequenced*, and the sequence is where the doctrine lives. Zoroastrian exposure-then-*astodan* is the model. Design the intermediate container, not just the final one.
- **Doctrine is the selector, not progress.** Cremation versus inhumation is not a sophistication ladder; it tracks doctrine, fuel cost, land cost, and contagion law. The modern Western shift back to cremation in the later nineteenth and twentieth centuries is a *legal and sanitary* history (and the Catholic permission for cremation in 1963 is the dated end of a long ban) — (to-verify) for the 1963 document. Never let an in-world character narrate disposal as development.

## 3. ASSEMBLAGE — the grave as a document written by the living

- **The governing rule, cited not restated:** "Burial is performed by the living; assemblages reflect funeral politics, succession claims, and mourning, not a dead person's CV" (`material-culture-inference-method.md:81`, citing Parker Pearson 1999). The grave is a *speech act by the survivors*. Every assemblage question is therefore a question about **who was speaking and to whom**.
- **The four decisions an assemblage makes.** For any culture, answer these and the inventory writes itself:
  1. **Who decides what goes in?** (the household head, the lineage, a burial society, the state, a guild). The decision-maker is the entry's *author*, and the culture's politics show in the byline.
  2. **What may NOT go in?** Every culture keeps a class of **inalienable** objects out of the grave precisely because they must stay with the living — the regalia, the title, the heirloom, the debt (couples `material-culture-inference-method.md:3`, whose toolbox names *inalienable possessions*; and `adornment-body-codes.md:12`, whose RITE column is inalienable by construction). **The prohibition list is more diagnostic than the inventory**, because it names what the group could not afford to lose.
  3. **What is destroyed versus deposited?** Breaking, folding, burning, "killing" an object before deposition is a **deliberate state change** and it is legible: a folded sword, a bent coin, a broken pot, and a coppers "killed" at a potlatch (`ritual-liminality.md:49`) are all statements that the object has left the economy. A deliberately destroyed object is not a broken object.
  4. **Who else is in the grave?** Retainers, animals, vehicles, and the second body. The **substitution arc** (`ritual-liminality.md:61`: Ur PG 1237 → Scythian royal burials, Herodotus 4.71–72 → Shang royal tombs → ushabti → *yong* → terracotta army → paper effigies) is the documented trajectory of the human content of a grave migrating into images as the state monopolizes violence. Design the *current position on that arc*, and design the argument about it.
- **Assemblage arithmetic.** Give every grave-good class three numbers and the assemblage becomes computable: **unit cost**, **source distance**, and **destructibility**. The first two produce a trade map (imported goods in a grave are the culture's contact evidence — `adornment-body-codes.md:17`, where merchant graves at Birka carry scales and weights); the third produces the doctrine (an object that *could* have been destroyed and was not is a claim about permanence).
- **Craft rule.** Do not deposit the culture's best object unless the culture can lose it. Every "richest grave ever found" is also a statement that the survivors could absorb the loss — which is why grave wealth tracks **succession crises** more reliably than it tracks affection.
- **The grave is also a chronometer.** Flinders Petrie's 1899 sequence dating of Naqada graves is the founding case of seriation (`vessel-container-lexicon.md:120`): because an assemblage is a *closed* group deposited at one moment, it dates. This is the one sense in which the inference is clean — **association, not meaning**. The style dates the context; it does not report the belief.
- **The looting clause.** Looting, reuse, and reburial are *formation processes*, not exceptions. A reopened grave is a normal archaeological object, and the culture that reopens graves has a doctrine about it (`material-culture-inference-method.md:3`, the *formation processes* framework; `chimu.md:140`, where looting and collecting shape the sample). Write the reopening rule into the culture: who may open, when, to take what, and what is owed.

## 4. MONUMENT — the thing that must be maintained

- **The maintenance clause is mandatory.** This entry adopts the transport file's discipline (DR-TR-02 in `transport-mobility-lexicon.md`: "no route ships without its upkeep rule") and applies it to death: **no monument ships without its upkeep rule.** A grave-marker, a mound, a tomb-chapel, a household altar, and a grave-sweeping calendar are all *institutions with a maintenance budget*, and the calendar is the budget's visible form.
- **The maintenance forms, and what each one is really doing:**

| Maintenance form | Real exemplar | What it commits you to |
|---|---|---|
| **Annual grave-sweeping** | Qingming and Zhongyuan, the ghost month (`ritual-liminality.md:87`) | a fixed calendar slot, a kin obligation that survives the household's split, and a *landscape of addresses* |
| **Welcome-and-send-off festival** | Obon — *mukaebi* welcome fires, *Bon odori*, *okuribi* send-off (`ritual-liminality.md:88`) | a **two-ended** rite: the dead arrive and must be sent back; a culture that welcomes but never sends off has a ghost problem, not a calendar |
| **Household altar** | the Japanese *butsudan*; the Roman *lararium* | the dead live in the house, which means the house has a permanent liturgical room and the daily offering has a labour cost |
| **Bone-cleaning cycle** | Merina *famadihana* (`malagasy-madagascar.md:98-106`); Toraja *ma'nene* | the tomb is *opened on a schedule*, so the culture needs an opening authority and a rule for what may be done while open — and, per `malagasy-madagascar.md:100`, **never a fixed "every N years" law** |
| **Monument as territorial claim** | Merina *tanindrazana*, ancestral land, coupling residence to tomb affiliation (`malagasy-madagascar.md:84`); the Saxe–Binford hypothesis that formal disposal areas claim rights over restricted resources | the dead are the **land title**; a monument is a legal document with a roof |
| **Lychgate and the coffin's last stop** | the roofed gate where a coffin rests before entry (the *lych* element < Old English *līc*, "body") | the procession has **stations**, and each station is a word — a threshold vocabulary the built environment does not otherwise have |
- **The monument is the culture's longest-lived artifact and its most conservative text.** A grave stele, an epitaph, a *matzevah*, a *stupa*, a *ling* 陵, a *haugr* — these outlive the language that carved them, which is why the **epitaph is the one text class where a dead register becomes a readable document.** Design the epitaph formula before the grave goods: formula survives.
- **Craft rule.** A monument without a caretaker is a ruin, and a ruin is a different story. Decide early whether the culture's monuments are **maintained**, **abandoned**, or **deliberately desecrated** — the three produce three genres (piety, elegy, and politics), and the third is the cheapest way to stage a succession crisis.

## 5. MOURNING ECONOMY — the enforcement layer (grief has a budget)

This is the section that turns the system into law, and it is the entry's most usable table.

| Instrument | Real exemplar | What it regulates | The design lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Funerary sumptuary law** | the Twelve Tables' limits on funeral expense, veils and flute-players (Cicero *De Legibus* 2.58–2.59, (to-verify)) | the **funeral**, not the wardrobe — a spending cap with a ceremonial shape | the earliest sumptuary law a state writes is often about death, because that is where a family can ruin itself and out-display its betters |
| **Mourning-grade law** | China's wǔfú 五服, keyed into the Tang Code so that kin-crime penalties scale with the mourning grade (`ritual-garments.md:108`, citing Johnson 1979) | **duration × cloth × conduct**, as a legal quantity with penalties | the mourning grade is the culture's **kinship-distance meter** — a five-step ruler anyone can read, which is why it can be legislated |
| **Shroud/trade law** | the English Burial in Woollen Acts 1666 and 1678, with affidavits (`dress-through-eras.md:6`, `:95`, `:120`) | the **material** of the shroud | most funeral legislation is really **industrial policy wearing mourning clothes** — check whose trade the statute protects before you call it piety |
| **Mourning dress market** | the European black-wool and crape economy; the Victorian stage system (Taylor 1983 via `ritual-garments.md:11`, `:149`) | **colour, cloth and stage duration** as purchasable goods | mourning dress is a *market with a schedule*, which means it can be cheated: the "slightly less black" wardrobe is a legible social crime |
| **Labour withdrawal** | the *iustitium* (public suspension of business during mourning); the mourning leave built into the grades | **time out of production** | this is the line that makes the state care: conscript labour and mourning leave are in direct competition, and the statute is the settlement |
| **The posthumous name price** | the Japanese *kaimyō* 戒名, conferred at death and priced by character count (to-verify) | **the word itself** as a priced good | the strongest single demonstration that the WORD layer is part of the economy: a funeral's most expensive line item can be a **name** |
| **The prohibition** | the 1829 Bengal regulation against *satī* (Regulation XVII of 1829, (to-verify)) | a mortuary practice, by statute | when a disposal-adjacent practice becomes a **political symbol**, the law that ends it becomes an era marker — the entry's cleanest case that mortuary change is periodization |
- **The assayability test applies here too.** The sumptuary file's craft claim (`sumptuary-law-rank-display.md:1`) is that a dress law survives only where the sign is **assayable**. Apply it to every mourning rule you write: **can a stranger tell, at the funeral, whether the rule is being kept?** If not, the rule is custom, not law, and the culture will enforce it by gossip rather than by penalty — which is a different and equally usable machine.
- **Craft rule.** Give every mourning grade **five columns** — duration, dress, food, speech, work — and the grade becomes a character sheet. A mourner who keeps the dress and breaks the food rule is legible; a mourner who keeps nothing is a scandal with a name.
- **The inversion lever.** Mourning codes invert between adjacent cultures (`adornment-body-codes.md:66`: one culture's mourning haircut is its neighbour's taboo; `color-symbolism.md:49-56`, `:65`: white as the "undone" colour; `ritual-garments.md:149`: mourning black is a period-coded European development). **Put two adjacent cultures in the same scene in opposite mourning codes and you have a diplomatic incident for free.**

## 6. WORD — the death register, in four strata

Build the lexicon in four strata and it will look lived-in. Each stratum has its own borrowability and its own drift.

| Stratum | What it contains | Real anchors | Design note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1. Avoidance / euphemism** | the words that *do not* name the corpse or the death | the general taboo machinery of naming the dead; the Zoroastrian *nasu* (corpse-pollution as a named entity) | this stratum is **unstable by design** — it drifts and fossilizes (`synthesis/taboo-euphemism-lifecycle.md`). A culture with no avoidance terms has no death taboo |
| **2. Rite terms** | the names of the steps | Greek *prothesis* → *ekphora* → *perideipnon*; Roman *funus*, *novendialis*, *iustitium*; Hindu *antyeshti*, *shraddha*, *pinda*; Islamic *janazah*, *kafan*; Jewish *shiva*, *shloshim*, *yahrzeit*; Japanese *tsuya*, *sōshiki*, *kotsuage*; Merina *famadihana* | **one word per step**, and the step count is the schedule's visible form. A rite term borrowed from a neighbour usually arrives *before* the rite does (couples `linguistics/language-contact.md`) |
| **3. Office terms** | the roles the trade creates | Roman *libitinarius*, *pollinctor*, *designator*, *praefica*, *vespillo*; the Jewish *chevra kadisha*; the Tibetan *rogyapa* | a mortuary system **always** creates a professional register, and that register is where the culture's contempt and its dependence both show. Plan the role noun with the rite noun |
| **4. Monument terms** | what the living build and name | *stēlē*; *epitaph*; *cenotaph*; *sarcophagus*; *mausoleum*; *necropolis*; Japanese *haka*, *butsudan*; Jewish *matzevah*; Norse *haugr*, *bautasteinn*; Chinese *ling* 陵; *dakhma*, *astodan*; *charnel*, *ossuary*, *catacomb* | this is the stratum that **becomes documents** — the epitaph is a text genre built from a monument word |
- **The etymology chains, and what each one teaches:**

| Chain | Lesson for a fictional lexicon |
|---|---|
| *sarcophagus* < Greek *sarkophagos*, "flesh-eating" (the stone of Assos; Pliny *Naturalis Historia* 36.27) | a **material property** becomes the container's name and then the container's name becomes generic — the word remembers a belief about the stone |
| *coffin* < Old French *cofin* "little basket" < Latin *cophinus* < Greek *kophinos* "basket" | the container's etymology remembers a **humbler container**; a culture's coffin word is usually its basket word, which is why container vocabularies and mortuary vocabularies overlap (`vessel-container-lexicon.md`) |
| *mausoleum* < *Mausolus* of Caria, whose tomb at Halicarnassus was a Wonder | a **person's name** becomes the generic word for a building class — the eponymy strategy in its purest form |
| *mummy* < Arabic *mūmiyā* < Persian *mūm* "wax" | **the word for the preserved body entered European languages as a commodity name** (medieval *mumia* was traded as a drug). A mortuary word can arrive through a *market*, not through a rite |
| *dirge* < Latin *Dirige*, the first word of the Office of the Dead's Matins antiphon; *requiem* < *Requiem aeternam* | **a genre is named by the first word of the rite.** The same mechanism names the *Te Deum*, the *Magnificat*, the *Placebo*. A culture with a liturgical funeral will have genre-nouns that are liturgical incipits |
| *epitaph*, *cenotaph* — both built on Greek *taphos*, "tomb" | **the tomb-word becomes the text-genre word**; and *cenotaph* proves the culture needed a word for an **empty** grave — the monument without the body, which is the transport-and-loss case |
| *necropolis* < *nekros* + *polis*, "city of the dead" | the cemetery is named as an **urban institution**, which is why its placement is a planning problem (extramural by law in Rome, intramural again when the city outgrows the wall) |
| *obituary* < Latin *obitus*, from *obire* "to go to meet"; the widespread motion-verb pattern for dying ("passed", "went before") | dying is described as **motion**, not cessation — a cross-linguistic tendency, not a universal; check the target language before asserting it |
| *hearse* — disputed: Middle English *herse*, a candle-framework over the coffin, from Old French *herce* "harrow"; the vehicle sense is a later shift | **flag the dispute in-world**: this is a word whose object changed and whose name stayed, which is exactly the loanword-outliving-the-referent case in `transport-mobility-lexicon.md` §7 |
| *catacomb* — from Latin *catacumbae*, further origin disputed (often tied to the site *ad catacumbas* near San Sebastiano) | a **place-name becomes a class-name**, and the chain is contested — the perfect in-world antiquarian argument |
| *shroud* < Old English *scrūd* "garment"; *pall* < Latin *pallium* "cloak"; *wake* < Old English *wacu* "watch"; *lych* < Old English *līc* "body" | the **plainest stratum is native and concrete** (a garment, a cloak, a watch, a body) while the elaborate stratum is borrowed and abstract. Design the two strata with different origins and the register will read as historical |
- **Craft rule.** Build the register in the strata order **1 → 4**, and give the *avoidance* stratum the most native phonology and the *monument* stratum the most borrowed forms. That is the attested direction in most of the chains above, and it is the cheapest way to make a death lexicon feel excavated rather than invented.
- **The name-change rule.** Many cultures rename the dead (the posthumous name, the temple name, the *kaimyō*), which means **the dead have a different onomastic system from the living** (couples `linguistics/naming-systems.md`). Design the two naming systems as two systems, and the moment of renaming becomes a scene.

## 7. READING GRAVES — the inference ladder and its traps

Use the eight-tier confidence ladder and the eleven-framework toolbox in `material-culture-inference-method.md`; this section adds only the **mortuary-specific traps**, which are the ones this artifact class generates.

| Trap | Why it fails | Counter-move |
|---|---|---|
| "The grave is the deceased's résumé" | burial is performed by the living for their own purposes (`material-culture-inference-method.md:81`) | read the **mourners'** politics: succession, faction, debt, obligation |
| "Rich grave = high social rank" | grave wealth tracks succession crises, gift-debt and faction as much as status; the Saxe–Binford correlation is a **hypothesis**, not a law (to-verify) | pair the grave with the whole settlement assemblage and with the *prohibition list* (§3) |
| "Unexplained, therefore ritual" | ritual and domestic life interpenetrate; "ritual" is the archaeologist's last resort (`material-culture-inference-method.md:82`) | demand the **deposition grammar** (`:56`, `:97`) and a stated alternative |
| "Pots = people" (style = ethnicity) | style is not ethnicity, and an assemblage's vocabulary is not a population's (`material-culture-inference-method.md:100`) | keep the culture-history equation out of the draft; use association for dating, not for identity |
| "The grave's orientation encodes cosmology" | often it encodes the digger's stance, the terrain, the season, or a neighbouring grave | test for **within-cemetery variance** first; a cosmology that admits no exceptions is usually a convention |
| "One burial proves a population-wide uniform" | `scythian-saka.md:24`; `mesopotamia-sumer-akkad.md:61` | require the sample statement: how many, from where, with what selection bias |
| "One burial proves an occupation" | `germanic-norse.md:143`, `:158` (a staff-like object does not make its owner a *vǫlva*) | require converging evidence from the body, the assemblage and the settlement |
| "An old object in a burial dates the burial" | `china-shang-zhou.md:106` (Fu Hao's tomb held Neolithic jades) | date the **context**, not the heirloom; an heirloom is a *statement* about descent |
| "Burial display = daily dress" | `egypt-ancient.md:148`; `maya-classic.md:145`; `china-shang-zhou.md:27` | separate the mortuary register from the everyday register explicitly, in the draft |
| "The grave's goods are the deceased's goods" | `moche.md:29`, `:110`, `:117` (do not merge tombs; not every garment worn in life) | assign goods to **depositors**, and never merge assemblages |
| "The preserved body's name is the body's name" | coffins and tombs are reused; the *mummy* word arrived as a commodity (see §6) | check container/occupant congruence before attaching a name |
| "The Death Pit proves mass sacrifice" | Ur PG 1237 is Woolley's reading and is contested in later literature (to-verify) | mark contested; use it as an in-world antiquarian's claim |
| "Cremation means a simpler religion" | cremation tracks doctrine, fuel, land and contagion law | cite the doctrinal source, never the disposal form |
| "A single ancient genome identifies an ethnicity" | a genome is an individual; a population claim needs a sample | keep the population claim bounded and separate from the burial's identity |
| "Charon's obol = a coin for the ferryman everywhere" | the label is a modern blanket term over a varied and partly literary practice (to-verify) | use the *practice* (a coin in a grave) and the *story* (the ferryman) as separate design slots |
- **The one clean inference.** *Association.* A closed assemblage dates and connects — that is what seriation was built on (`vessel-container-lexicon.md:120`). Everything about **meaning** must clear the ladder, and most mortuary meaning claims do not.
- **In-world epistemics is a design deliverable.** Give the culture's own antiquarians a wrong theory on purpose (`material-culture-inference-method.md:100`): the grave-résumé reading, the ritual-dustbin reading, the pots-as-peoples reading. Those errors become published monographs, court scandals and revisionist arguments — three scenes for the price of one.

## 8. Design order of operations (M1–M8)

1. **M1 — Choose the disposal technology** from the environment and the doctrine, using the four-question test: what may the body become (earth / fire / air / water / bone / image)? what does the environment permit? what does the doctrine require? what can the economy pay for?
2. **M2 — Write the schedule** (the day-count, and the Hertz interval if there is one). Name every stage in the culture's own language (stratum 2).
3. **M3 — Write the assemblage rule**: who decides, what may not go in (the prohibition list), what is destroyed before deposition, and who else is in the grave (the substitution-arc position).
4. **M4 — Write the monument and its maintenance clause**: form, caretaker, calendar slot, and the reopening rule. No monument without an upkeep rule.
5. **M5 — Write the mourning grades** as law: duration × dress × food × speech × work, with the assayability test applied to each row.
6. **M6 — Write the register** in the four strata, with the avoidance stratum most native and the monument stratum most borrowed; add the name-change rule if the dead are renamed.
7. **M7 — Write the in-world epistemics**: the culture's own wrong theory about its own graves, and who holds it.
8. **M8 — Couple to the era file**: is a disposal change a period marker in this culture's own historiography? If yes, name the seam and the rival account of it (couples `era-periodization-models.md`, whose Three-Age system is itself named after a deposition practice).
- **NON-CANON worked slot** (a demonstration of M1–M8's shape, not a setting element, and not canon): a river-delta culture that practices **primary inhumation for adults, water deposition for children, and bone-collection at one year** would generate — from M2 — a one-year mourning clock and a "first-year" name for the un-collected dead; from M3 — a prohibition on depositing fishing gear (it must pass to the next boat, because boats are inalienable), which makes the gear's *absence* the archaeologist's key signal; from M4 — a flood-season tomb-repair levy, and a grave-address that moves when the river moves; from M5 — a mourning grade whose heaviest column is *work*, because the levy competes with the harvest; from M6 — a native set of water-verbs for death and a borrowed set of stone-words for the markers, because the markers arrived with an imported masonry tradition; from M7 — an in-world antiquarian who reads the child-water-deposition as "the delta peoples had no affection for children", which is the entry's §7 trap #1 in its in-world form. **This paragraph demonstrates a method; it decides nothing about any world.**
- **Anti-pastiche guard.** Transplant the **structure** (the four machines, the commitment columns, the trap table), never a real culture's mortuary system wholesale (`material-culture-inference-method.md:99`; `verification/real-world-inspiration-boundaries.md`).
