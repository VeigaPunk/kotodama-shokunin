<span class="mw-empty-elt" about="#mwt2" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwAw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Other uses","href":"./Template:Other_uses"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"Cant (disambiguation)"}},"i":0}}]}'><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1368532237" typeof="mw:Extension/templatestyles" about="#mwt3" data-mw='{"name":"templatestyles","attrs":{"src":"Module:Hatnote/styles.css"},"body":{"extsrc":""}}'>.mw-parser-output .hatnote{font-style:italic}.mw-parser-output div.hatnote{padding-left:1.6em;margin-bottom:0.5em}.mw-parser-output .hatnote i{font-style:normal}.mw-parser-output .hatnote+.mw-empty-elt+.hatnote{margin-top:-0.5em}@media print{body.ns-0 .mw-parser-output .hatnote{display:none!important}}</style></span><div role="note" class="hatnote navigation-not-searchable" about="#mwt2" id="mwBA">For other uses, see <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cant_(disambiguation)" title="Cant (disambiguation)" class="mw-disambig">Cant (disambiguation)</a>.</div>

<p id="mwBQ">A <b id="mwBg">cant</b> is the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jargon" title="Jargon" id="mwBw">jargon</a> or language of a group, often employed to exclude or mislead people outside the group.<sup about="#mwt6" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-McArthur2_1-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"McArthur2"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-McArthur2-1"}}'><a href="#cite_note-McArthur2-1" id="mwCA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwCQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwCg">[</span>1<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwCw">]</span></span></a></sup> It may also be called a <b id="mwDA">cryptolect</b>, <b id="mwDQ">argot</b>, <b id="mwDg">pseudo-language</b>, <b id="mwDw">anti-language</b> or <b id="mwEA">secret language</b>. Each term differs slightly in meaning; their uses are inconsistent.</p>

<meta property="mw:PageProp/toc" id="mwEQ" data-mw='{"autoGenerated":true}'/></section><section data-mw-section-id="1" id="mwEg" aria-labelledby="Etymology"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Etymology">Etymology</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cant&amp;action=edit&amp;section=1" title="Edit section: Etymology"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwEw">There are two main schools of thought on the origin of the word <i id="mwFA">cant</i>:</p>

<ul id="mwFQ"><li id="mwFg">In <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics" title="Linguistics" id="mwFw">linguistics</a>, the derivation is normally seen to be from the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language" title="Irish language" id="mwGA">Irish</a> word <span title="Irish-language text" about="#mwt7" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwGQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"ga"},"2":{"wt":"caint"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="ga">caint</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_Irish-language_text" about="#mwt7" id="mwGg"/> (older spelling <span title="Irish-language text" about="#mwt8" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwGw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"ga"},"2":{"wt":"cainnt"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="ga">cainnt</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_Irish-language_text" about="#mwt8" id="mwHA"/>), "speech, talk",<sup about="#mwt10" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Queen's2_2-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Queen&apos;s2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Queen's2-2" id="mwHQ"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwHg"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwHw">[</span>2<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwIA">]</span></span></a></sup> or <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic" title="Scottish Gaelic" id="mwIQ">Scottish Gaelic</a> <span title="Scottish Gaelic-language text" about="#mwt9" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwIg" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"gd"},"2":{"wt":"cainnt"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="gd">cainnt</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_Scottish_Gaelic-language_text" about="#mwt9" id="mwIw"/>. It is seen to have derived amongst the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itinerant_groups_in_Europe" title="Itinerant groups in Europe" id="mwJA">itinerant</a> groups of people in <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland" title="Ireland" id="mwJQ">Ireland</a> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland" title="Scotland" id="mwJg">Scotland</a>, who hailed from both Irish/Scottish Gaelic and English-speaking backgrounds, ultimately developing as various <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creole_language" title="Creole language" id="mwJw">creole languages</a>.<sup about="#mwt11" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Queen's2_2-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Queen&apos;s2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Queen's2-2" id="mwKA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwKQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwKg">[</span>2<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwKw">]</span></span></a></sup> However, the various types of cant (Scottish/Irish) are mutually unintelligible. The Irish creole variant is termed "<a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shelta" title="Shelta" id="mwLA">the cant</a>". Its speakers from the <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Traveller" title="Irish Traveller" class="mw-redirect" id="mwLQ">Irish Traveller</a> community know it as <i id="mwLg">Gammon</i>, while the linguistic community identifies it as <i id="mwLw">shelta</i>.<sup about="#mwt12" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Queen's2_2-2" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Queen&apos;s2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Queen's2-2" id="mwMA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwMQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwMg">[</span>2<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwMw">]</span></span></a></sup></li>
<li id="mwNA">Outside Gaelic circles, the derivation is typically seen to be from <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin" title="Latin" id="mwNQ">Latin</a> <span title="Latin-language text" about="#mwt13" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwNg" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"la"},"2":{"wt":"cantāre"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="la">cantāre</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_Latin-language_text" about="#mwt13" id="mwNw"/>, 'to sing', via <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_French" title="Norman French" class="mw-redirect" id="mwOA">Norman French</a> <span title="Norman-language text" about="#mwt14" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwOQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"nrf-Latn"},"2":{"wt":"canter"},"size":{"wt":"100%"},"italic":{"wt":"yes"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="nrf-Latn" style="font-size: 100%;">canter</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_Norman-language_text" about="#mwt14" id="mwOg"/>.<sup about="#mwt15" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-McArthur2_1-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"McArthur2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-McArthur2-1" id="mwOw"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwPA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwPQ">[</span>1<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwPg">]</span></span></a></sup><sup about="#mwt18" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Collins2_3-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Collins2"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-Collins2-3"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Collins2-3" id="mwPw"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwQA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwQQ">[</span>3<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwQg">]</span></span></a></sup> Within this derivation, the history of the word is seen to have referred to the chanting of friars initially, used disparagingly some time between the 12th<sup about="#mwt19" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Collins2_3-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Collins2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Collins2-3" id="mwQw"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwRA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwRQ">[</span>3<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwRg">]</span></span></a></sup> and 15th centuries.<sup about="#mwt20" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-McArthur2_1-2" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"McArthur2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-McArthur2-1" id="mwRw"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwSA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwSQ">[</span>1<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwSg">]</span></span></a></sup> Gradually, the term was applied to the singsong of beggars and eventually a criminal jargon.</li></ul>

</section><section data-mw-section-id="2" id="mwSw" aria-labelledby="Argot"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Argot"><i id="mwTA">Argot</i></h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cant&amp;action=edit&amp;section=2" title="Edit section: Argot"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwTQ">An <i id="mwTg">argot</i> (<span class="rt-commentedText nowrap" about="#mwt21" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwTw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"IPAc-en","href":"./Template:IPAc-en"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"lang"},"2":{"wt":"pron"},"3":{"wt":"ˈ"},"4":{"wt":"ɑr"},"5":{"wt":"ɡ"},"6":{"wt":"oʊ"}},"i":0}}]}'><style data-mw-deduplicate="TemplateStyles:r1177148991" typeof="mw:Extension/templatestyles" about="#mwt22" data-mw='{"name":"templatestyles","attrs":{"src":"Module:IPA/styles.css"},"body":{"extsrc":""}}'>.mw-parser-output .IPA-label-small{font-size:85%}.mw-parser-output .references .IPA-label-small,.mw-parser-output .infobox .IPA-label-small,.mw-parser-output .navbox .IPA-label-small{font-size:100%}</style><span class="IPA-label IPA-label-small">English:  </span><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ˈ/: primary stress follows">ˈ</span><span title="/ɑːr/: 'ar' in 'far'">ɑːr</span><span title="/ɡ/: 'g' in 'guy'">ɡ</span><span title="/oʊ/: 'o' in 'code'">oʊ</span></span>/</a></span></span>, <span class="rt-commentedText nowrap" about="#mwt23" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwUA" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"IPAc-en","href":"./Template:IPAc-en"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"-"},"2":{"wt":"g"},"3":{"wt":"@"},"4":{"wt":"t"}},"i":0}}]}'><span class="IPA nopopups noexcerpt" lang="en-fonipa"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/English" title="Help:IPA/English">/-<span style="border-bottom:1px dotted"><span title="/ɡ/: 'g' in 'guy'">ɡ</span><span title="/ə/: 'a' in 'about'">ə</span><span title="'t' in 'tie'">t</span></span>/</a></span></span>; from <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language" title="French language" id="mwUQ">French</a> <i id="mwUg">argot</i> <span class="IPA nowrap" lang="fr-Latn-fonipa" about="#mwt24" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwUw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"IPA","href":"./Template:IPA"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"aʁɡo"},"3":{"wt":""}},"i":0}}]}'><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Help:IPA/French" title="Help:IPA/French"><span typeof="mw:Entity">[</span>aʁɡo<span typeof="mw:Entity">]</span></a></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Pages_with_French_IPA" about="#mwt24" id="mwVA"/> '<a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slang" title="Slang" id="mwVQ">slang</a>'<sup about="#mwt27" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-4" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-4"}}'><a href="#cite_note-4" id="mwVg"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwVw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwWA">[</span>4<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwWQ">]</span></span></a></sup>) is a language used by various groups to prevent outsiders from understanding their conversations. The term <i id="mwWg">argot</i> is also used to refer to the informal specialized vocabulary from a particular field of study, occupation, or hobby, in which <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_sense" title="Word sense" id="mwWw">sense</a> it overlaps with <i id="mwXA"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jargon" title="Jargon" id="mwXQ">jargon</a></i>.</p>

<p id="mwXg">In his 1862 novel <i id="mwXw"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Misérables" title="Les Misérables" id="mwYA">Les Misérables</a>,</i> <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Hugo" title="Victor Hugo" id="mwYQ">Victor Hugo</a> refers to that argot as both "the language of the dark" and "the language of misery".<sup about="#mwt30" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-5" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-5"}}'><a href="#cite_note-5" id="mwYg"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwYw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwZA">[</span>5<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwZQ">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwZg">The earliest known record of the term <i id="mwZw">argot</i> in this context was in a 1628 document. The word was probably derived from the contemporary name <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt31" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwaA" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"les argotiers"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr">les argotiers</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt31" id="mwaQ"/>, given to a group of thieves at that time.<sup about="#mwt32" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-6" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-6"}}'><a href="#cite_note-6" id="mwag"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwaw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwbA">[</span>6<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwbQ">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwbg">Under the strictest definition, an <i id="mwbw">argot</i> is a proper language with its own grammatical system.<sup about="#mwt38" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-7" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-7"}}'><a href="#cite_note-7" id="mwcA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwcQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwcg">[</span>7<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwcw">]</span></span></a></sup> Such complete secret languages are rare because the speakers usually have some public language in common, on which the argot is largely based. Such argots are <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicon" title="Lexicon" id="mwdA">lexically</a> divergent <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_(linguistics)" title="Variety (linguistics)" id="mwdQ">forms</a> of a particular language, with a part of its vocabulary replaced by words unknown to the larger public; <i id="mwdg">argot</i> used in this <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_sense" title="Word sense" id="mwdw">sense</a> is <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym" title="Synonym" id="mweA">synonymous</a> with <i id="mweQ">cant</i>. For example, <i id="mweg">argot</i> in this sense is used for systems such as <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt33" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwew" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"[[verlan]]"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verlan" title="Verlan">verlan</a></i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt33" id="mwfA"/> and <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt34" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwfQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"[[louchébem]]"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louchébem" title="Louchébem">louchébem</a></i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt34" id="mwfg"/>, which retain French syntax and apply transformations only to individual words (and often only to a certain subset of words, such as nouns, or semantic content words).<sup about="#mwt41" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-valdman2_8-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"valdman2"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-valdman2-8"}}'><a href="#cite_note-valdman2-8" id="mwfw"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwgA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwgQ">[</span>8<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwgg">]</span></span></a></sup> Such systems are examples of <i id="mwgw">argots</i> <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt35" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwhA" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"à clef"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr">à clef</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt35" id="mwhQ"/>, or "coded argots".<sup about="#mwt42" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-valdman2_8-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"valdman2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-valdman2-8" id="mwhg"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwhw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwiA">[</span>8<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwiQ">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwig">Specific words can go from argot into everyday speech or the other way. For example, modern French <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt43" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwiw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"loufoque"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr">loufoque</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt43" id="mwjA"/> 'crazy', 'goofy', now common usage, originated in the <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt44" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwjQ" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"louchébem"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr">louchébem</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt44" id="mwjg"/> transformation of Fr. <span title="French-language text" about="#mwt45" typeof="mw:Transclusion" id="mwjw" data-mw='{"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"Lang","href":"./Template:Lang"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"fr"},"2":{"wt":"fou"}},"i":0}}]}'><i lang="fr">fou</i></span><link rel="mw:PageProp/Category" href="./Category:Articles_containing_French-language_text" about="#mwt45" id="mwkA"/> 'crazy'.</p>

<p id="mwkQ">In the field of medicine, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physician" title="Physician" class="mw-redirect" id="mwkg">physicians</a> have been said to have their own spoken argot, cant, or slang, which incorporates commonly understood abbreviations and acronyms, frequently used technical <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloquialism" title="Colloquialism" id="mwkw">colloquialisms</a>, and many everyday professional slang words (that may or may not be institutionally or geographically localized).<sup about="#mwt48" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Hukill-19612_9-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Hukill-19612"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-Hukill-19612-9"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Hukill-19612-9" id="mwlA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwlQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwlg">[</span>9<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwlw">]</span></span></a></sup> While many of these colloquialisms may prove impenetrable to most lay people, few seem to be specifically designed to conceal meaning from patients (perhaps because standard medical terminology would usually suffice anyway).<sup about="#mwt49" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Hukill-19612_9-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Hukill-19612"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Hukill-19612-9" id="mwmA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwmQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwmg">[</span>9<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwmw">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

</section><section data-mw-section-id="3" id="mwnA" aria-labelledby="Anti-language"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Anti-language">Anti-language</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cant&amp;action=edit&amp;section=3" title="Edit section: Anti-language"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwnQ">The concept of the <i id="mwng">anti-language</i> was first defined and studied by the linguist <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Halliday" title="Michael Halliday" id="mwnw">Michael Halliday</a>, who used the term to describe the <i id="mwoA"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lingua_franca" title="Lingua franca" id="mwoQ">lingua franca</a></i> of an <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-society" title="Anti-society" id="mwog">anti-society</a>.<sup about="#mwt52" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-:02_10-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":":02"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-:02-10"}}'><a href="#cite_note-:02-10" id="mwow"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwpA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwpQ">[</span>10<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwpg">]</span></span></a></sup> An anti-society is a small, separate community intentionally created within a larger society as an alternative to or resistance of it.<sup about="#mwt53" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-:02_10-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":":02"}}'><a href="#cite_note-:02-10" id="mwpw"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwqA"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwqQ">[</span>10<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwqg">]</span></span></a></sup> For example, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Podgórecki" title="Adam Podgórecki" id="mwqw">Adam Podgórecki</a> studied one anti-society composed of Polish prisoners; Bhaktiprasad Mallik of Sanskrit College studied another composed of criminals in Calcutta.<sup about="#mwt54" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-:02_10-2" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":":02"}}'><a href="#cite_note-:02-10" id="mwrA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwrQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwrg">[</span>10<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwrw">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwsA">These societies develop anti-languages as a means to prevent outsiders from understanding their communication and as a manner of establishing a subculture that meets the needs of their alternative social structure.<sup about="#mwt57" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-:12_11-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":":12"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-:12-11"}}'><a href="#cite_note-:12-11" id="mwsQ"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwsg"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwsw">[</span>11<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwtA">]</span></span></a></sup> Anti-languages differ from <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slang" title="Slang" id="mwtQ">slang</a> and jargon in that they are used solely among ostracized social groups, including prisoners,<sup about="#mwt60" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-12" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-12"}}'><a href="#cite_note-12" id="mwtg"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwtw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwuA">[</span>12<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwuQ">]</span></span></a></sup> criminals, homosexuals,<sup about="#mwt61" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-:12_11-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":":12"}}'><a href="#cite_note-:12-11" id="mwug"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwuw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwvA">[</span>11<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwvQ">]</span></span></a></sup> and teenagers.<sup about="#mwt64" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-autogenerated12_13-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"autogenerated12"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-autogenerated12-13"}}'><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated12-13" id="mwvg"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwvw"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwwA">[</span>13<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwwQ">]</span></span></a></sup> Anti-languages use the same basic vocabulary and grammar as their native language in an unorthodox fashion. For example, anti-languages borrow words from other languages, create unconventional compounds, or utilize new suffixes for existing words. Anti-languages may also change words using <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metathesis_(linguistics)" title="Metathesis (linguistics)" id="mwwg">metathesis</a>, reversal of sounds or letters (e.g., apple to <i id="mwww">elppa</i>), or substituting their consonants.<sup about="#mwt65" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-:02_10-3" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":":02"}}'><a href="#cite_note-:02-10" id="mwxA"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwxQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwxg">[</span>10<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwxw">]</span></span></a></sup> Therefore, anti-languages are distinct and unique and are not simply <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialect" title="Dialect" id="mwyA">dialects</a> of existing languages.</p>

<p id="mwyQ">In his essay "Anti-Language", Halliday synthesized the research of Thomas Harman, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Podgórecki" title="Adam Podgórecki" id="mwyg">Adam Podgórecki</a>, and Bhaktiprasad Mallik to explore anti-languages and the connection between verbal communication and the maintenance of a social structure. For this reason, the study of anti-languages is both a study of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology" title="Sociology" id="mwyw">sociology</a> and linguistics. Halliday's findings can be compiled as a list of nine criteria that a language must meet to be considered an anti-language:</p>

<ul id="mwzA"><li id="mwzQ">An anti-society is a society set up within another society as a conscious alternative to it.</li>
<li id="mwzg">Like the early records of the languages of exotic cultures, the information usually comes to us as word lists.</li>
<li id="mwzw">The simplest form taken by an anti-language is that of new words from old: it is a language relexicalised.</li>
<li id="mw0A">The principle is that of same grammar, different vocabulary.</li>
<li id="mw0Q">Effective communication depends on exchanging meanings that are inaccessible to the layperson.</li>
<li id="mw0g">The anti-language is not just an optional extra; it is the fundamental element in the existence of the "second life" phenomenon.</li>
<li id="mw0w">The most important vehicle of reality-maintenance is conversation. All who employ this same form of communication are reality-maintaining others.</li>
<li id="mw1A">The anti-language is a vehicle of resocialisation.</li>
<li id="mw1Q">There is continuity between language and anti-language.</li></ul>

<p id="mw1g">Examples of anti-languages include <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cockney_rhyming_slang" title="Cockney rhyming slang" class="mw-redirect" id="mw1w">Cockney rhyming slang</a>, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CB_slang" title="CB slang" class="mw-redirect" id="mw2A">CB slang</a>, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verlan" title="Verlan" id="mw2Q">verlan</a>, the <i id="mw2g"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grypsera" title="Grypsera" id="mw2w">grypsera</a></i> of Polish prisons, <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thieves'_cant" title="Thieves' cant" id="mw3A">thieves' cant</a>,<sup about="#mwt68" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-14" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-14"}}'><a href="#cite_note-14" id="mw3Q"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mw3g"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mw3w">[</span>14<span class="cite-bracket" id="mw4A">]</span></span></a></sup> <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polari" title="Polari" id="mw4Q">Polari</a>,<sup about="#mwt69" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-15" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-15"}}'><a href="#cite_note-15" id="mw4g"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mw4w"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mw5A">[</span>15<span class="cite-bracket" id="mw5Q">]</span></span></a></sup> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bangime" title="Bangime" class="mw-redirect" id="mw5g">Bangime</a>.<sup about="#mwt72" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-16" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-16"}}'><a href="#cite_note-16" id="mw5w"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mw6A"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mw6Q">[</span>16<span class="cite-bracket" id="mw6g">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<section data-mw-section-id="4" id="mw6w" aria-labelledby="In_popular_culture"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading3"><h3 id="In_popular_culture">In popular culture</h3><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cant&amp;action=edit&amp;section=4" title="Edit section: In popular culture"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mw7A">Anti-languages are sometimes created by authors and used by characters in novels. These anti-languages do not have complete lexicons, cannot be observed in use for <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_description" title="Linguistic description" id="mw7Q">linguistic description</a>, and therefore cannot be studied in the same way a language spoken by an existing anti-society would. However, they are still used in the study of anti-languages. Roger Fowler's "Anti-Languages in Fiction" analyzes <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_Burgess" title="Anthony Burgess" id="mw7g">Anthony Burgess</a>'s <i id="mw7w"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Clockwork_Orange_(novel)" title="A Clockwork Orange (novel)" id="mw8A">A Clockwork Orange</a></i> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_S._Burroughs" title="William S. Burroughs" id="mw8Q">William S. Burroughs</a>' <i id="mw8g"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_Lunch" title="Naked Lunch" id="mw8w">Naked Lunch</a></i> to redefine the nature of the anti-language and to describe its ideological purpose.<sup about="#mwt75" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-17" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-17"}}'><a href="#cite_note-17" id="mw9A"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mw9Q"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mw9g">[</span>17<span class="cite-bracket" id="mw9w">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mw-A"><i id="mw-Q">A Clockwork Orange</i> is a popular example of a novel where the main character is a teenage boy who speaks an anti-language called <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadsat" title="Nadsat" id="mw-g">Nadsat</a>. This language is often referred to as an argot, but it has been argued that it is an anti-language because of the social structure it maintains through the social class of the droogs.<sup about="#mwt76" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-autogenerated12_13-1" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"autogenerated12"}}'><a href="#cite_note-autogenerated12-13" id="mw-w"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mw_A"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mw_Q">[</span>13<span class="cite-bracket" id="mw_g">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

</section></section><section data-mw-section-id="5" id="mw_w" aria-labelledby="Regional_usage_of_term"><div class="mw-heading mw-heading2"><h2 id="Regional_usage_of_term">Regional usage of term</h2><span class="mw-editsection"><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">[</span><a href="/w/index.php?title=Cant&amp;action=edit&amp;section=5" title="Edit section: Regional usage of term"><span>edit</span></a><span class="mw-editsection-bracket">]</span></span></div>
<p id="mwAQA">In parts of <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connacht" title="Connacht" id="mwAQE">Connacht</a>, in Ireland, <i id="mwAQI">cant</i> mainly refers to an <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auction" title="Auction" id="mwAQM">auction</a>, typically on <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair" title="Fair" id="mwAQQ">fair day</a> ("Cantmen and Cantwomen, some from as far away as Dublin, would converge on Mohill on a Fair Day, ... set up their stalls ... and immediately start auctioning off their merchandise") and secondly means talk ("very entertaining conversation was often described as 'great cant'" or "crosstalk").<sup about="#mwt77" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEDolan200643_18-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Transclusion mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"group":"","name":"FOOTNOTEDolan200643"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-FOOTNOTEDolan200643-18"},"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"sfn","href":"./Template:Sfn"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"Dolan"},"2":{"wt":"2006"},"pp":{"wt":"43"}},"i":0}}]}'><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEDolan200643-18" id="mwAQU"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwAQY"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAQc">[</span>18<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAQg">]</span></span></a></sup><sup about="#mwt80" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-FOOTNOTEO'Crohan1987_19-0" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Transclusion mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"group":"","name":"FOOTNOTEO&apos;Crohan1987"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-FOOTNOTEO&apos;Crohan1987-19"},"parts":[{"template":{"target":{"wt":"sfn","href":"./Template:Sfn"},"params":{"1":{"wt":"O&apos;Crohan"},"2":{"wt":"1987"}},"i":0}}]}'><a href="#cite_note-FOOTNOTEO'Crohan1987-19" id="mwAQk"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwAQo"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAQs">[</span>19<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAQw">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwAQ0">In Scotland, two unrelated creole languages are termed <i id="mwAQ4">cant</i>. <i id="mwAQ8"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Cant" title="Scottish Cant" id="mwARA">Scottish Cant</a></i> (a mixed language, primarily <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scots_Language" title="Scots Language" class="mw-redirect" id="mwARE">Scots</a> and <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_language" title="Romani language" id="mwARI">Romani</a> with <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic" title="Scottish Gaelic" id="mwARM">Scottish Gaelic</a> influences) is spoken by lowland Roma groups. <i id="mwARQ">Highland Traveller's Cant</i> (or <i id="mwARU"><a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beurla_Reagaird" title="Beurla Reagaird" id="mwARY">Beurla Reagaird</a></i>) is a <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic" title="Scottish Gaelic" id="mwARc">Gaelic</a>-based cant of the Indigenous Highland Traveller population.<sup about="#mwt85" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-Queen's2_2-3" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"Queen&apos;s2"},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-Queen&apos;s2-2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-Queen's2-2" id="mwARg"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwARk"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwARo">[</span>2<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwARs">]</span></span></a></sup> The cants are mutually unintelligible.</p>

<p id="mwARw">In June 2009, it was reported that inmates in one English prison were using "Elizabethan cant" as a means of communication that guards would not understand, although the words used are not part of the canon of recognised cant.<sup about="#mwt88" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-20" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-20"}}'><a href="#cite_note-20" id="mwAR0"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwAR4"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAR8">[</span>20<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwASA">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwASE">The word has also been used as a <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix" title="Suffix" id="mwASI">suffix</a> to coin names for modern-day jargons such as "medicant", a term used to refer to the type of language employed by members of the medical profession that is largely unintelligible to lay people.<sup about="#mwt89" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-McArthur2_1-3" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{"name":"McArthur2"}}'><a href="#cite_note-McArthur2-1" id="mwASM"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwASQ"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwASU">[</span>1<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwASY">]</span></span></a></sup></p>

<p id="mwAgY">The <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thieves'_cant" title="Thieves' cant" id="mwAgc">thieves' cant</a> was a feature of popular pamphlets and plays, particularly between 1590 and 1615, but continued to feature in literature through the 18th century. There are questions about how genuinely the literature reflected <a rel="mw:WikiLink" href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vernacular" title="Vernacular" id="mwAgg">vernacular</a> use in the criminal underworld. A thief in 1839 claimed that the cant he had seen in print was nothing like the cant then used by "gypsies, thieves, and beggars." He also said that each of these used distinct vocabularies, which overlapped, the gypsies having a cant word for everything, and the beggars using a lower style than the thieves.<sup about="#mwt101" class="mw-ref reference" id="cite_ref-25" rel="dc:references" typeof="mw:Extension/ref" data-mw='{"name":"ref","attrs":{},"body":{"id":"mw-reference-text-cite_note-25"}}'><a href="#cite_note-25" id="mwAgk"><span class="mw-reflink-text" id="mwAgo"><span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAgs">[</span>25<span class="cite-bracket" id="mwAgw">]</span></span></a></sup></p>
