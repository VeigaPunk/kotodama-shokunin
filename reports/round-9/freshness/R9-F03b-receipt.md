## 3. Passage legs

**Leg 1 — the royal-cap contrast, stated as a rule (S1, S2).**
Ensert (p. 95): "It may be concluded that the pointed hat was worn by both the kings and the gods since Khattushili III is depicted with the pointed hat at Abu Simbel. However the lack of the horn with the hat is important. Because the most important divinity sign is the horned hat in both Anatolia and Mesopotamia. Therefore these figures should be the figures of the kings and princes deified after death, but not of those in life." (note markers 21/22 dropped)
Ensert (p. 96): "Presently there are two images of Tudkhaliya IV that are confirmed by inscriptions: Tudkhaliya IV is depicted in the king iconography with the cap, the long mantel and the lituus in reliefs 64 and 81 at Yazılıkaya." — i.e. **royal = cap + long mantle + lituus; god/divinized = horned hat**, exactly the contrast the entry's line 137 asserts ("long robe and rounded cap in some images").
Ensert (p. 96): "Van den Hout agrees that the Hittite kings were depicted with horny hats only when they were deified after death." (n. 41 = van den Hout 1995a, 559) and n. 34: "The mortal, deified while in life, was not allowed to have himself depicted with the horned hat and in god dress (Boehmer 1972-75, 431-432)."
*Supports:* entry lines 123, 135, 137 — the horned cap is a **divinity/deification marker**, the royal cap a separate everyday-royal marker; and the entry's caution that horns are not a generic soldier's helmet.
*Does not support:* any claim that the rule is exceptionless — Ensert's own text records the contested side (Akurgal, Neve on deification in life) on the same page.

**Leg 2 — dated identification with the number of horns and the figure labels (S1, S2).**
Ensert (p. 96): "The king wears a pointed hat with five pairs of horn, a short skirt and a pair of shoes with the upturned toes and holds a lance resting on the right shoulder (Fig. 3)." — the Boğazköy House-(chapel)-a stele, identified by its hieroglyphic inscription as "Great King Tudkhaliya" (her n. 42–44).
Durusu-Tanriöver (p. 301): "In the representation of Tudhaliya IV in Yazılıkaya 64, the king is shown standing on two mountains, a symbol of divinity, while his name in hieroglyphic Luwian stretches below a drawing of the winged sun disk (Figure 8)." — a **dated, labelled** image with the identification argument spelled out; and: "the kings are depicted with unmistakably divine attributes, specifically with conical hats adorned with horns that have been used as symbols of divinity in the Near East since the third millennium bc."
*Supports:* entry lines 123/126/137 — rank gradient and label-based identification; adds an explicit, dated horizon for the horn motif (Near East, third millennium BCE) without collapsing it into "Hattic".
*Does not support:* a strict god-only rule. Same page: "The Südburg monument in Hattuša depicts a Šuppiluliuma in the martial outfit wearing a horned hat. Originally dated to the reign of Šuppiluliuma II, there is now an intense academic debate surrounding the patronage of this monument."; "As seals would be in circulation during the lifetime of the king, this particular seal of Muršili III calls into question his earthly nature, and may mean that Muršili III started a tradition of incorporating aspects of divine iconography into royal representation while he still reigned as a living king."; and on Tudhaliya IV: "Emirgazi altars, erected during the reign of Tudhaliya IV, mention votive offerings to be made to him, making it possible to suggest that the king was already deified before his death." The matching-hat cylinder seal ("both the god and the king are wearing matching hats and outfits") is the strongest single image behind the entry's "Horns mark divine status in characteristic representations".

**Leg 3 — later Neo-Hittite comparator, dated, with its own social reading (S3/Gilibert).**
Gilibert, p. 144 n. 46 (Malatya, Relief E of the PUGNUS-mili cycle): "one of them is bearded and wears a horned, pointed hat; the other one is beardless, wears no hat, and sports long hair styled in peculiar curls. The PUGNUS-mili with beard and horned hat may be the dead grandfather of PUGNIS-mili (II), who sponsored the relief cycle (Brown 2008: 303)." (repository spelling 'PUGNIS-mili' in the second occurrence, sic)
Dating, same page n. 43: "…I propose to re-organize the chronological sequence of the reliefs at Malatya according the order Malatya I – Malatya III – Malatya II, all of them dating before the destruction of the 'Porta Schaeffer' at the end of the 11th/beginning of the 10th century BC."
*Supports:* the entry's line 49 brake and the round-8 limit "dater Neo-Hittite headgear witness". The Iron Age counterpart repeats the Empire distinction — horned hat for a **dead/divine ancestor**, no hat for the living ruler — while the chapter's own frame ("between 1200 and 950 BC") keeps the Iron Age material from being back-projected.
*Does not support:* using Malatya/Karkemiš dress as Late Bronze Age evidence (the chapter itself is about post-1200 public art).

**Leg 4 — earlier (pre-Hittite) comparator: dated objects, not horned caps (S3/Taracha, S4, S5).**
Taracha p. 60: "The association with Alacahöyük (Arinna?) is irresistible. In the third millennium BC already, before the growth of the importance of nearby Ḫattusa, Alacahöyük had been the seat of a local dynasty. It peaked in prosperity in the EB III period, which is how the royal tombs there are dated."
Wygnańska p. 93: "…the openwork headbands from Alacahüyük that probably functioned as a crown (Koşay 1938: Pl. 82; 1951: Pl. 129; Muscarella 2003: 280)" (artifact spells 'Alacahüyük' at this locus; elsewhere 'Alacahöyük'), p. 94: "the diadems from Alacahöyük were found in a restricted number of elite burials and were clearly attributes of power and elevated social position"; n. 7: "Two of the five diadems from Alacahöyük had additional strips hanging from the headband (Hickman 2008: 249), representing a specifically local tradition."; p. 93 context/dating: the later-3rd-millennium finds "came from Alacahöyük … and Kültepe … in central Anatolia", with "was carried on in the later 3rd millennium BC (a more exact dating was impossible in most cases)".
Klaunzer p. 89: "we know diadems since the 3rd Millennium onwards from several archaeological sites, most of the time out of funeral context."; p. 90: "Diadems can be decorated with techniques of repoussé, engravings or open worked decorations. They are known from … as well as from Alacahöyük"; plate caption p. 90: "Fig. 1: Alacahöyük Tomb A, Diadem: Photo DBM, Yalçın."
Archi p. 14 (for what the scholarly Hattic layer actually covers): "The Hittite received from the Hattians not only the gods for their official pantheon together with the related cultual ceremonials, but also the belief that the land had to supply its gods with food consigned yearly by those who represented the central administration in each of the major centres."
*Supports (partial):* a **dated pre-Hittite elite-headgear object horizon** (Alaca EB III diadems/headbands, Kültepe) and a dated pre-Hittite dynastic seat — the "earlier comparator" now has dated objects; and it documents that the scholarly Hattic-inheritance discussion in this volume is about **cult and administration, not headgear**.
*Does not support:* any pre-Hittite *horned-cap* image or a Hattian headgear term. The earlier horizon is diadems/headbands, i.e. a **different wearable class** from the Empire horned cap. Treating the EB III Alaca "royal" horizon as "Hattic" remains the modern convention flagged in round 8 and is not certified here.
